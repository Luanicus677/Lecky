abstract: |
  <p>Coleridge is annotating Jeremy Taylor’s <a class="popup-ref" href="taylor-liberty-of-prophesying.html"><em>Liberty of Prophesying</em></a> (1647), the great Anglican plea for toleration, and he cannot forgive Taylor for disowning it once the Church was back in power after 1660. Lecky quotes him, calling the judgment “a little too severe”, and tones it down in passing: where Coleridge wrote that but for this Taylor’s character “would at least have been stainless”, Lecky prints “almost stainless”. Coleridge then goes further than anything Lecky quotes: “Alas, alas, most dearly do I love Jeremy Taylor … But this is too foul a blotch of leprosy to be forgiven. He who pardons such an act in such a man partakes of its guilt.”</p>

<section id="ruse-de-guerre" class="passage">
<h2>“A ruse de guerre” (vol. I, p. 209)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: London 1853, vol. I, p. 209, <a href="https://archive.org/details/notesonenglishd00colegoog/page/n222/mode/2up">scan</a>; a note on Taylor’s <em>Liberty of Prophesying</em>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: Lecky’s quotation.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/coleridge-divines-i-p209.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/coleridge-divines-i-p209.jpg" alt="A printed page headed “Jeremy Taylor. 209”: “Or if Jeremy Taylor had not in effect retracted after the Restoration …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Coleridge, <i>Notes on English Divines</i> (1853), vol. I, p. 209. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (Google Books) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>[p. 208, OCR:] O! had this work been published when Charles I., Archbishop Laud, whose chaplain Taylor was, and the other Star Chamber inquisitors, were sentencing Prynne, Bastwick, Leighton, and others, to punishments that have left a brand-mark on the Church of England, the sophistry might have been forgiven for the sake of the motive, which would then have been unquestionable.</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">Or if Jeremy Taylor had not in effect retracted after the Restoration;—if he had not, as soon as the Church had gained its power, most basely disclaimed and disavowed the principle of toleration, and apologised for the publication by declaring it to have been a <i>ruse de guerre</i>, currying pardon for his past liberalism by charging, and most probably slandering himself with the guilt of falsehood, treachery, and hypocrisy, his character as a man would at least have been stainless.</span> Alas, alas, most dearly do I love Jeremy Taylor; most religiously do I venerate his memory! But this is too foul a blotch of leprosy to be forgiven. He who pardons such an act in such a man partakes of its guilt.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky’s differences: “the Church had gained power” for “gained its power”, and “almost stainless” for “at least have been stainless”, which turns Coleridge’s “Taylor would have been without stain” into “Taylor was nearly without stain”.</p>
