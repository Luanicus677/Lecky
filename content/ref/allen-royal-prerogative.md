abstract: |
  <p>Lecky notes that the Restoration made every subject swear it was not lawful “on any pretence whatever to take up arms against the king”, and that “this clause was expunged at the Revolution”. Allen, his source, tells a longer story. The oath, he says, was the monarchical theory’s “day of triumph”, a declaration which, “if acted on literally, must have converted our limited government into an absolute monarchy”. After 1688 it would have been “preposterous and indecorous” to keep it, and the declarations in the Militia Act and the Act of Uniformity were abrogated by William and Mary’s first parliament. But “by some oversight it was left in force under the Corporation Act, and was not finally expunged from our statute book till the accession of the house of Brunswick”, repealed only by an act of the fifth year of George I (Allen cites 5 Geo. I. c. 6), some thirty years after the Revolution.</p>

<section id="any-pretence" class="passage">
<h2>“Not lawful on any pretence whatever” (pp. 84–85)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: 1849, chapter on allegiance, pp. 84–85, <a href="https://archive.org/details/inquiryintoriseg00alle/page/n183/mode/2up">scan n183</a>–n184. Side-notes: “Oaths and declarations exacted after the Restoration”; “Abrogated after the Revolution”. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky’s note reports, and what it leaves out.</p>

<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/allen-1849-p85.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/allen-1849-p85.jpg" alt="Printed page 85: “… The declaration required by the militia act and act of uniformity was accordingly abrogated by the first parliament of William and Mary. By some oversight it was left in force under the Corporation Act, and was not finally expunged from our statute book till the accession of the house of Brunswick.”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>p. 85. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>At the Restoration, as was naturally to be expected, these pretensions of the two houses were abrogated and annulled; and the current setting strongly in the opposite direction, laws were passed and declarations enforced, which, if acted on literally, must have converted our limited government into an absolute monarchy. All persons, bound to take the oath of allegiance, were made to swear, <span class="cited-passage">that it is not lawful <i>on any pretence whatever</i> to take up arms against the King</span>, and were called upon to renounce with abhorrence the traitorous position, that arms may be taken by his authority against his person or against those who are commissioned by him.</p>
<p>This was the day of triumph for the monarchical theory, which had never before obtained a parliamentary sanction for its extravagances. But in a few years the Revolution followed, and demonstrated the futility of oaths and declarations as securities to a King of England in his endeavours to subvert the fundamental laws of the kingdom. To have retained as part of the oath of allegiance a declaration, in direct violation of which the kingdom had been rescued from arbitrary power, and the nation restored to its ancient rights and liberties, would have been preposterous and indecorous. <span class="cited-passage">The declaration required by the militia act and act of uniformity was accordingly abrogated by the first parliament of William and Mary. By some oversight it was left in force under the Corporation Act, and was not finally expunged from our statute book till the accession of the house of Brunswick.</span></p>
<p class="passage-source">Allen’s footnotes: the declarations were required by the Corporation Act, 13 Ca. II. st. 2. c. 1. § 5, 12; the Militia Act, 13 and 14 Ca. II. c. 2. § 18, 19; and the Act of Uniformity, 13 and 14 Ca. II. c. 4. § 9; abrogated by 1 W. &amp; M. Sess. 1. c. 8. § 11; finally repealed by 5 Geo. I. c. 6. § 2.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. V, note 90): “This clause was expunged at the Revolution (Allen’s <i>Hist. of Royal Prerogative in England</i>, p. 89).” True of two of the three statutes; Allen’s own page says the third kept it until the reign of George I.</p>
