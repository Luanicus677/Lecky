<h2>P. 305: Venice 1518, and Como</h2>

<p class="passage-source">English: London, 1857, vol. I, p. 305 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/phantasmataorill01madd/page/n354/mode/2up">scan</a>). The text was read from the scan’s text layer; short quotations only. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the words Lecky’s sentence rests on.</p>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="en">
<p>In 1518, in the Venetian territories, a great number of persons, accused of sorcery and heresy, were condemned by the Inquisition, and handed over to the secular arm […]. <span class="cited-passage">But the people of Venice rose up against the Inquisitors, and were hardly to be appeased by the Council.</span></p>
<p>[…] The Dominicans were charged with its execution in Lombardy. <span class="cited-passage">In the district of Como alone, the number who perished are estimated by Barth. de Spina (in Malleo Maleficorum) at a thousand a year</span>; and in the space of seven years, in Piedmont, Lombardy, and Mirandola, the number who perished was enormous.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p class="editorial-note">The source is in English, so there is no translation. The passage after the first paragraph, omitted here, is a sentence about Pope Adrian VI’s bull of 1523.</p>
</div>
</div>

<p>So Lecky’s sentence is a compression of Madden’s two sentences, with the “thousand” turned from a figure for those who “perished” into “executed”, and the Venetian rising turned into a general “absolute rebellion”.</p>

<section id="venice-1518" class="passage">
<h2>What Happened in 1518: A Second Source</h2>

<p>Madden gives no source for his sentence about Venice. The event he seems to mean is the Val Camonica witch trials of 1518, when an inquisitor’s court in the Alpine valley north of Brescia (then Venetian territory) burned dozens of people. Two sources were checked:</p>

<ul>
<li>Stefano Brambilla and Attilio Toffolo, <em>Lo scontro sulla stregoneria in Valle Camonica tra la Repubblica di Venezia e il Papato nei documenti del 1518–1521</em> (an introduction and transcription of the Venetian archive documents; <a href="https://www.circologhislandi.net/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/Lo-scontro-sulla-stregoneria-in-Valle-Camonica.pdf">PDF</a>), which cites Marin Sanudo’s <em>Diarii</em> and the registers of the Venetian Council of Ten.</li>
<li><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Val_Camonica_witch_trials">Wikipedia, “Val Camonica witch trials”</a>, a secondary summary that cites no source for the 1518–1521 claims.</li>
</ul>

<p>What the archival study reports, in summary:</p>
<ul>
<li>Between late June and mid-July 1518, an inquisitor’s court in the valley had between 62 and 80 people executed. Sanudo reports the inquisitor saying he had burned about seventy.</li>
<li>On 14 July 1518 the Venetian Council of Ten, the Republic’s secret executive body, was informed by the Brescia governors. Sanudo says three of its heads wrote to Brescia complaining that they had not been told of so grave a matter, and ordering a halt to further executions.</li>
<li>On 31 July 1518 the Council ordered that no further proceedings go ahead against the accused, and demanded the trial records and an inquiry into the judges. The study reads this as a defence of Venetian jurisdiction against the Church, and as a response to the property confiscations by the ecclesiastical authorities and the costs borne by the valley’s communes.</li>
<li>The trial records reached Venice on 11 August; on 12 August the papal nuncio spoke with the Council, and on 23 August the case was referred to him. On 9 September he read Pope Leo X’s brief authorising a review, and the Council said the Bishop of Brescia and his officials had proceeded with great severity and from greed.</li>
<li>The affair then ran on for years, with Brescia’s governors writing against the Dominicans in the autumn of 1518 and in 1519. The Council issued stricter rules for inquisition trials on 21 March 1521, and on 27 July 1521 formally suspended the proceedings in the valley.</li>
</ul>

<p>Set against Madden’s sentence:</p>

</tbody>

<p><strong>Verdict:</strong> the trials and Venice’s intervention are real and well attested, in Sanudo and in the Council’s own records. A popular “rising” against the inquisitors is not: it is Madden’s wording, and probably a misreading of the Council of Ten’s action as the people’s. So Lecky’s “absolute rebellion” created by “the severity of the inquisitors” rests on an error of Madden’s. What the documents do show is a state resisting an inquisition, not a rebellion by the people. This check is limited: only the study’s introduction and notes were read in full, not all 92 pages of the transcribed trial records, and nothing else on the 1518 events (for example Sanudo’s own text) was read directly.</p>
</section>

<section id="sanudo-1518" class="passage">
<h2>Sanudo’s Own Entries, Read Directly</h2>

<p class="passage-source">Italian: Marino Sanudo, <em>I Diarii</em>, vol. XXV (Venice, 1889), the entries for July–August 1518 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/idiariidimarinos25sanu">scan</a>; text read from the scan’s OCR layer, which is rough, so only short phrases are quoted and no page images were checked). The edition’s page numbers were not read; the entries are cited by Sanudo’s own dates. These are the entries Brambilla and Toffolo cite for 14 and 31 July 1518.</p>

<h3>14 July 1518</h3>
<p>In the entry for the day on which the Council of Ten sat with its Zonta on a matter of salt, Sanudo adds a paragraph beginning “È da saper”. Letters had come from Brescia saying that the Inquisitor “haver fato brusar da 70 strige di quelle vale di Valcamonica e altrove”, with their goods taken and given to the churches. Because of this, three heads of the Council of Ten (named as Michiel Salamon, Jacomo Badoer and Alvise Gradenigo) wrote to the Brescia governors, “dolendosi che di tanta cossa non era sta dà alcun aviso” (complaining that no notice of so great a matter had been given). The governors were to halt further executions and tell the Inquisitor that they wished the trial record sent to the heads of the Ten, “aziò vedino la cossa” (so that they might see the matter). The date heading in the scan is garbled (“A dì M.”) and is read as 14 July; it agrees with the date Brambilla and Toffolo give.</p>

<h3>31 July 1518</h3>
<p>Sanudo’s entry for “A dì ultimo Luio” says the Council of Ten sat in simple (ordinary) session. It records the election of the month’s heads and appointments to the chancery, and a hanging; nothing about Val Camonica, and the phrases the archival study quotes from the Council’s 31 July decision (“nostra ferma intention è de intender tuto el successo…”) were not found anywhere in volume XXV’s text layer. Either the study draws them from the Council’s register and not from Sanudo’s entry for that date, or the OCR missed them. The entry cannot be said to confirm the 31 July order.</p>

<h3>Other entries that bear on the question</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>17 July:</strong> a letter from the Brescia governors “si scusano in materia di le strige di Valcamonica” (excuse themselves); the podestà was to ride to the valley and send the record to the heads of the Ten.</li>
<li><strong>11–12 August:</strong> the trial record from Brescia was presented to the heads of the Ten, and the papal legate had “suspeso” (suspended) the bishop from proceeding further; “di queste strige per la terra si parla”, Sanudo adds (people in the city were talking of these witches). On the 12th the legate spoke with the heads of the Ten at length, with some who had come from Brescia and from the valley.</li>
<li><strong>August letters copied by Sanudo:</strong> a Brescian letter reports about 64 burned in four places in the valley and about 5,000 suspected, and its writer, who saw eight women burned at Pisogne on 17 July, writes that “in lo procedere se usi termini non convenienti” (improper methods were used in the proceedings).</li>
<li><strong>23 August:</strong> the Council of Ten with its Zonta decided to refer the matter to the papal legate, the bishop of Pola (Altobello Averoldi). Sanudo says that “zerca 62” had been burned alive (about 40 of them women), and that the inquiry “per li Cai di X fu sospesa”. He then lists <strong>five “ambassadors of Valcamonica”</strong> then in Venice, who appeared before the College and the heads of the Ten to ask the Signoria to <em>continue</em> the inquisition (“supplicando la Signoria vi vadi drio la inquisition”): three priests, the Dominican inquisitor, and two laymen (Bernardin di Bendi and Damian de Federicis of Edolo).</li>
</ul>

<h3>Is any popular unrest mentioned?</h3>
<p>In the passages read there is none. Sanudo’s entries record the Council of Ten’s intervention, a dispute between Venice and the Church’s officials, talk in Venice about the trials, and, on 23 August, delegates from the valley asking that the inquisition <em>go on</em>. I searched the surrounding text for words of tumult or revolt (<em>romor, tumult, sollev, ribel, insorg, popolo</em> and similar) in the entries on this affair and found none in connection with it. Nowhere did Sanudo say that “the people of Venice rose up against the Inquisitors”. This reading rests on the scan’s OCR and covers the entries listed above, not every mention of the valley in the volume.</p>

<p><strong>Updated verdict:</strong> Sanudo, the best contemporary source, confirms the Council of Ten’s intervention (14 July onward and 23 August 1518) and the numbers of those burned. He records no Venetian popular rising, and the only valley voices he reports in the passages read are on the inquisitors’ side. Madden’s sentence and Lecky’s “absolute rebellion” are therefore unsupported by the contemporary record. Two points remain unconfirmed: the 31 July order, which I could not find in Sanudo’s text, and whether other sources record any local unrest in the valley itself.</p>
</section>
