abstract: |
  <p>Near the end of his first volume Lecky sets Bacon’s ideal of disinterested truth against the theological habit of judging opinions by their consequences. The passage he quotes is aphorism 129 of Book I, where Bacon, having praised inventions above conquests, adds that the mere sight of light is fairer than all its uses, and so “the very contemplation of things, as they are, without superstition or imposture, error or confusion, is in itself more worthy than all the fruit of inventions”. Lecky does not say whose translation he used; it is not Spedding’s, which is given here, nor the nineteenth-century translation in Project Gutenberg’s text (ebook 45988), so it may be his own.</p>
  <p>In Chapter III Lecky says that Bacon, facing the “torpor of the middle ages”, answered that “a theological system had lain like an incubus upon Christendom, and to its influence, more than to any other single cause, the universal paralysis is to be ascribed.” The nearest passage is aphorism 79: after Christianity was received “by far the greater number of the best wits applied themselves to theology”. But Bacon gives this as the cause “in the second place”. His first (aphorism 78) is that only three short periods in twenty-five centuries favoured learning at all, and the same aphorism 79 blames moral philosophy among the Romans and Greeks just as it blames theology among Christians. “More than to any other single cause” is Lecky’s emphasis.</p>

<section id="best-wits-79" class="passage">
<h2>“The best wits applied themselves to theology” (Book I, aphorism 79)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: Spedding and Ellis’s translation, <em>Works</em> vol. IV (1858), p. 78, <a href="https://archive.org/details/worksoffrancisba0004baco/page/n91/mode/2up">scan n91</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky’s text before note 103 reports.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/bacon-novum-organum-aph79.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/bacon-novum-organum-aph79.jpg" alt="Printed page: “LXXIX. In the second place there presents itself a cause of great weight in all ways … after the Christian religion was received and grew strong, by far the greater number of the best wits applied themselves to theology …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Bacon, <em>Novum Organum</em> I.79, trans. Spedding and Ellis, <em>Works</em> vol. IV (1858), p. 78. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>In the second place there presents itself a cause of great weight in all ways; namely, that during those very ages in which the wits and learning of men have flourished most, or indeed flourished at all, the least part of their diligence was given to natural philosophy. Yet this very philosophy it is that ought to be esteemed the great mother of the sciences. For all arts and all sciences, if torn from this root, though they may be polished and shaped and made fit for use, yet they will hardly grow. <span class="cited-passage">Now it is well known that after the Christian religion was received and grew strong, by far the greater number of the best wits applied themselves to theology; that to this both the highest rewards were offered, and helps of all kinds most abundantly supplied; and that this devotion to theology chiefly occupied that third portion or epoch of time among us Europeans of the West</span>; and the more so because about the same time both literature began to flourish and religious controversies to spring up.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The aphorism continues (read in the OCR only) with the second period, when among the Romans “the meditations and labours of philosophers were principally employed and consumed on moral philosophy, which to the Heathen was as theology to us”, and with the Greeks after Socrates. Aphorism 89 (p. 87) adds that natural philosophy has always had “a troublesome adversary and hard to deal with; namely, superstition, and the blind and immoderate zeal of religion”.</p>

<section id="contemplation" class="passage">
<h2>“The very beholding of the light” (Book I, aphorism 129)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: Spedding’s translation, Works vol. IV, p. 115, <a href="https://archive.org/details/worksoffrancisba0004baco/page/n128/mode/2up">scan</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the sentence Lecky quotes in another translation.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/bacon-novum-organum-i-129.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/bacon-novum-organum-i-129.jpg" alt="A printed page headed “Novum Organum. 115”: “And yet (to speak the whole truth), as the uses of light are infinite …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Bacon, <i>Novum Organum</i> I.129, in Spedding’s translation (<i>Works</i>, vol. IV, 1858), p. 115. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>Again, if men have thought so much of some one particular discovery as to regard him as more than man who has been able by some benefit to make the whole human race his debtor, how much higher a thing to discover that by means of which all things else shall be discovered with ease! <span class="cited-passage">And yet (to speak the whole truth), as the uses of light are infinite, in enabling us to walk, to ply our arts, to read, to recognise one another; and nevertheless the very beholding of the light is itself a more excellent and a fairer thing than all the uses of it;—so assuredly the very contemplation of things, as they are, without superstition or imposture, error or confusion, is in itself more worthy than all the fruit of inventions.</span></p>
<p>Lastly, if the debasement of arts and sciences to purposes of wickedness, luxury, and the like, be made a ground of objection, let no one be moved thereby. For the same may be said of all earthly goods; of wit, courage, strength, beauty, wealth, light itself, and the rest. Only let the human race recover that right over nature which belongs to it by divine bequest, and let power be given it; the exercise thereof will be governed by sound reason and true religion.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky’s version: “And yet (to speak the whole truth), just as we are deeply indebted to light because it enables us to enter on our way, to exercise arts, to read, to distinguish one another, and nevertheless the sight of light is itself more excellent and beautiful than the manifold uses of it; so, assuredly, the very contemplation of things as they are, without superstition or imposture, without error or confusion, is in itself more worthy than all the produce of discoveries.”</p>
</section>
