abstract: |
  <p>Lecky reports that Heyne “thinks the statues of Dædalus were in wood”, and objects: “this appears very doubtful. Pausanias says he saw a statue ascribed to Dædalus which was of stone.” Lecky’s page is right: on p. 339 Heyne says that the works of Daedalus “are handed down by constant report as made of wood”.</p>
  <p>But Lecky’s objection is already answered in the next sentence. Heyne cites the same passage of Pausanias (book IX, ch. 40), where Pausanias had persuaded himself that a work of Daedalean art survived in his own day “wrought in white stone”, and adds that Pausanias had “no doubt allowed himself to be imposed on by popular report”. Lecky gives Heyne’s conclusion and then repeats, as a refutation, the evidence Heyne had weighed and set aside.</p>

<h2>The works of Daedalus “e ligno” (p. 339)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin: Heyne, <em>Opuscula academica</em> vol. V, p. 339, <a href="https://archive.org/details/opusculaacademic05heyn/page/n354/mode/2up">scan n354</a>. Heyne is describing the first, “mythical” age of Greek art, and has just mentioned the chorus wrought by Daedalus in the <em>Iliad</em>. The long s is printed as s. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky reports.</p>

<p>Anaglyphum fuisse credas. <span class="cited-passage">Daedali tamen opera constanti fama traduntur e ligno facta fuisse, ξόανα.</span> Pausanias quidem (lib. IX, 40. p. 793.) suo adhuc tempore superstes esse e candido lapide elaboratum opus illud artis Daedaleae sibi persuaserat; vulgari fama haud dubie sibi imponi passus.</p>

<div class="rendering">
<p>One would suppose it to have been a relief. <span class="cited-passage">Yet the works of Daedalus are handed down by constant report as made of wood, <i>xoana</i>.</span> Pausanias, it is true (book IX, 40, p. 793), had persuaded himself that this work of Daedalean art survived in his own time, wrought in white stone; no doubt he let himself be imposed on by popular report.</p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation: this edition’s draft.</p>
</div>

<p>“Opus illud” is the work Heyne has just discussed, the dancing-floor or chorus that the <em>Iliad</em> compares with the chorus on Achilles’ shield; the page number after Pausanias’ book and chapter refers to the edition Heyne used.</p>
