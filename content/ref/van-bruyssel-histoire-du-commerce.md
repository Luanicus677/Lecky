abstract: |
  <p>Lecky’s sentence “At a still earlier period Belgium had entered into relations with more than thirty kingdoms or states” is Van Bruyssel’s, and his page is a catalogue of a medieval world market: wool, hides, lead and sea-coal from Britain; gerfalcons from Norway; horses from Denmark; furs and wax from Russia; saffron and rice from Aragon; cochineal and quicksilver from Castile; dates from the Atlas; and cloth of gold “brought by way of Tartary”.</p>
  <p>The other passage is the scene behind Lecky’s account of the first theologian to defend tyrannicide. In 1408, a few months after the Duke of Burgundy’s men had cut down the Duke of Orleans in a Paris street, “a Norman Franciscan, Jean Petit” told the dauphin and the princes that it was “lawful for every subject … to kill or have killed a traitorous and disloyal tyrant, and not only lawful, but honourable and meritorious”. Van Bruyssel: “These sinister words, which allowed everyone to set himself up as judge and executioner, raised no protest.”</p>

<h2>“Plus de trente royaumes et états” (tome I, p. 234)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: Brussels 1861, tome I, p. 234, <a href="https://archive.org/details/histoireducommer01bruy/page/n245/mode/2up">scan</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky reports.</p>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>Un document des plus curieux, publié par M. Warnkœnig, nous fournit des renseignements plus complets … <span class="cited-passage">Plus de trente royaumes et états, de toutes les parties de l’Ancien Monde, établirent des relations, durant le XIII<sup>e</sup> et le XIV<sup>e</sup> siècle, avec la Belgique.</span> L’Angleterre, l’Écosse et l’Irlande nous envoyaient des laines, des cuirs, du plomb, des charbons de roche, des fromages et des suifs; nous recevions de la Norwége des gerfauts et des merriens …; le Danemark nous livrait ses coursiers, et la Russie ses pelleteries; la cire nous arrivait aussi de cette dernière contrée, ainsi que de la Bohême, de la Hongrie et de la Pologne, d’où nous retirions également de l’or et de l’argent en lingots. … l’Aragon envoyait en Flandre son safran et son riz; le pays de Liége ses cuivres, et l’Allemagne ses vins, ses blés et ses fers. … les pelleteries de Fez, de Tunis et de Bugie; les sucres bruts venant du Maroc; les aluns de Majorque et de Constantinople; les dattes de l’Atlas; les épices de l’Égypte, de la Palestine et de l’Arménie; et enfin les draps d’or, si riches et si élégants, que nos négociants faisaient venir par la Tartarie.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>A most curious document, published by M. Warnkönig, gives us fuller information … <span class="cited-passage">More than thirty kingdoms and states, from every part of the Old World, entered into relations with Belgium during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries.</span> England, Scotland and Ireland sent us wool, hides, lead, sea-coal, cheeses and tallow; from Norway we received gerfalcons and stave-wood …; Denmark supplied its chargers, and Russia its furs; wax came to us from Russia too, and from Bohemia, Hungary and Poland, from which we also drew gold and silver in ingots. … Aragon sent Flanders its saffron and rice; the land of Liège its copper, and Germany its wines, corn and iron. … the furs of Fez, Tunis and Bougie; raw sugar from Morocco; alum from Majorca and Constantinople; dates from the Atlas; spices from Egypt, Palestine and Armenia; and finally cloth of gold, so rich and elegant, which our merchants brought by way of Tartary.</p>
</div>

<section id="jean-petit" class="passage">
<h2>“Honorable et méritoire”: Jean Petit and the murder of Orleans (tome II, pp. 48–49)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: Brussels 1861, tome II, pp. 48–49, <a href="https://archive.org/details/histoireducommer02bruy/page/n52/mode/2up">scan of p. 49</a>. Abridged; p. 48 from the OCR. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky draws on.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/van-bruyssel-1861-tome2-p49.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/van-bruyssel-1861-tome2-p49.jpg" alt="Printed French: “Quelques jours plus tard, dans une des salles du palais royal, un cordelier normand, Jean Petit, prenait la parole”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Van Bruyssel, tome II, p. 49. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>[p. 48] … Il était arrivé en face de l’hôtel du maréchal de Rieux, lorsqu’une vingtaine d’hommes se précipitèrent tout à coup sur son escorte, tandis qu’un gentilhomme normand, Raoul d’Auquetonville, s’élançait sur le duc lui-même et le frappait d’un coup de hache. Le prince, à moitié étourdi par le choc, n’eut que le temps de s’écrier: «&#8239;Garde que fais-tu! Je suis le duc d’Orléans!&#8239;» Une voix lui répondit: «&#8239;C’est ce que nous quérons!&#8239;» …</p>
<p>[p. 49] Quelques jours plus tard, dans une des salles du palais royal, <span class="cited-passage">un cordelier normand, Jean Petit, prenait la parole en présence du dauphin, des princes du sang et d’une foule de hauts personnages, et entreprenait solennellement la justification du meurtre du duc d’Orléans.</span> … «&#8239;<span class="cited-passage">Il est licite à chaque sujet</span>, disait l’orateur officiel, <span class="cited-passage">selon la loi morale, naturelle ou divine, d’occire ou de faire occire un traître et déloyal tyran, et non pas seulement licite, mais honorable et méritoire</span>; surtout lorsqu’il est de si grande puissance que justice ne peut pas bonnement être faite par le souverain.&#8239;» Ces paroles sinistres, qui permettaient à chacun de s’ériger en juge et en bourreau, ne soulevèrent aucune protestation.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>[p. 48] … He had reached the front of the house of the marshal de Rieux when some twenty men suddenly rushed upon his escort, while a Norman gentleman, Raoul d’Auquetonville [as in the OCR], threw himself on the duke himself and struck him with an axe. The prince, half stunned by the blow, had only time to cry out: “What are you doing? I am the duke of Orleans!” A voice answered: “That is who we are looking for!” …</p>
<p>[p. 49] A few days later, in one of the halls of the royal palace, <span class="cited-passage">a Norman Franciscan, Jean Petit, spoke in the presence of the dauphin, the princes of the blood and a crowd of great personages, and solemnly undertook the justification of the murder of the duke of Orleans.</span> … “<span class="cited-passage">It is lawful for every subject</span>,” said the official orator, “<span class="cited-passage">by the moral, natural or divine law, to kill or have killed a traitorous and disloyal tyrant, and not only lawful, but honourable and meritorious</span>; above all when he is of such great power that justice cannot well be done by the sovereign.” These sinister words, which allowed everyone to set himself up as judge and executioner, raised no protest.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Van Bruyssel calls <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Petit_(theologian)">Jean Petit</a> a <i>cordelier</i>, a Franciscan, the point Lecky says “a writer in the <i>Journal des Sçavans</i> of 1748” disputed. The rest of Lecky’s parenthesis, that <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Fearless">the duke who instigated the murder</a> was himself assassinated, is not on these two pages.</p>
</section>
