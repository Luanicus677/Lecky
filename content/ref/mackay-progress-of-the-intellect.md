abstract: |
  <p>Lecky traces the modern theatre back to religion: “From the earliest times men seem to have been accustomed to throw into dramatic forms the objects of their belief; and the pagan mysteries, which were essentially dramatic, retained their authority over the popular mind long after every other portion of the ancient worship was despised.” His authority is Mackay, who argued that ancient teachers, following Nature’s own method, taught “through the eye”: the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Roman_mysteries">mysteries</a> were “a sacred drama”, a show with almost no explanation, which left the spectators to draw their own conclusions.</p>
  <p>Lecky’s page reference is a puzzle. The pages he gives (“vol. ii. pp. 286–297”) are, in the 1850 edition, about Hebrew ideas of the afterlife. Mackay’s own section on the form of the mysteries begins on p. 115 of vol. II.</p>

<section id="sacred-drama" class="passage">
<h2>“The mysteries were a sacred drama” (vol. II, § 18, pp. 115–116)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: London 1850, vol. II, ch. V (“On the theory of mediation”), § 18, <a href="https://archive.org/details/progressofintell02mack/page/n126/mode/2up">scan n126</a>–<a href="https://archive.org/details/progressofintell02mack/page/n127/mode/2up">n127</a>. Mackay’s footnote markers omitted. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the words that fit Lecky’s claim.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/mackay-1850-vol2-p115.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/mackay-1850-vol2-p115.jpg" alt="Printed page: “§ 18. Form of the mysteries. Nature is as free from dogmatism as from tyranny … The mysteries were a sacred drama, ex-”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Mackay, vol. II, p. 115. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>Nature is as free from dogmatism as from tyranny; and the earliest instructors of mankind not only adopted her lessons, but as far as possible adhered to her method of imparting them. They attempted to reach the understanding through the eye, and the greater part of all religious teaching was conveyed through this ancient and most impressive mode of “exhibition” or demonstration. <span class="cited-passage">The mysteries were a sacred drama, exhibiting some legend significant of Nature’s change</span>, of the visible universe in which the divinity is revealed, and whose import was in many respects as open to the Pagan as to the Christian. Beyond the current traditions or <i lang="grc">ἱεροὶ λόγοι</i> of the temple, few explanations were given to the spectators, who were left, as in the school of Nature, to make inferences for themselves.</p>
<p class="note">[Mackay’s note 2, p. 115:] … <span class="cited-passage">The mysteries consisted seemingly of two parts, the scenic exhibition, and a commentary on it</span> …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The rest of Lecky’s note, that the gladiatorial games were of Etruscan origin and were first held at graves in honour of the dead, is not on these pages.</p>
