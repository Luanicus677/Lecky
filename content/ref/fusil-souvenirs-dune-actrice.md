abstract: |
  <p>Lecky credits <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois-Joseph_Talma">Talma</a>, prompted by the painter David, with banishing from the French stage “the custom of representing the heroes of Greece and Rome with powdered wigs and in the garb of the courtiers of Versailles”, and adds in a note that, according to Madame Fusil, the first step was taken by “an actress named Madame Saint-Hubert, who discarded powder and took the ancient sculptures as her model”.</p>
  <p>Fusil says exactly that, in one short paragraph: “It has often been said that Talma was the first to revolutionise costume; but Mme Saint-Huberty had already begun to imitate those of the Greek and Roman statues. She had already given up powder and hip-pads”, though she had not dared go as far as Talma, “who had been seconded by David and by the Revolution.” Two small corrections to Lecky: the name is Saint-Huberty, and she was a singer of the Paris Opéra rather than an actress (on the same page Fusil praises her as the queen of Carthage and says she “wept while singing”).</p>

<h2>Mme Saint-Huberty before Talma (ch. III, p. 95)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: Ginisty edition, ch. III, p. 95, <a href="https://archive.org/details/souvenirsduneact00fusiuoft/page/n104/mode/2up">scan n104</a>. The chapter’s summary includes “Le salon de Mme Saint-Huberty. — Ses succès.” <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky’s note reports.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/fusil-souvenirs-p95.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/fusil-souvenirs-p95.jpg" alt="Printed page 95: “On a souvent répété que Talma était le premier qui eût fait révolution dans les costumes; mais Mme Saint-Huberty avait déjà commencé à imiter ceux des statues grecques et romaines. Elle avait déjà supprimé la poudre et les hanches …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Fusil, <em>Souvenirs d’une actrice</em>, Ginisty edition, p. 95. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (University of Toronto) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p><span class="cited-passage">On a souvent répété que Talma était le premier qui eût fait révolution dans les costumes; mais Mme Saint-Huberty avait déjà commencé à imiter ceux des statues grecques et romaines. Elle avait déjà supprimé la poudre et les hanches</span>, et si l’on recherchait dans les costumes du temps, il serait facile de s’en convaincre. Cependant elle n’avait pas encore osé les aborder aussi franchement que Talma, qui avait été secondé par David et par la Révolution.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p><span class="cited-passage">It has often been said that Talma was the first to make a revolution in costume; but Mme Saint-Huberty had already begun to imitate the costumes of the Greek and Roman statues. She had already given up powder and hip-pads</span>, and anyone who looked into the costumes of the time could easily satisfy himself of it. Still, she had not yet dared to take them up as boldly as Talma, who had been seconded by David and by the Revolution.</p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation: this edition’s draft. “Les hanches”: the padding worn at the hips under eighteenth-century stage dresses.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. V, note 138): “The first step, according to Madame Fusil (<i>Souvenirs d’une Actrice</i>, pp. 27–54), in this direction was taken by an actress named Madame Saint-Hubert, who discarded powder and took the ancient sculptures as her model; but it was the genius of Talma, warmly seconded by the antiquarians, by the revolutionists, and especially by the Girondins, that finally vanquished the prevailing prejudice.” The David and the Revolution of Lecky’s text and note are already in Fusil’s sentence; the antiquarians and the Girondins are not on this page.</p>
