abstract: |
  <p>Lecky’s sketch of the pagan hell leans on Denis twice. The “faint traces of a doctrine of hell, which are said to have been elaborated by Pythagoras” are, in Denis, “a very old book on the underworld, attributed to Pythagoras”, in which Mercury leads pure souls to heaven and hands the rest to the Furies, “who torment them and bind them with unbreakable chains”. Denis says only that this was “probably” the doctrine of that book; Lecky’s “said to have been” keeps the caution.</p>
  <p>For Plato, “who did more than any other ancient philosopher to develope the notion of expiation”, Denis’s pages show a Plato who found expiation already in popular rites (the judgment below, sacrifices, fasts and purifications) and purified it: what we must fear is the crime, not the punishment, and punishment is “a remedy for inner corruption”, not a scarecrow.</p>

Mercure, y est-il dit, tire les âmes du corps et de la terre, et conduit celles qui sont pures au ciel. <p><span class="cited-passage">It is probably this last doctrine that was set out in a very old book on the underworld, attributed to Pythagoras. Mercury, it says, draws the souls out of the body and the earth, and leads those that are pure to heaven. The others are handed over to the Furies, who torment them and bind them with unbreakable chains.</span></p>

<p>“This last doctrine” is, in the sentences just before (read in the OCR), the form of transmigration in which souls are rewarded or punished in another world for a fixed time before returning to new bodies, as in the myth of Er and the sixth book of the <em>Aeneid</em>. The paragraph continues on p. 19.</p>

<h2>Plato and the necessity of expiation (pp. 105–106)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: tome I, pp. 105–106, <a href="https://archive.org/details/histoiredestho01deniuoft/page/n116/mode/2up">scan n116</a>–<a href="https://archive.org/details/histoiredestho01deniuoft/page/n117/mode/2up">n117</a>; Lecky cites pp. 104–106. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the notion of expiation Lecky credits to Plato.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/denis-idees-morales-i-p105.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/denis-idees-morales-i-p105.jpg" alt="Printed pages: “Cependant Platon ne prétendait rien dire d’extraordinaire; il retrouvait cette nécessité morale de l’expiation dans les croyances naïves et traditionnelles de l’humanité … il ne voit en elle qu’un remède à la corruption intérieure.”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Denis, tome I, pp. 105 (above) and 106. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p><span class="cited-passage">Cependant Platon ne prétendait rien dire d’extraordinaire; il retrouvait cette nécessité morale de l’expiation dans les croyances naïves et traditionnelles de l’humanité.</span> Que signifie en effet ce jugement, qui nous attend aux enfers? Que signifient ces cérémonies religieuses, ces sacrifices expiatoires, ces offrandes, ces jeûnes, ces purifications? Platon, qui aime à citer les symboles, ne fait que dégager les vérités cachées sous ces fictions poétiques; mais en les dégageant, il les épure. Ces symboles exprimaient naïvement et la nécessité de la réparation du crime, et la terreur de la punition. Platon montre que c’est le crime, et non la punition qu’il faut craindre, et qu’on doit se préserver du péché, non parce qu’il traîne après lui des châtiments inévitables, mais uniquement parce qu’il est mauvais et qu’il dégrade la meilleure partie de nous-mêmes. <span class="cited-passage">Il ne considère point, comme le font presque toutes les religions, la punition comme un épouvantail et comme un frein; il ne voit en elle qu’un remède à la corruption intérieure.</span></p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p><span class="cited-passage">Yet Plato did not claim to be saying anything extraordinary; he found this moral necessity of expiation in the simple, traditional beliefs of mankind.</span> What, after all, is the meaning of the judgment that awaits us in the underworld? What do these religious ceremonies mean, these expiatory sacrifices, offerings, fasts and purifications? Plato, who likes to cite the symbols, only draws out the truths hidden under these poetic fictions; but in drawing them out he purifies them. The symbols expressed, simply, both the necessity of making amends for crime and the terror of punishment. Plato shows that it is the crime, not the punishment, that we must fear, and that we should keep ourselves from sin not because it drags inevitable chastisements after it, but solely because it is evil and degrades the best part of ourselves. <span class="cited-passage">He does not regard punishment, as nearly all religions do, as a scarecrow and a curb; he sees in it only a remedy for inner corruption.</span></p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation: this edition’s draft.</p>
</div>
</div>
