abstract: |
  <p>In Chapter III Lecky uses Tertullian to show that the early Church taught that the fear of hell was a moral motive of an altogether higher order than any earthly punishment. Note 136 gives, in Latin, a passage of the <em>Apology</em> (c. 45) in which Tertullian says that Epicurus makes light of pain by calling it either small and therefore contemptible, or great and therefore not lasting, while Christians, who live under a God who sees everything and foresee an eternal penalty, rightly fear it.</p><p>Lecky’s Latin is accurate, but he leaves out one clause without a mark, and adds an ellipsis where the original has none. See <a href="#comparison">the comparison</a>.</p>

<section id="apol-45" class="passage">
<h2>Apology, Chapter 45</h2>

<p class="passage-source">Latin: Migne, <em>PL</em> 1, cols. 499–500 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_patrologiae-cursus-completus-_1844_1/page/n267/mode/2up">scan</a>). Spelling regularised (<em>æ</em> as <em>ae</em>). The passage begins at the foot of col. 499 with the word “Sic”. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the words Lecky quotes.</p>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p><span class="cited-passage">Sic et Epicurus omnem cruciatum doloremque depretiat, modicum quidem contemptibilem pronuntiando, magnum vero non diuturnum.</span> <span class="cited-passage">Enimvero nos qui sub Deo omnium speculatore dispungimur, quique aeternam ab eo poenam providemus, merito soli innocentiae occurrimus, et pro scientiae plenitudine, et pro latebrarum difficultate, et pro magnitudine cruciatus, non diuturni, verum sempiterni,</span> eum timentes, quem timere debebit et ipse qui timentes iudicat, Deum, non proconsulem timentes.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p><span class="cited-passage">So too Epicurus makes light of every torment and pain, declaring a moderate one contemptible and a great one not lasting.</span> <span class="cited-passage">But we, who are judged under God as the watcher of all things, and who foresee eternal punishment from him, rightly run to meet innocence alone, in view of the fullness of our knowledge, and the difficulty of hiding-places, and the greatness of a torment not lasting but everlasting,</span> fearing him whom he too must fear who judges those who fear, God, not the proconsul.</p>
</div>
</div>

<details class="collapse">
<summary>Doubtful points</summary>
<p>The passage is hard Latin (<em>dispungimur</em>, “are reckoned up, examined”; <em>latebrarum difficultate</em>, “the difficulty of hiding-places”, i.e. of escape from God’s sight). The translation is a first draft and may be improved. The sentence “Sic” at the head of this extract continues an argument begun on the left-hand column, which I did not transcribe. Migne’s notes and variants are omitted.</p>
</details>

<p>Differences: (1) the three dots after “diuturnum” mark a gap that is not there in Migne, whose next word is “Enimvero” (an edition with an intervening sentence is possible; I have not checked); (2) “et pro latebrarum difficultate” (“and for the difficulty of hiding-places”) is missing from Lecky’s text without a mark, which makes “plenitudine” and “magnitudine” sit directly together; (3) he ends at “sempiterni”, before the clause that says what the fear is directed at, the God “whom the judge too must fear”, not the proconsul. Spelling and punctuation are otherwise modernised. The sense Lecky wants (a fear of everlasting torment makes Christians the more careful) is plainly in the passage.</p>
