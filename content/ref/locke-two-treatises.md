abstract: |
  <p>Lecky uses Locke as the turning point at which English political thought stopped arguing from Scripture and started arguing from consent. His three quotations are all genuine, but each is slightly smoothed. The most interesting change is in the famous sentence on taxation: Locke condemns anyone who levies taxes “by his own authority, and without such consent of the people”; Lecky drops “by his own authority”. And the Preface shows why Locke bothered to refute Filmer at all, a writer he thought “glib nonsense”: because “the pulpit, of late years, publicly owned his doctrine”.</p>

<section id="taxes" class="passage">
<h2>Taxes without consent (Second Treatise, ch. XI, § 140)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: Second Treatise, chapter XI (“Of the Extent of the Legislative Power”), § 140. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: Lecky’s quotation (note 100).</p>
<blockquote>
<p>It is true, governments cannot be supported without great charge, and it is fit every one who enjoys his share of the protection, should pay out of his estate his proportion for the maintenance of it. But still it must be with his own consent, i.e. the consent of the majority, giving it either by themselves, or their representatives chosen by them: for <span class="cited-passage">if any one shall claim a power to lay and levy taxes on the people,</span> by his own authority, and <span class="cited-passage">without such consent of the people, he thereby invades the fundamental law of property, and subverts the end of government</span>: for what property have I in that, which another may by right take, when he pleases, to himself?</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky: “If anyone shall claim a power to lay <strong>or</strong> levy taxes on the people without <strong>their</strong> consent …”; he omits “by his own authority”.</p>
</section>

<section id="good-of-the-community" class="passage">
<h2>The end of government (Second Treatise, ch. XIV, § 163)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: Second Treatise, chapter XIV (“Of Prerogative”), § 163. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: Lecky’s quotation (note 98).</p>
<blockquote>
<p>And therefore they have a very wrong notion of government, who say, that the people have encroached upon the prerogative, when they have got any part of it to be defined by positive laws … for <span class="cited-passage">the end of government being the good of the community, whatsoever alterations are made in it, tending to that end, cannot be an encroachment upon any body, since no body in government can have a right tending to any other end</span>: and those only are encroachments which prejudice or hinder the public good.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky has “whatever” for “whatsoever” and “any right” for “a right”.</p>
</section>

<section id="filmer" class="passage">
<h2>Why answer Filmer? (Preface)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: the Preface to the <em>Two Treatises</em>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: Lecky’s quotation (note 97).</p>
<blockquote>
<p>… if any one … will but take the pains himself … to strip Sir Robert’s discourses of the flourish of doubtful expressions, and endeavour to reduce his words to direct, positive, intelligible propositions, and then compare them one with another, he will quickly be satisfied, there was never so much glib nonsense put together in well-sounding English. … <span class="cited-passage">I should not speak so plainly of a gentleman, long since past answering, had not the pulpit, of late years, publicly owned his doctrine, and made it the current divinity of the times.</span> It is necessary those men, who taking on them to be teachers, have so dangerously misled others, should be openly shewed of what authority this their Patriarch is, whom they have so blindly followed …</p>
</blockquote>
</section>
