abstract: |
  <p>Why was the stag a symbol of Christ? Lecky gives two reasons, “because it bears the cross on its forehead, and from an old notion that it was the irreconcilable enemy of serpents”, and adds that stags “were invested with a kind of prophetic power”. All three come from Maury’s pages. The serpent story has the best detail, from a note in which Maury quotes Origen: the stag smells out where serpents hide, puts its mouth to the hole and draws them out with its breath so strongly that they come out and fall between its teeth; and after eating them it is so thirsty that it runs to the springs, and if it goes three hours without drinking it dies. So the psalmist’s hart that “panteth after the water brooks” became the Christian who has swallowed the old serpent’s poison and runs to the fountain of divine mercy.</p>

<section id="stag" class="passage">
<h2>“Un ennemi acharné du serpent”: the stag (pp. 174–178)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: Paris 1843, p. 176, <a href="https://archive.org/details/essaisurleslge00mauruoft/page/n215/mode/2up">scan</a>, with Maury’s note 2, and p. 178, <a href="https://archive.org/details/essaisurleslge00mauruoft/page/n217/mode/2up">scan</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky draws on.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/maury-legendes-1843-p176.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/maury-legendes-1843-p176.jpg" alt="Printed French page: “Dans les superstitions des premiers âges, le cerf était encore regardé comme un ennemi acharné du serpent”, with a note quoting Origen and Chrysostom" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Maury, p. 176, with the note on Origen. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>[p. 174, from the OCR] … des cerfs portant réellement sur leur tête l’instrument de la passion … Le cerf … non seulement exprimait emblématiquement Jésus-Christ, mais était encore l’image de l’âme altérée de la parole divine, d’après ces paroles du psaume (XLI, 1): «&#8239;<i lang="la">Quemadmodum desiderat cervus ad fontes aquarum, ita desiderat anima mea ad te, Deus.</i>&#8239;»</p>
<p>[p. 176] <span class="cited-passage">Dans les superstitions des premiers âges, le cerf était encore regardé comme un ennemi acharné du serpent, vivante image du démon, et cette idée fut un motif nouveau pour que le cerf devînt un symbole de Jésus-Christ.</span></p>
<p class="note">Note 2: Origène, Homél. XVII, in Genes., c. 5, dit que le cerf sent les lieux où sont cachés les serpens, et que, s’étant mis à l’entrée de leur trou, en tirant son haleine, il les attire à soi d’une telle force, qu’ils sortent et se jettent entre ses dents, où il les dévore; qu’aussitôt après les avoir mangés, il en est si altéré, qu’il court vers les fontaines pour se rafraîchir, et s’il demeure trois heures sans boire, il meurt aussitôt … «&#8239;<i lang="la">Non immerito animal comparatum est fidelibus</i>, dit saint Bruno … <i lang="la">serpentes naribus trahit quos ut voraverit aestuante veneno, ad fontem festinat. Hoc nos admonet ut quando venena haurimus antiqui serpentis, ad fontem divinae misericordiae festinemus.</i>&#8239;»</p>
<p>[p. 178] … <span class="cited-passage">La vertu prophétique déjà attribuée à cet animal</span> aura accrédité davantage ces histoires dont le merveilleux était plus qu’un appât suffisant pour la crédulité du vulgaire. Les saints auront été transformés en chasseurs, comme saint Eustache et saint Hubert, et le cerf mystérieux sera devenu le Sauveur qui, sous cette forme, leur était apparu.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>[p. 174] … stags really carrying the instrument of the Passion on their heads … The stag … not only stood emblematically for Jesus Christ, but was also the image of the soul thirsting for the divine word, from the words of the psalm (41 [42]:1): “As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God.”</p>
<p>[p. 176] <span class="cited-passage">In the superstitions of the first ages, the stag was also regarded as a relentless enemy of the serpent, the living image of the demon, and this idea was a new reason for the stag to become a symbol of Jesus Christ.</span></p>
<p class="note">Note 2: Origen (<i>Homily XVII on Genesis</i>, c. 5) says that the stag smells out the places where serpents are hidden, and that, putting itself at the mouth of their hole and drawing its breath, it pulls them towards itself with such force that they come out and throw themselves between its teeth, where it devours them; that as soon as it has eaten them it is so thirsty that it runs to the springs to refresh itself, and if it stays three hours without drinking it dies at once … “Not without reason is the animal compared to the faithful,” says St Bruno … “it draws serpents out with its nostrils, and when it has swallowed them, burning with the poison, it hurries to the spring. This teaches us that when we drink the venom of the old serpent we should hurry to the fountain of divine mercy.”</p>
<p>[p. 178] … <span class="cited-passage">The prophetic virtue already attributed to this animal</span> will have given these stories more credit, their marvels being more than enough bait for the credulity of the common people. The saints will have been turned into huntsmen, like St Eustace and St Hubert, and the mysterious stag will have become the Saviour who appeared to them in that form.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Maury’s note also quotes the same lore from John Chrysostom’s exposition of Psalm 41 (in Greek); this edition has not checked Origen, Chrysostom or Bruno.</p>
</section>

<section id="angels-stars" class="passage">
<h2>Angels carrying the sun, the moon and the star (pp. 17–18)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: Paris 1843, pp. 17–18, <a href="https://archive.org/details/essaisurleslge00mauruoft/page/n56/mode/2up">scans n56</a>–<a href="https://archive.org/details/essaisurleslge00mauruoft/page/n57/mode/2up">n57</a>; from the OCR only, not read on the images.</p>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>Les anges qui, plus tard, ont été transformés en de purs esprits occupés à servir Dieu et à transmettre ses ordres à la terre, n’ont été d’abord que les agents personnifiés de la nature … [p. 18] … on voit au-dessus de la croix où expire le Christ, le soleil et la lune, portés chacun par un ange. Suivant quelques pères de l’Église, notamment saint Jean-Chrysostôme, et certains évangiles apocryphes, c’est un ange qui portait l’étoile qui annonça aux bergers la naissance du fils de Marie. … Les tempêtes, les vents, les pluies furent l’œuvre des mauvais génies, des démons.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>The angels, who were later turned into pure spirits busy serving God and carrying his orders to the earth, were at first only the personified agents of nature … [p. 18] … above the cross on which Christ dies one sees the sun and the moon, each carried by an angel. According to some Fathers of the Church, notably St John Chrysostom, and certain apocryphal gospels, it was an angel who carried the star that announced to the shepherds the birth of Mary’s son. … Storms, winds and rains were the work of the evil spirits, the demons.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>This is the context of Lecky’s note 113 (“Angels were sometimes represented … bearing along the stars (and especially the Star of Bethlehem) in their hands”), and of his statement in the text that atmospheric changes were believed to be “effected by angels”. See also <a href="roccamora-de-cometis.html#angels">Roccamora on angels carrying the stars</a>.</p>
</section>
