abstract: |
  <p>A popular history of sorcery in France, published in 1818, which Lecky cites four times in the first twenty pages of Chapter I. Before the history proper, Garinet runs through the laws against sorcerers from Moses to the Roman emperors. Lecky’s sentences on ancient Greece and Rome (Augustine and the Epicureans, the Decemvirs, the Greek law, and the Athenian sorceress “Lemia”) follow this passage point by point.</p><p>Three things do not survive the trip. Lecky’s “pp. 13, 14” are really pp. xiii–xiv of the preface. Garinet says the philosophers <em>condemned</em> sorcerers; Lecky has them <em>believing</em> in sorcery. And “Lemia” is not a name the ancient sources give: the speech Garinet means names the sorceress as Theoris of Lemnos. See <a href="#lecky-summary">the comparison below</a>.</p>

<section id="discours-xiii" class="passage">
<h2>Discours sur l’imagination, pp. xiii–xiv: Ancient Laws Against Sorcerers</h2>

<p class="passage-source">French: Paris, 1818, pp. xiii–xiv (<a href="https://archive.org/details/histoiredelamagi00gari/page/n16/mode/2up">scan</a>), spelling as printed. Garinet’s footnotes follow the text. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the statements Lecky reproduces.</p>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>Tous les législateurs ont porté des peines contre les insensés qui se croient sorciers; et <span class="cited-passage">saint Augustin et Plutarque ont observé que les différentes sectes de philosophes les ont condamnés, à l’exception des seuls épicuriens, qui niaient l’existence des esprits.</span></p>
<p>Dans les lois que Moïse donna au peuple juif, on trouve des dispositions, non-seulement contre les sorciers, mais encore contre ceux qui les consultent (1).</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">Les Grecs avaient une loi très-expresse,</span> qui porte que tout sorcier ou magicien qui, par charmes, paroles, ligatures, images de cire ou autre maléfice, enchantent ou charment quelqu’un, ou qui s’en servent pour faire périr les hommes ou le bétail, <span class="cited-passage">soient punis de mort.</span> Platon conseille à ceux qui se marient, de prendre garde à ces charmes ou ligatures, qui troublent la paix des ménages (2).</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">En vertu de cette loi, Lemia, sorcière d’Athènes, fut punie du dernier supplice, au rapport de Démosthène.</span> Pausanias ajoute que cette république établit une chambre de justice, destinée à punir les sorciers (1).</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">Nous voyons cette législation adoptée, dans les lois des douze tables.</span> La tête du sorcier est dévouée à Cérès (2).</p>
<p class="source-notes">(1) Exode, ch. xxii, v. 18. — Lévitique, ch. xix, v. 31; ch. xx, v. 6.<br>(2) Platon, <em>des Lois</em>, liv. xi.<br>[p. xiv] (1) Pausanias. Mœlia, lib. v.<br>(2) <em lang="la">Qui fruges excantasset cereri necanto</em>, liv. vii, table 3<sup>e</sup>.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>All lawgivers have laid down penalties against the madmen who believe themselves sorcerers; and <span class="cited-passage">Saint Augustine and Plutarch have observed that the different sects of philosophers condemned them, with the sole exception of the Epicureans, who denied the existence of spirits.</span></p>
<p>In the laws that Moses gave the Jewish people there are provisions not only against sorcerers but also against those who consult them (1).</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">The Greeks had a very explicit law,</span> which provides that every sorcerer or magician who, by charms, spoken words, ligatures, wax images, or any other evil spell, enchants or bewitches anyone, or uses them to bring about the death of men or cattle, <span class="cited-passage">shall be punished with death.</span> Plato advises those who marry to beware of these charms or ligatures, which trouble the peace of households (2).</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">Under this law Lemia, a sorceress of Athens, suffered the extreme penalty, as Demosthenes reports.</span> Pausanias adds that this republic set up a court of justice for the punishment of sorcerers (1).</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">We see this legislation adopted in the laws of the Twelve Tables.</span> The sorcerer’s head is forfeit to Ceres (2).</p>
<p class="source-notes">(1) Exodus 22:18; Leviticus 19:31, 20:6.<br>(2) Plato, <em>Laws</em>, book xi.<br>[p. xiv] (1) Pausanias, “Mœlia” [sic], book v.<br>(2) “Whoever has charmed away crops … let them put him to death for Ceres”, book vii, third table.</p>
</div>
</div>

<details class="collapse">
<summary>Who was “Lemia”?</summary>
<p>No ancient source names an Athenian sorceress Lemia. The passage Garinet means is in the speech <em>Against Aristogeiton</em> (I), §79, which has come down under Demosthenes’ name (its authenticity has long been doubted). There the speaker mentions “<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoris_of_Lemnos">Theoris of Lemnos</a>, the filthy sorceress whom you put to death on that account with all her family” (A. T. Murray’s translation, Loeb, 1939). In the Greek she is “the Lemnian” (<span lang="grc">Λημνία</span>), so “Lemia” looks like a garbled “Lemnia”, an adjective of origin mistaken for a personal name. Whether the slip is Garinet’s own or comes from an earlier French writer he copied has not been checked.</p>
<p>Garinet’s note on the Twelve Tables also looks garbled. The clause <em lang="la">qui fruges excantassit</em> (“whoever has charmed away crops”) is the Twelve Tables’ law against crop-charming as quoted by Pliny the Elder; “Cereri necanto” (“let them kill for Ceres”) resembles the Tables’ separate penalty for grazing or cutting another’s crops by night. Garinet’s note runs the two together. This is this edition’s reading of the note, not checked against Pliny’s text.</p>
</details>
</section>

<section id="lecky-summary" class="passage">
<h2>Lecky’s Summary Compared</h2>

<p>Lecky does not quote Garinet; he condenses him, on <a href="https://archive.org/details/rationalismeuro01leckuoft/page/19/mode/2up">p. 19</a> of Chapter I (<a href="../rationalism/vol1-ch1.html#fn14">note 14</a>). Point by point:</p>

<div class="table-wrap">
<table class="collation">
<thead>
<tr><th></th><th>Lecky, 1865</th><th>Garinet, 1818</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><th>Authorities</th><td>“St. Augustine assures us”</td><td lang="fr">“saint Augustin <strong>et Plutarque</strong> ont observé”</td></tr>
<tr><th>The philosophers</th><td>all the sects “<strong>admitted</strong> it” (the belief that evil can be inflicted by supernatural agency)</td><td lang="fr">the sects of philosophers “<strong>les ont condamnés</strong>” (condemned sorcerers)</td></tr>
<tr><th>The Epicureans</th><td>excepted, as they “denied the existence of evil spirits”</td><td lang="fr">excepted, as they “niaient l’existence des esprits” (denied the existence of spirits)</td></tr>
<tr><th>Order</th><td>Decemvirs first, then Greece</td><td>Greece first, then the Twelve Tables</td></tr>
<tr><th>The sorceress</th><td>“Lemia”, in the days of Demosthenes</td><td lang="fr">“Lemia, sorcière d’Athènes”, on Demosthenes’ report</td></tr>
<tr><th>Page reference</th><td>“pp. 13, 14”</td><td>pp. xiii–xiv (<a href="https://archive.org/details/histoiredelamagi00gari/page/n16/mode/2up">scan</a>); arabic pp. 13–14 are about Clovis</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>

<p>What the differences mean:</p>
<ul>
<li>The page numbers are a simple slip: the roman numerals of the preface became arabic in Lecky’s note. Anyone following his reference to pp. 13–14 lands in the reign of Clovis.</li>
<li>Garinet’s sentence is muddled: it says the Epicureans did <em>not</em> condemn sorcerers because they denied spirits, which only makes sense if “condemned” means “took seriously”. Lecky’s “admitted it” is the reading that makes the sentence coherent, and it is also the one his argument needs. Whether Augustine actually says this has not been checked here.</li>
<li>“Lemia” passes unchanged from Garinet to Lecky, so Lecky evidently took the case from Garinet without going back to the Greek.</li>
</ul>
</section>

<section id="famine-38" class="passage">
<h2>Pp. 38 and 42: The Famine of 795 and the Eclipse (Louis the Debonair)</h2>

<p class="passage-source">French: Paris, 1818, p. 38 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/histoiredelamagi00gari/page/n95/mode/2up">scan</a>) and p. 42 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/histoiredelamagi00gari/page/n99/mode/2up">scan</a>), spelling as printed. Garinet’s footnotes follow each passage. “[…]” marks omitted text. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the statements Lecky reproduces.</p>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p><em>P. 38.</em> On y lisait que les païens, les sorciers, et généralement tous ceux qui ne voudraient pas payer la dîme s’exposaient à ne rien récolter; et Jésus-Christ, sortant de son caractère de clémence, menaçait d’envoyer dans les maisons des impies, des serpens ailés qui dévoreraient le sein de leurs femmes…. (1)</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">Une grande famine qui survint, en 795, confirma la première menace.</span> On avait trouvé tous les épis de blé vides (2); des personnes dignes de foi entendirent en l’air plusieurs voix de démons, <span class="cited-passage">qui déclarèrent qu’ils avaient dévoré la moisson, parce qu’on ne payait pas les dîmes aux ecclésiastiques: c’est pourquoi il fut ordonné, sous des peines sévères, qu’on les paierait à l’avenir.</span></p>
<p class="source-notes">(1) Mably, obs. 5. Histoire de France, liv. II.<br>(2) Capitulaires, an 794, art. 23.</p>
<p><em>P. 42.</em> […] Le Débonnaire, fatigué du voyage, et rebuté d’avoir toujours ses enfans à combattre, tomba dangereusement malade. <span class="cited-passage">Une éclipse totale de soleil, qui survint la veille de l’Ascension, effraya encore cet esprit, que les malheurs et la superstition avaient affaibli (1). La peur augmentant son mal,</span> l’empereur se fit porter dans une île près de Mayence. […]</p>
<p class="source-notes">(1) Le président Hénault. — Louis-le-Débonnaire passait cependant pour un grand astronome, et s’effrayait d’une éclipse; mais tout s’allie dans des têtes aussi faibles.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p><em>P. 38.</em> It was read there that pagans, sorcerers, and in general all who would not pay the tithe exposed themselves to reaping nothing; and Jesus Christ, departing from his character of clemency, threatened to send into the houses of the impious winged serpents that would devour the breasts of their wives…. (1)</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">A great famine that followed, in 795, confirmed the first threat.</span> All the ears of corn were found empty (2); people worthy of belief heard in the air several voices of demons, <span class="cited-passage">who declared that they had devoured the harvest because the tithes were not being paid to the clergy; whereupon it was ordered, under severe penalties, that they should be paid in future.</span></p>
<p class="source-notes">(1) Mably, obs. 5, <em>Histoire de France</em>, book II.<br>(2) Capitularies, year 794, art. 23.</p>
<p><em>P. 42.</em> […] The Debonair, weary from the journey and disheartened at always having his children to fight, fell dangerously ill. <span class="cited-passage">A total eclipse of the sun, which occurred on the eve of the Ascension, frightened once more that mind, which misfortunes and superstition had weakened (1). Fear increasing his illness,</span> the emperor had himself carried to an island near Mainz. […]</p>
<p class="source-notes">(1) President Hénault. — Louis the Debonair nevertheless passed for a great astronomer, and was frightened by an eclipse; but everything is combined in such weak minds.</p>
</div>
</div>

<details class="collapse">
<summary>Which year, and which king?</summary>
<p>Garinet does not date the eclipse. The paragraph before the one quoted opens “En 839”, and the emperor is Louis the Pious (“le Débonnaire”), whom Garinet treats as a French king. The “Mably” and “Hénault” in the notes are the eighteenth-century historians Gabriel Bonnot de Mably and Charles-Jean-François Hénault; the notes name them but do not give pages for the eclipse. The reference “Capitulaires, an 794, art. 23” is Garinet’s own, and has not been traced to its text here.</p>
</details>
</section>

<section id="famine-eclipse-lecky" class="passage">
<h2>Lecky’s Use of Pp. 38 and 42 Compared</h2>

<p>Lecky’s sentences are in <a href="../rationalism/vol1-ch1.html#fn39">notes 39</a> and <a href="../rationalism/vol1-ch1.html#fn40">40</a> of Chapter I (<a href="https://archive.org/details/rationalismeuro01leckuoft/page/44/mode/2up">p. 44</a>): “in the eighth century, a fearful famine afflicted France, and was generally represented as a consequence of the repugnance which the French people manifested to the payment of tithes” (Garinet, p. 38), and “in the ninth century, a total eclipse of the sun struck terror through Europe, and is said to have been one of the causes of the death of a French king” (<em>Ibid.</em>, p. 42). Point by point:</p>
