abstract: |
  <p>Lecky ends his chapter on miracles by pointing to the most sophisticated Victorian defence of them: an article that accepts the universal reign of natural law and then widens “nature” to include the action of mind and will, human and divine, so that a miracle need not break any law at all. The reviewer’s starting point is the difficulty Lecky himself presses: “to a man thoroughly possessed of the idea of natural law as universal, nothing ever could be admitted as supernatural”. The books under review, listed at the head of the article, include Guizot, the Scottish philosopher James McCosh, Horace Bushnell, and Mansel’s essay on miracles in <em>Aids to Faith</em>, which Lecky cites in the next notes. The reviewer’s proof-text for the wider sense of nature is Wordsworth (“Tintern Abbey”), slightly misquoted.</p>

<section id="reign-of-law" class="passage">
<h2>“The reign of law in nature is, indeed, … universal” (p. 380)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: vol. 116 (October 1862), p. 380, <a href="https://archive.org/details/sim_edinburgh-review-critical-journal_1862-10_116_236/page/n85/mode/2up">scan</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the sentences Lecky quotes (with omissions, marked by him with dots).</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/edinburgh-review-1862-supernatural-p380.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/edinburgh-review-1862-supernatural-p380.jpg" alt="A double-column-free page headed “380 The Supernatural. Oct.”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>“The Supernatural”, <i>Edinburgh Review</i>, October 1862, p. 380. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>But let us observe exactly where and how the difficulty arises. <span class="cited-passage">The reign of law in nature is, indeed, so far as we can observe it, universal. But the common idea of the ‘supernatural’ is that which is at variance with natural law, above it, or in violation of it.</span> Nothing, however wonderful, which happens according to natural law, would be considered by any one as ‘supernatural.’ The law in obedience to which a wonderful thing happens may not be known; but this would not give it a supernatural character, so long as we assuredly believe that it did happen according to <i>some</i> law. <span class="cited-passage">Hence it would appear to follow that to a man thoroughly possessed of the idea of natural law as universal, nothing ever could be admitted as supernatural</span>; because on seeing any fact, however new, marvellous, or incomprehensible, he might escape into the conclusion that it was the result of some natural law of which he had before been ignorant. No one will deny that, in respect to the vast majority of all new and marvellous phenomena, this would be the true and reasonable conclusion. It is not the conclusion of pride, but of humility of mind. … <span class="cited-passage">But then</span>, to justify this conclusion, <span class="cited-passage">we must understand</span> ‘<span class="cited-passage">nature</span>’ in the largest sense,—as <span class="cited-passage">including</span> all that is</p>
<p style="text-align:center">‘In the round world, and in the living air,<br>And the blue sky, and in the mind of man.’</p>
<p>We must understand it as <span class="cited-passage">including every agency which we see entering, or can conceive from analogy as capable of entering, into the causation of the world.</span> First and foremost among these is the agency of our own mind and will.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky compresses the third sentence (“But then we must understand nature as including every agency …”) and has “capable of entering” for “as capable of entering”. The verse is from Wordsworth’s “Lines written a few miles above Tintern Abbey”, where it reads “And the round ocean and the living air”.</p>
</section>
