abstract: |
  <p>Lecky writes that among the Greeks “nakedness was associated rather with dignity than with shame. The gods, it was emphatically said, were naked”, and his only authority is three words of Seneca: “Deus nudus est.”</p>
  <p>In context the words have nothing to do with the body or with art. Seneca, a Roman Stoic, is telling Lucilius how a man may become “equal to God”: not by money, “for God has nothing”; not by the purple-bordered toga of a magistrate, “for God is naked”; not by fame, for no one knows God. “Naked” means stripped of rank and possessions. The saying is about one god, the Stoic deity, and about the worthlessness of office, not about the gods of Greek sculpture.</p>

<section id="deus-nudus-est" class="passage">
<h2>“Deus nudus est” (§§ 9–10)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin: Seneca, <em>Ep.</em> XXXI §§ 9–10, Gummere’s Loeb text (1917), <a href="https://archive.org/details/adluciliumepistu01seneuoft/page/n249/mode/2up">scan n249</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the words Lecky quotes.</p>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>“Quomodo,” inquis, “isto pervenitur?” Non per Poeninum Graiumve montem nec per deserta Candaviae, nec Syrtes tibi nec Scylla aut Charybdis adeundae sunt, quae tamen omnia transisti procuratiunculae pretio; tutum iter est, iucundum est, ad quod natura te instruxit. Dedit tibi illa, quae si non deserueris, par deo surges. Parem autem te deo pecunia non faciet; deus nihil habet. Praetexta non faciet; <span class="cited-passage">deus nudus est</span>. Fama non faciet nec ostentatio tui et in populos nominis dimissa notitia; nemo novit deum, multi de illo male existimant, et inpune.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p>“How,” you ask, “does one get there?” You need not cross the Pennine or the Graian Alps, nor the wastes of Candavia; you need not face the Syrtes, or Scylla, or Charybdis, though you have passed through all of them for the price of a petty governorship. The road for which nature has equipped you is safe and pleasant. She has given you gifts which, if you do not abandon them, will raise you to equality with God. But money will not make you God’s equal: God has nothing. The bordered robe of office will not: <span class="cited-passage">God is naked</span>. Fame will not, nor showing yourself off and spreading the knowledge of your name among the peoples: no one knows God, many think ill of him, and go unpunished.</p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation: this edition’s draft.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>The <em>praetexta</em> was the purple-bordered toga worn by Roman magistrates; Lucilius, as procurator, had a right to the official dress. Seneca’s point is that God lacks every mark of status, so a man does not need them to resemble him.</p>
