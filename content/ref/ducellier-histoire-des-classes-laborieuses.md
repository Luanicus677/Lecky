abstract: |
  <p>Lecky says the Church’s “Truce of God” was “the first effective barrier to the lawlessness of the barons”, and adds that it “was confirmed as part of the general law of the Church by Alexander III. in 1179”. Both come from Ducellier, and the second is his clause turned round: “outside the [royal] domain the Truce of God had fallen into disuse, although Alexander III had had it confirmed in 1179 as a general law of the Church.” Ducellier’s earlier page tells how the bishops, after fifty years of trying to impose a full “peace of God”, settled in 1041 for a truce from Wednesday evening to Monday morning, because they feared “disarming the sincere and religious, while the violent alone would stay armed”. His later page has a stranger episode: in 1183 a woodcutter of Le Puy, Pierre Durand, after a vision, founded the confraternity of the White Hoods, whose two hundred thousand members imposed the peace of God on the whole South, until it was denounced as a peasants’ rising and a speculation in blessed medals.</p>

<section id="truce-1041" class="passage">
<h2>The Truce of God, 1041 (p. 87)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: p. 87, <a href="https://archive.org/details/histoiredesclas00duce/page/n104/mode/2up">scan n104</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the truce and its terms.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/ducellier-1860-p87.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/ducellier-1860-p87.jpg" alt="Printed page 87: “Toutefois, une certaine résistance à ce mouvement enthousiaste se manifesta dans le sein même du clergé. … il fallut, après cinquante ans d’efforts, accepter une transaction, la trêve de Dieu, proposée en 1041. … Les hostilités étaient interdites depuis le mercredi jusqu’au lundi matin.”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Ducellier, <em>Histoire des classes laborieuses en France</em>, p. 87. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (University of Ottawa) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>Toutefois, une certaine résistance à ce mouvement enthousiaste se manifesta dans le sein même du clergé. Quelques évêques craignaient d’assumer une trop grande responsabilité en prenant ainsi la place du pouvoir séculier, en provoquant des serments qui ne pouvaient guère être tenus, et surtout en désarmant les hommes sincères et religieux, tandis que les hommes violents resteraient seuls en armes. Ces prévisions n’étaient que trop justes, et il fallut, après cinquante ans d’efforts, accepter une transaction, <span class="cited-passage">la <i>trêve de Dieu</i>, proposée en 1041</span>. Cette transaction fut acceptée partout, même dans la Normandie, qui s’y rallia au concile de Caen en 1142. Des pactes à peu près semblables, mais non pas uniformes, furent jurés dans tous les diocèses. <span class="cited-passage">Les hostilités étaient interdites depuis le mercredi jusqu’au lundi matin.</span></p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p>Even within the clergy, however, there was some resistance to this enthusiastic movement. Some bishops feared taking on too great a responsibility by thus putting themselves in place of the secular power, by provoking oaths that could hardly be kept, and above all by disarming the sincere and religious, while the violent alone would stay armed. These fears were only too justified, and after fifty years of effort a compromise had to be accepted, <span class="cited-passage">the <i>Truce of God</i>, proposed in 1041</span>. The compromise was accepted everywhere, even in Normandy, which joined it at the council of Caen in 1142 [<i>sic</i>]. Pacts more or less alike, though not uniform, were sworn in every diocese. <span class="cited-passage">Hostilities were forbidden from Wednesday until Monday morning.</span></p>

<section id="alexander-1179" class="passage">
<h2>Confirmed in 1179, already in disuse (p. 127)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: p. 127, <a href="https://archive.org/details/histoiredesclas00duce/page/n144/mode/2up">scan n144</a>, in the chapter on the reign of Philip Augustus and St Louis. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: Lecky’s fact.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/ducellier-1860-p127.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/ducellier-1860-p127.jpg" alt="Printed page 127: “En dehors du domaine, la trêve de Dieu était tombée en désuétude, bien qu’Alexandre III l’eût fait confirmer en 1179 comme loi générale de l’Église. … L’évêque du Puy avait bien, en 1183, approuvé la confrérie de l’Agnus Dei, ou des Capuchons blancs, fondée par le bûcheron Pierre Durand …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>p. 127. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (University of Ottawa) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>En dehors du domaine, la trêve de Dieu était tombée en désuétude, <span class="cited-passage">bien qu’Alexandre III l’eût fait confirmer en 1179 comme loi générale de l’Église.</span> Une grande partie du clergé hésitait à faire appel aux réunions populaires, redoutait la suite de ces soulèvements des associés de la paix contre les excommuniés que l’Église avait été autrefois unanime à provoquer. L’évêque du Puy avait bien, en 1183, approuvé la confrérie de l’<i>Agnus Dei</i>, ou des <i>Capuchons blancs</i>, fondée par le bûcheron Pierre Durand, à la suite d’une vision. Deux cent mille adhérents renouvelèrent la paix de Dieu dans tout le Midi, et l’imposèrent aux récalcitrants; mais, après un succès éphémère, cette tentative échoua. Elle avait, dès les premiers jours, provoqué beaucoup de défiances; elle était dénoncée comme une émeute des paysans contre l’autorité des seigneurs, et une spéculation sur la vente des médailles bénites. Elle finit par être désavouée par le haut clergé, et ne laissa d’autre trace que des confréries locales souvent suspectes.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p>Outside the [royal] domain the Truce of God had fallen into disuse, <span class="cited-passage">although Alexander III had had it confirmed in 1179 as a general law of the Church.</span> Much of the clergy hesitated to appeal to popular assemblies, and feared the consequences of those risings of the associates of the peace against excommunicated men which the Church had once been unanimous in provoking. The bishop of Le Puy had indeed, in 1183, approved the confraternity of the <i>Agnus Dei</i>, or of the <i>White Hoods</i>, founded by the woodcutter Pierre Durand after a vision. Two hundred thousand members renewed the peace of God throughout the South, and imposed it on the recalcitrant; but after a brief success the attempt failed. From the first days it had aroused much distrust; it was denounced as a peasants’ rising against the authority of the lords, and as a speculation in the sale of blessed medals. In the end it was disowned by the higher clergy, and left no other trace than local confraternities, often suspect.</p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation: this edition’s draft.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. V, note 2): “It was confirmed as part of the general law of the Church by Alexander III. in 1179.” His text adds that “in spite of many attempts to revive it” the truce lost its force once the royal power was consolidated; Ducellier’s next sentences (p. 127–128) make the same point, with Philip Augustus taking over the idea as a “trêve du roi”.</p>
