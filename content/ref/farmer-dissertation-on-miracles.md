abstract: |
  <p>If both God and the Devil can work miracles, a miracle proves nothing: that was the old difficulty with the argument from miracles, and Lecky notes that the dissenter Hugh Farmer tried to cut it out at the root by arguing “that there were no such things as diabolical miracles of any kind”. Farmer puts his purpose on his first page: Christianity’s proof is “that miraculous testimony, which was borne to those who first published it to the world. But unhappily for the interests of the Gospel, it’s most learned advocates have greatly impaired, if not destroyed, the force of this testimony, by asserting the power of invisible beings, of different and opposite characters, to work miracles.” The title says the rest: miracles are “Arguments of a Divine Interposition, and Absolute Proofs of the Mission and Doctrine of a Prophet”.</p>

<blockquote>
<p>The Christian revelation well deserves the esteem of mankind, on account of it’s intrinsic excellence; nevertheless, the proper proof of it’s divine original, is that miraculous testimony, which was borne to those who first published it to the world. <span class="cited-passage">But unhappily for the interests of the Gospel, it’s most learned advocates have greatly impaired, if not destroyed, the force of this testimony, by asserting the power of invisible beings, of different and opposite characters, to work miracles.</span></p>
<p>This opinion (than which scarce any has been more generally inculcated) has occasioned much perplexity to many sincere …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (ch. II, note 13): “Farmer, who was a dissenting minister, desired to destroy the difficulty arising from the fact that miracles were generally represented as attesting both truth and error. He attempted to show that there were no such things as diabolical miracles of any kind.” A fair account of the book’s aim, which its preface states in these words.</p>
