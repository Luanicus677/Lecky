abstract: |
  <p>Lecky mentions, among the commonplace wonders of the Middle Ages, the Virgin of the Pillar at Saragossa restoring an amputated leg, and adds that “Hume has noticed it”. It is usually identified with the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miracle_of_Calanda">Miracle of Calanda</a> of 1640, which Spanish theologians held to be unusually well attested. Hume uses it as his hardest case: the witness, <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_de_Retz">Cardinal de Retz</a>, was a sceptic and a man of genius who saw the man with two legs; the whole town and all the canons vouched for it; the miracle was of a kind that could hardly be faked. And yet, says Hume, the cardinal rightly dismissed it without being able to disprove it, because “such an evidence carried falsehood upon the very face of it”.</p>

<section id="saragossa" class="passage">
<h2>“A man who had served seven years as a door-keeper” (section X, part II)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: <em>Enquiry concerning Human Understanding</em>, section X, part II, from the Gutenberg text.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>There is also a memorable story related by Cardinal de Retz, which may well deserve our consideration. When that intriguing politician fled into Spain, to avoid the persecution of his enemies, he passed through Saragossa, the capital of Arragon, where he was shewn, in the cathedral, a man, who had served seven years as a door-keeper, and was well known to every body in town, that had ever paid his devotions at that church. He had been seen, for so long a time, wanting a leg; but recovered that limb by the rubbing of holy oil upon the stump; and the cardinal assures us that he saw him with two legs. This miracle was vouched by all the canons of the church; and the whole company in town were appealed to for a confirmation of the fact; whom the cardinal found, by their zealous devotion, to be thorough believers of the miracle. Here the relater was also cotemporary to the supposed prodigy, of an incredulous and libertine character, as well as of great genius; the miracle of so <i>singular</i> a nature as could scarcely admit of a counterfeit, and the witnesses very numerous, and all of them, in a manner, spectators of the fact, to which they gave their testimony. And what adds mightily to the force of the evidence, and may double our surprise on this occasion, is, that the cardinal himself, who relates the story, seems not to give any credit to it, and consequently cannot be suspected of any concurrence in the holy fraud. He considered justly, that it was not requisite, in order to reject a fact of this nature, to be able accurately to disprove the testimony, and to trace its falsehood, through all the circumstances of knavery and credulity which produced it. … He therefore concluded, like a just reasoner, that such an evidence carried falsehood upon the very face of it, and that a miracle, supported by any human testimony, was more properly a subject of derision than of argument.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky’s note describes a picture in the cathedral of angels fitting on a ready-made leg, and adds that “the more approved story is that the leg gradually grew”; in Hume’s version, from de Retz, the leg returned “by the rubbing of holy oil upon the stump”.</p>
</section>
