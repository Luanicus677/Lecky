abstract: |
  <p>Lecky observes that Lactantius, unlike later Fathers, “in the reign of Constantine, asserted the iniquity of persecution quite as strongly as any previous writer”. The chapter he cites is among the strongest statements of religious freedom in early Christian literature. “There is no need of force and injury, because religion cannot be compelled; the matter must be carried on by words rather than by blows, so that there may be free will.” And: “Religion is to be defended not by killing but by dying, not by cruelty but by patience, not by crime but by faith … For if you wish to defend religion by bloodshed and torture, it will not be defended but polluted and profaned. For nothing is so voluntary as religion.”</p>
  <p>The argument is aimed at the pagan persecutors, and it was soon forgotten by Christian emperors and bishops; Lecky’s chapter goes on to trace how.</p>

<h2>“Religio cogi non potest” (V.20, cols. 613–614)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin: Migne, PL 6, cols. 613–614, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_patrologiae-cursus-completus-_1844_6/page/n308/mode/2up">scan n308</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the sentences that make Lecky’s point.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/lactantius-inst-v-pl6-col613.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/lactantius-inst-v-pl6-col613.jpg" alt="Migne column: “Non est opus vi et injuria, quia religio cogi non potest: verbis potius quam verberibus res agenda est, ut sit voluntas. Distringant aciem ingeniorum suorum: si ratio eorum vera est, afferatur. Parati sumus audire, si doceant: tacentibus certe nihil credimus; sicut ne sævientibus quidem cedimus.”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Lactantius, <em>Divinae Institutiones</em> V.20, in Migne, PL 6. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>… <span class="cited-passage">Non est opus vi et injuria, quia religio cogi non potest: verbis potius quam verberibus res agenda est, ut sit voluntas.</span> Distringant aciem ingeniorum suorum: si ratio eorum vera est, afferatur. Parati sumus audire, si doceant: tacentibus certe nihil credimus; sicut ne sævientibus quidem cedimus.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p>… <span class="cited-passage">There is no need of force and injury, because religion cannot be compelled: the matter must be carried on by words rather than by blows, so that the will may be free.</span> Let them draw the sword of their wits: if their reasoning is true, let it be brought forward. We are ready to listen, if they teach; we certainly believe nothing from those who are silent, just as we do not yield to those who rage.</p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation: this edition’s draft.</p>
</div>
</div>
</section>

<section id="non-occidendo" class="passage">
<h2>“Non occidendo, sed moriendo” (V.20, cols. 615–616)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin: Migne, PL 6, cols. 615–616, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_patrologiae-cursus-completus-_1844_6/page/n309/mode/2up">scan n309</a>. The picture shows the foot of the left column (“Longe diversa sunt carnificina et pietas”) beside the head of the right.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/lactantius-inst-v-pl6-col615-616.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/lactantius-inst-v-pl6-col615-616.jpg" alt="Two Migne columns. Left: “Quid ergo sæviunt? … Longe diversa sunt carnificina et pietas; nec potest aut veritas cum vi, aut justitia cum crudelitate conjungi.” Right: “Defendenda enim religio est, non occidendo, sed moriendo; non sævitia, sed patientia; non scelere, sed fide … Nihil est enim tam voluntarium, quam religio …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>PL 6, cols. 615–616. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>Quid ergo sæviunt? ut stultitiam suam, dum minuere volunt, augeant. <span class="cited-passage">Longe diversa sunt carnificina et pietas; nec potest aut veritas cum vi, aut justitia cum crudelitate conjungi.</span> …</p>
<p>… <span class="cited-passage">Defendenda enim religio est, non occidendo, sed moriendo; non sævitia, sed patientia; non scelere, sed fide</span>: illa enim malorum sunt, hæc bonorum. Et necesse est, bonum in religione versari, non malum. <span class="cited-passage">Nam si sanguine, si tormentis, si malo religionem defendere velis, jam non defendetur illa, sed polluetur, atque violabitur. Nihil est enim tam voluntarium, quam religio</span>, in qua si animus sacrificantis aversus est, jam sublata, jam nulla est.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p>Why then do they rage? To increase their folly while they try to lessen it. <span class="cited-passage">Butchery and piety are far apart; neither can truth be joined with force, nor justice with cruelty.</span> …</p>
<p>… <span class="cited-passage">For religion is to be defended, not by killing, but by dying; not by cruelty, but by patience; not by crime, but by faith</span>: the first belong to the wicked, the second to the good, and in religion there must be good, not evil. <span class="cited-passage">For if you wish to defend religion by bloodshed, by torture, by evil, it will not be defended but polluted and profaned. For nothing is so voluntary as religion</span>: if the mind of the worshipper is turned away from it, it is already taken away, it is nothing.</p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation: this edition’s draft.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Lecky’s note (vol. II, ch. IV, note 11) adds that the Institutions “were mainly written, or at least published, at Treves during the reign of Constantine”, so that this is a Christian voice for toleration from inside the new Christian empire, not only from the persecuted Church.</p>
</section>
