abstract: |
  <p>Lecky writes that <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Jurieu">Jurieu</a> “strenuously asserted” the Protestants’ right to resist religious persecution, “although <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Saurin">Saurin</a> is said to have taken the opposite view”, and cites Michelet. Michelet’s page is harsher than Lecky’s “is said”: in 1689 Jurieu stood “seul contre Bossuet”, disowned by Geneva and mocked by Bayle, while “le doux Saurin, le pacifique Basnage” did worse than disagree: they “will act to suppress resistance, and will do Louis XIV the vital service of sapping the drive of the Cévennes”, presumably the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camisard">Camisard</a> rising of the Protestant peasants of the Cévennes after 1702.</p>
  <p>On the same page Michelet admits that Bossuet’s case for Christian non-resistance was hard to answer: Grotius allowed resistance to man but not to the Christian, and Milton and Sidney had to lean on the Old Testament. On the next page Jurieu answers that if the first Christians’ example forbade all resistance it proved too much: they would have had to let thieves rob them too.</p>

<h2>Jurieu alone; Saurin and Basnage for obedience (p. 432)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: 1860, p. 432, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_U71JAAAAcAAJ/page/n455/mode/2up">scan n455</a>, under the years 1689–1690. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky’s note refers to.</p>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>Cela avait arrêté Grotius. Il établit le droit de résistance pour l’homme, mais non pour le chrétien, lié par l’Évangile. Milton ne reprend pied qu’en laissant l’Évangile, et s’appuyant sur l’Ancien Testament. Il en est de même de Sidney, dans son livre si fort, si net, mais très-biblique encore. Milton, Sidney, sont des Anglais; la liberté de Milton ne s’appuierait que sur une chambre des lords non héréditaire, et la liberté de Sidney sur la monarchie mixte et tempérée des trois pouvoirs.</p>
<p>Sidney, du reste, n’était pas imprimé. Locke n’avait pas écrit. <span class="cited-passage">Jurieu, en 1689, est seul contre Bossuet. Seul, nullement appuyé des siens. Bien plus, désavoué de Genève, de l’école d’obéissance. Bien plus, moqué de Bayle, des indifférents, des sceptiques. Le doux Saurin, le pacifique Basnage, feront bien plus; ils agiront pour supprimer les résistances, et rendront à Louis XIV l’essentiel service d’énerver l’élan des Cévennes.</span></p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p>That is what had stopped Grotius. He established the right of resistance for the man, but not for the Christian, who is bound by the Gospel. Milton finds his feet again only by leaving the Gospel and leaning on the Old Testament. So too Sidney, in his book, so strong, so clear, but still very biblical. Milton and Sidney are Englishmen; Milton’s liberty would rest only on a non-hereditary House of Lords, and Sidney’s on the mixed monarchy, tempered by the three powers.</p>
<p>Sidney, besides, was not yet printed. Locke had not yet written. <span class="cited-passage">Jurieu, in 1689, stands alone against Bossuet. Alone, not supported at all by his own side. Worse, disowned by Geneva, by the school of obedience. Worse still, mocked by Bayle, by the indifferent, by the sceptics. The gentle Saurin and the peaceable Basnage will do much more: they will act to suppress resistance, and will render Louis XIV the vital service of sapping the drive of the Cévennes.</span></p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation: this edition’s draft.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. V, text at note 107): “Jurieu strenuously asserted the first right; and although Saurin is said to have taken the opposite view …” Lecky reports Saurin’s view as hearsay; Michelet presents it as fact and adds Basnage, and goes further, crediting both with helping the king against the Camisards. (“Basnage” is <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Basnage">Jacques Basnage</a>, the Huguenot pastor at The Hague: our identification.)</p>
