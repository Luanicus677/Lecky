abstract: |
  <p>Lecky quotes Rousseau’s argument that civil and theological intolerance cannot be separated, and his conclusion that those who say “outside the Church, no salvation” must be driven from the state unless the state is the Church. The quotation is accurate to the sense. Lecky leaves out the middle of the passage (on priests becoming the true masters), and his French has a few differences: older spelling and, in two places, a grammatical slip that may be his edition’s (“tous les religions … leur dogmes”).</p>

<section id="deux-intolerances" class="passage">
<h2>The two intolerances (pp. 331–333)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: pp. 331–333, <a href="https://archive.org/details/ducontratsocialt00rous/page/n334/mode/2up">scan</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: Lecky’s words. The elided text is shown in grey.</p>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p><span class="cited-passage">Ceux qui distinguent l’intolérance civile et l’intolérance théologique se trompent, à mon avis. Ces deux intolérances sont inséparables. Il est impossible de vivre en paix avec des gens qu’on croit damnés ; les aimer serait haïr Dieu qui les punit ; il faut absolument qu’on les ramène ou qu’on les tourmente.</span> <span style="color:var(--fg-faint)">Partout où l’intolérance théologique est admise, il est impossible qu’elle n’ait pas quelque effet civil ; et sitôt qu’elle en a, le souverain n’est plus souverain, même au temporel : dès lors les prêtres sont les vrais maîtres ; les rois ne sont que leurs officiers.</span></p>
<p>Maintenant qu’il n’y a plus et qu’il ne peut plus y avoir de religion nationale exclusive, <span class="cited-passage">on doit tolérer toutes celles qui tolèrent les autres, autant que leurs dogmes n’ont rien de contraire aux devoirs du citoyen. Mais quiconque ose dire : <em>hors de l’Église point de salut</em>, doit être chassé de l’État, à moins que l’État ne soit l’Église, et que le prince ne soit le pontife.</span> Un tel dogme n’est bon que dans un gouvernement théocratique ; dans tout autre il est pernicieux. …</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p><span class="cited-passage">Those who distinguish civil intolerance from theological intolerance are mistaken, in my opinion. These two intolerances are inseparable. It is impossible to live in peace with people whom one believes damned; to love them would be to hate God, who punishes them; it is absolutely necessary that they be brought back or tormented.</span> <span style="color:var(--fg-faint)">Wherever theological intolerance is admitted, it is impossible that it should not have some civil effect; and as soon as it has, the sovereign is no longer sovereign, even in temporal matters: from then on the priests are the true masters; kings are only their officers.</span></p>
<p>Now that there is no longer, and can no longer be, an exclusive national religion, <span class="cited-passage">all those that tolerate others must be tolerated, so far as their dogmas contain nothing contrary to the duties of the citizen. But whoever dares to say: <em>outside the Church no salvation</em> ought to be driven from the State, unless the State is the Church and the prince the pontiff.</span> Such a dogma is good only in a theocratic government; in any other it is pernicious. …</p>
</div>

<p>The differences are probably the edition he used, not slips of Lecky’s; I have not seen it. The omission matters a little: Rousseau’s “Maintenant qu’il n’y a plus … de religion nationale exclusive” is what makes his toleration a modern rule, which Lecky’s run-on version blurs. Lecky’s rendering of the whole as a defence of toleration with one exception is nonetheless fair.</p>
