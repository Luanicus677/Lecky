abstract: |
  <p>Lecky notes that Milton, the great advocate of toleration, “rigidly excludes” Catholics from it, and that he “expressly refuses” to rest the exclusion on their politics. The tract bears him out. Milton leaves the political question to others (“whether therefore it be fit or reasonable, to tolerate men thus principl’d in Religion towards the State, I submit it to the consideration of all Magistrates”) and rests his own answer on religion: the Mass is idolatry, and idolatry “can be tolerated neither way: not publicly, without grievous and unsufferable scandal giv’n to all conscientious Beholders; not privately, without great offence to God”. His proof text is Ezekiel 8, where the prophet digs through a wall and finds the elders of Israel at their secret idolatries.</p>
  <p>On the next page Milton draws back from the obvious consequence. Should Catholics then be punished “by corporal punishment, or fines in their Estates, upon account of their Religion? I suppose it stands not with the Clemency of the Gospel, more then what appertains to the security of the State.” What must be removed is “their Idolatry, and all the furniture thereof”: their images and their Mass.</p>

<blockquote>
<p>… Whether therefore it be fit or reasonable, to tolerate men thus principl’d in Religion towards the State, I submit it to the consideration of all Magistrates, who are best able to provide for their own and the publick safety. <span class="cited-passage">As for tolerating the exercise of their Religion, supposing their State activities not to be dangerous, I answer, that Toleration is either public or private; and the exercise of their Religion, as far as it is Idolatrous, can be tolerated neither way: [p. 11] not publicly, without grievous and unsufferable scandal giv’n to all conscientious Beholders; not privately, without great offence to God, declar’d against all kind of Idolatry, though secret.</span> <i>Ezekiel</i> 8.7,8. <i>And he brought me to the door of the Court, and when I looked, behold a hole in the wall. Then said he unto me, Son of Man, digg now in the wall; and when I had digged, behold a Door, and he said unto me, go in, and behold the wicked Abominations that they do here.</i> And verse 12. <i>Then said he unto me, Son of Man, hast thou seen what the Antients of the house of Israel do in the dark?</i> &amp;c. <span class="cited-passage">And it appears by the whole Chapter, that God was no less offended with these secret Idolatries, then with those in public; and no less provok’t, then to bring on and hasten his Judgements on the whole Land for these also.</span></p>
<p>Having shown thus, that Popery, as being Idolatrous, is not to be tolerated either in Public or in Private; it must be now thought how to remove it and hinder the growth thereof, I mean in our Natives, and not Foreigners, Privileg’d by the Law of Nations. Are we to punish them by corporal punishment, or fines in their Estates, upon account of their Religion? I suppose it stands not with the Clemency of the Gospel, more then what appertains to the security of the State: But first we must remove their Idolatry, and all the furniture thereof …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky’s quotation (vol. II, ch. IV, note 92) is accurate in modern spelling, with “those secret idolatries” for Milton’s “these”. His comment, that Milton’s exclusion “rests upon a distinct religious principle” and not on the political danger, is exactly what the preceding sentence says.</p>
