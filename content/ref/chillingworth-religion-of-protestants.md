abstract: |
  <p>Lecky calls this “the noble language of Chillingworth”: his answer to the Catholic charge that Protestants who err in good faith are damned. If Protestants do their best to free themselves from error and yet fail “through human Frailty; so well am I perswaded of the Goodness of God, that if in me alone should meet a Confluence of all such Errors of all the Protestants in the World, that were thus qualified, I should not be so much afraid of them all, as I should be to ask pardon for them.”</p>
  <p>The next sentence explains the paradox: to ask pardon for “simple and purely involuntary Errors” would imply that God is angry with us for them, and that “were to impute to him the strange Tyranny of requiring Brick, when he gives no Straw”.</p>

<h2>“As I should be to ask pardon for them” (p. 44)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: <em>Works</em>, London 1742, “The Answer to the Preface”, p. 44, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_the-works-of-william-chi_chillingworth-william_1742/page/n51/mode/2up">scan n51</a>. Long s modernised; Chillingworth’s spelling kept. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: Lecky’s quotation.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/chillingworth-1742-p44.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/chillingworth-1742-p44.jpg" alt="Printed page 44, headed ‘The Answer to the Preface’: “… if they do their best endeavour to free themselves from all Errors, and yet fail of it through human Frailty; so well am I perswaded of the Goodness of God, that if in me alone should meet a Confluence of all such Errors of all the Protestants in the World, that were thus qualified, I should not be so much afraid of them all, as I should be to ask pardon for them.”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Chillingworth, <em>Works</em>, London 1742, p. 44. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (ECCO) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>[p. 43] … When therefore you can shew, that in this and all other their Controversies, God hath interposed his Testimony on one side or other; so that [p. 44] either they do see it, and will not; or, were it not for their own voluntary and avoidable Fault, might and should see it, and do not; let all such Errors be as damnable as you please to make them. In the mean while, if they suffer themselves neither to be betrayed into their Errors, nor kept in them by any Sin of their Will; <span class="cited-passage">if they do their best endeavour to free themselves from all Errors, and yet fail of it through human Frailty; so well am I perswaded of the Goodness of God, that if in me alone should meet a Confluence of all such Errors of all the Protestants in the World, that were thus qualified, I should not be so much afraid of them all, as I should be to ask pardon for them.</span> For, whereas that which you affright us with, of calling God’s Veracity in question, is but a panick Fear, a Fault that no Man thus qualified is or can be guilty of; to ask pardon of simple and purely involuntary Errors is tacitly to imply, that God is angry with us for them, and that were to impute to him the strange Tyranny of <i>requiring Brick, when he gives no Straw; of expecting to gather, where he strewed not; to reap, where he sowed not</i> …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. IV, text at note 84) quotes the highlighted words accurately in modern spelling, with two small changes: “If men do their best endeavours” for “if they do their best endeavour”, and “so well I am persuaded” for “so well am I perswaded”. His note 93 dates the book 1637; the first edition’s catalogue date is 1638 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1475-1640_the-religion-of-protesta_chillingworth-william_1638_1">EEBO scan</a>, title page not viewed).</p>
