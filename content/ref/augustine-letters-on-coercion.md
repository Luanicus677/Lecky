abstract: |
  <p>Lecky calls Augustine “the framer and the representative of the theology of intolerance”, a man who first condemned persecution and then “recanted his condemnation”. Augustine tells the story himself, to a Donatist bishop: “My opinion at first was that no one should be coerced into the unity of Christ; that we must act by word, fight by argument, conquer by reason, lest we should have feigned Catholics whom we had known as open heretics. But this opinion of mine was overcome, not by the words of those who contradicted it, but by the examples of those who demonstrated.” The first example was his own town, Hippo, once wholly Donatist and now Catholic “by fear of the imperial laws”.</p>
  <p>To Boniface he gave the theory: a king serves God as a man by living faithfully, but as a king by making laws “with fitting vigour” against what God forbids, as Hezekiah, Josiah, the king of Nineveh, Darius and Nebuchadnezzar did. Lecky’s list in his text is the same, without Darius.</p>

<section id="ep93-recantation" class="passage">
<h2>Letter 93 § 17: “Nam mea primitus sententia …” (PL 33, cols. 329–330)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin: Migne PL 33, cols. 329–330, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_patrologiae-cursus-completus-_1845_33/page/n164/mode/2up">scan n164</a> (the end of one column, the top of the next). <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the change of mind Lecky describes.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/augustine-ep93-pl33-col329.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/augustine-ep93-pl33-col329.jpg" alt="Printed Latin: “17. His ergo exemplis a collegis meis mihi propositis cessi. Nam mea primitus sententia non erat, nisi neminem ad unitatem Christi esse cogendum; verbo esse agendum, disputatione pugnandum, ratione vincendum …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Augustine, Letter 93, Migne PL 33, cols. 329–330. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>17. His ergo exemplis a collegis meis mihi propositis cessi. <span class="cited-passage">Nam mea primitus sententia non erat, nisi neminem ad unitatem Christi esse cogendum; verbo esse agendum, disputatione pugnandum, ratione vincendum, ne fictos catholicos haberemus, quos apertos hæreticos noveramus. Sed hæc opinio mea, non contradicentium verbis, sed demonstrantium superabatur exemplis.</span> Nam primo mihi opponebatur civitas mea, quæ cum tota esset in parte Donati, ad unitatem catholicam timore legum imperialium conversa est; …</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>17. To these examples, then, put before me by my colleagues, I gave way. <span class="cited-passage">For my opinion at first was that no one should be coerced into the unity of Christ; that we must act by word, fight by argument, conquer by reason, lest we should have feigned Catholics whom we had known as open heretics. But this opinion of mine was overcome, not by the words of those who contradicted it, but by the examples of those who demonstrated.</span> For first my own city was set against me, which, though it was wholly on the side of Donatus, was converted to Catholic unity by fear of the imperial laws; …</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. IV, text at note 27): “For a time he shrank from, and even condemned, persecution; but he soon perceived in it the necessary consequence of his principles. He recanted his condemnation.” Augustine’s own account is less about principles than about results: what changed his mind was the sight of Donatist towns turned Catholic by the laws. The same letter (§ 5, OCR only) already applies the parable’s “cogite intrare”, “compel them to come in” (Luke 14:23), to the Donatists.</p>
</section>

<section id="ep185-kings" class="passage">
<h2>Letter 185 § 19: kings serve God by punishing (PL 33, col. 801)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin: Migne PL 33, col. 801, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_patrologiae-cursus-completus-_1845_33/page/n400/mode/2up">scan n400</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the precedents Lecky lists.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/augustine-ep185-pl33-col801.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/augustine-ep185-pl33-col801.jpg" alt="Printed Latin: “Quomodo ergo reges Domino serviunt in timore, nisi ea quæ contra jussa Domini fiunt, religiosa severitate prohibendo atque plectendo? … Sicut servivit Ezechias … sicut servivit Josias … sicut servivit rex Ninivitarum … sicut servivit Darius … sicut servivit Nabuchodonosor …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Augustine, Letter 185 (to Boniface), Migne PL 33, col. 801. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>… Quomodo ergo reges Domino serviunt in timore, nisi ea quæ contra jussa Domini fiunt, religiosa severitate prohibendo atque plectendo? Aliter enim servit, quia homo est; aliter, quia etiam rex est: quia homo est, ei servit vivendo fideliter; quia vero etiam rex est, servit leges justa præcipientes et contraria prohibentes convenienti vigore sanciendo. <span class="cited-passage">Sicut servivit Ezechias</span>, lucos et templa idolorum, et illa excelsa quæ contra præcepta Dei fuerant constructa destruendo (IV <i>Reg.</i> XVIII, 4): <span class="cited-passage">sicut servivit Josias</span>, talia et ipse faciendo (<i>Ibid.</i>, XXIII, 4, 5): <span class="cited-passage">sicut servivit rex Ninivitarum</span>, universam civitatem ad placandum Dominum compellendo (<i>Jonæ</i> III, 6-9): sicut servivit Darius, idolum frangendum in potestatem Danieli dando, et inimicos ejus leonibus ingerendo (<i>Dan.</i> XIV, 21, 41): <span class="cited-passage">sicut servivit Nabuchodonosor</span>, de quo jam diximus, omnes in regno suo positos a blasphemando Deo lege terribili prohibendo (<i>Id.</i> III, 96). In hoc ergo serviunt Domino reges, in quantum sunt reges, cum ea faciunt ad serviendum illi, quæ non possunt facere nisi reges.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>… How then do kings serve the Lord in fear, if not by forbidding and punishing with religious severity what is done against the Lord’s commands? For a king serves him in one way because he is a man, in another because he is also a king: because he is a man, he serves him by living faithfully; because he is also a king, he serves him by enacting, with fitting vigour, laws that command what is just and forbid the contrary. <span class="cited-passage">So Hezekiah served him</span>, by destroying the groves and temples of the idols and the high places built against God’s commands (2 Kings 18:4); <span class="cited-passage">so Josiah served him</span>, by doing the same (2 Kings 23:4–5); <span class="cited-passage">so the king of Nineveh served him</span>, by compelling the whole city to appease the Lord (Jonah 3:6–9); so Darius served him, by giving the idol into Daniel’s power to be broken, and throwing his enemies to the lions (Daniel 14 [Bel and the Dragon]); <span class="cited-passage">so Nebuchadnezzar served him</span>, of whom we have already spoken, by forbidding all in his kingdom, with a terrible law, to blaspheme God (Daniel 3:96 [3:29]). In this, then, kings serve the Lord as kings: when they do in his service what only kings can do.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. IV, text at note 28): “Did not Hezekiah, and Josiah, and the king of Nineveh, and Nebuchadnezzar after his conversion, destroy by force idolatry within their dominions, and were they not expressly commended for their piety?” His list is Augustine’s, without Darius; Elijah, whom Lecky names in the sentence before, is not in this paragraph.</p>
</section>

<section id="ep105-nebuchadnezzar" class="passage">
<h2>Nebuchadnezzar’s edict against blasphemers (Ep. 105 § 7, cols. 398–399)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin: Migne, PL 33, Epistola CV (to the Donatists), § 7, cols. 398–399, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_patrologiae-cursus-completus-_1845_33/page/n198/mode/2up">scan n198</a>–n199. The scan is heavily inked; read on the images (the OCR of this page is unusable). <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the passage behind Lecky’s note 29.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/augustine-ep105-pl33-cols398-399.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/augustine-ep105-pl33-cols398-399.jpg" alt="Migne columns: “Quando autem imperatores veritatem tenent, pro ipsa veritate contra errorem jubent; quod quisquis contempserit, ipse sibi judicium acquirit … Sicut ipse Nabuchodonosor postea miraculo salutis trium puerorum commotus atque mutatus, pro veritate contra errorem edictum proposuit, ut quicumque blasphemarent Deum Sidrach, Misach et Abdenago, in interitum irent, et domus eorum in dispersionem (Dan. III, 96): et non vultis ut tale aliquid contra vos jubeant imperatores Christiani …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Augustine, Ep. 105 § 7, in Migne, PL 33, cols. 398–399. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>Quando autem imperatores veritatem tenent, pro ipsa veritate contra errorem jubent; quod quisquis contempserit, ipse sibi judicium acquirit. Nam et inter homines pœnas luit, et apud Deum frontem non habebit, qui hoc facere noluit, quod ei per cor regis ipsa veritas jussit. <span class="cited-passage">Sicut ipse Nabuchodonosor postea miraculo salutis trium puerorum commotus atque mutatus, pro veritate contra errorem edictum proposuit, ut quicumque blasphemarent Deum Sidrach, Misach et Abdenago, in interitum irent, et domus eorum in dispersionem (Dan. III, 96): et non vultis ut tale aliquid contra vos jubeant imperatores Christiani, cum sciant a vobis, in eis quos rebaptizatis, Christum exsufflari?</span></p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p>But when emperors hold the truth, they command on behalf of that truth against error; and whoever despises their command brings judgement on himself. For he pays the penalty among men, and will have no face before God, who would not do what truth itself commanded him through the heart of the king. <span class="cited-passage">So Nebuchadnezzar himself, moved and changed by the miracle of the three children’s deliverance, issued an edict for the truth against error, that whoever blasphemed the God of Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego should be destroyed, and their houses scattered (Dan. 3:96 [3:29]); and do you not wish Christian emperors to command something of the kind against you, when they know that in those whom you rebaptize Christ is exorcised?</span></p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation: this edition’s draft. “Exsufflari”: blown out, as in the rite of exorcism the Donatists performed on Catholics they rebaptized.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. IV, note 29): Augustine “assimilates heresy to blasphemy, and says that blasphemy is justly punished by death.” The letter does the first: rebaptism treats Christ as an evil spirit to be driven out, and so is blasphemy. On the second it goes no further than holding up Nebuchadnezzar’s decree of destruction as an example of an edict “for the truth against error”, and asking why the Donatists object to Christian emperors decreeing “something of the kind”. It does not itself ask for death.</p>
</section>
