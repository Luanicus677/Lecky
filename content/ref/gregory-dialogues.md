abstract: |
  <p>Lecky’s note: a nun, “when walking in a garden, began to eat without making the sign of the cross. She had bitter cause to repent of her indecent haste, for she immediately swallowed a devil in a lettuce.” Gregory’s story is better than Lecky’s summary, because the devil speaks. When the holy abbot Equitius came to the garden, the devil, speaking through the nun’s tongue, tried to excuse himself: “What have I done? What have I done? I was sitting there upon the lettice, and she came and did eat me.” Equitius ordered him out, and he went. Lecky calls the whole passage “extremely curious”; it is also, in the old English version, very funny.</p>

<h2>The nun and the lettuce (Book I, the chapter on Equitius)</h2>

<blockquote>
<p>Upon a certain day, <span class="cited-passage">one of the Nuns of the same monastery, going into the garden, saw a lettice that liked her, and forgetting to bless it before with the sign of the cross, greedily did she eat it: whereupon she was suddenly possessed with the devil</span>, fell down to the ground, and was pitifully tormented. Word in all haste was carried to Equitius, desiring him quickly to visit the afflicted woman, and to help her with his prayers: who so soon as he came into the garden, the devil that was entered began by her tongue, as it were, to excuse himself, saying: “What have I done? What have I done? I was sitting there upon the lettice, and she came and did eat me.” But the man of God in great zeal commanded him to depart, and not to tarry any longer in the servant of almighty God, who straightways went out, not presuming any more to touch her.</p>
</blockquote>

<p>The paragraph just before this one ends with a sorcerer who, “through the zeal of good people, for his wickedness was burnt” in Rome. The same Equitius turns up in the <em>Malleus Maleficarum</em> as an example of a holy man freed from lust (<a href="sprenger-malleus-maleficarum.html#serenus-150">Malleus page</a>).</p>
