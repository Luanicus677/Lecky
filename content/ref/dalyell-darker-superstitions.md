abstract: |
  <p>Lecky’s picture of the Scottish ministers hunting witches rests on a handful of pages of Dalyell. They bear him out on the Aberdeen inquisition of 1603 and on “waking” (keeping a prisoner from sleep, up to nine nights), but in three places Lecky states as fact what Dalyell reports with a qualification: that boxes “were placed in the churches” to receive accusations (Dalyell says “it is said”, and cites Bodin on Milan), that a minister from the pulpit “denounced her by name” (I did not find that on the page cited), and that pricking was a “regular profession” (not on p. 645, but earlier in the chapter).</p><p>Dalyell also says that waking was not peculiar to Scotland: it was used in England, “common on the continent”, and in the Turkish dominions. See <a href="#comparison">the comparison</a>.</p>

<section id="boxes-623" class="passage">
<h2>The Box in the Church (p. 623) and the Aberdeen Inquisition (p. 624)</h2>

<p class="passage-source">English: Dalyell, 1834, pp. 623–624 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/darkersuperstit00dalygoog/page/n639/mode/2up">p. 623</a>, <a href="https://archive.org/details/darkersuperstit00dalygoog/page/n640/mode/2up">p. 624</a>). As printed (the scan’s long text has been read from the image). <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the sentences behind Lecky’s notes 146–148.</p>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="en">
<p>[p. 623] … Clandestine information, <span class="cited-passage">it is said, was sought, “by placing an empty box in church, to receive a billet with the sorcerer’s name, and the date and description of his deeds.”<sup>‖</sup></span> Too many readily assented, and a secret enquiry on the truth or probability of the accusation, before some of the ecclesiastical judicatories, such as the kirk session, followed.</p>
<p><sup>‖</sup> <em>Bodinus</em>, lib. iv. c. 1. p. 321. illustrates the custom of Milan from Scotish practice.</p>
<p>[p. 624] But besides the operation of private calumnies, a general discovery of offenders was occasionally contemplated by the zealous, and conducted on a scale of unexampled magnitude, like huntsmen beating up extensive coverts for dislodging beasts of prey. A whole presbytery was up in arms. <span class="cited-passage">Thus, “at the College of auld Abirdene, the sext of Januar 1603,” it was ordained that every minister should make “ane subtill and privie inquisitioun, heirin: videlicet, the minister with tua of his elderis, that feris God and ar maist zealous for his glorie, at ilk particular kirk <em>respectivè</em>, tak the aithis of the inhabitants within thair charge, quhat thay knaw of wiches and consultaris with thame: and wreitt thair depositiounis, and returne the same to the presbyterie, with the names of sic as ar metest to be assysouris to thame, that the same may be sent to the Marques with all hastie expeditioun, conforme to the desyre of his Lordschip’s letter.”</span> Meantime the conduct of individuals underwent a secret scrutiny …</p>
</div>
</div>

<details class="collapse">
<summary>What the box note says</summary>
<p>Dalyell’s footnote to the “box” sentence cites Bodin’s <em>Démonomanie</em> (book IV, ch. 1, p. 321), and says Bodin “illustrates the custom of Milan from Scotish practice”. That is hard to read: on the ordinary sense, Bodin uses the Scottish practice to illustrate Milan. I have not looked at Bodin’s p. 321, so I do not know which direction the evidence runs. What matters here is that Dalyell himself reports the Scottish box as something “it is said”, not as established.</p>
</details>

<section id="waking-645" class="passage">
<h2>“Watching” (pp. 644–645)</h2>

<p class="passage-source">English: Dalyell, 1834, pp. 644–645 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/darkersuperstit00dalygoog/page/n660/mode/2up">p. 644</a>, <a href="https://archive.org/details/darkersuperstit00dalygoog/page/n661/mode/2up">p. 645</a>), under the heading “Watching”. As printed. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the sentences behind Lecky’s notes 150–153.</p>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="en">
<p>[p. 644] <em>Watching.</em>—Though privation of sleep be described as “the choicest means they use in Scotland for discovery of witches,” it was not restricted to them only; for, after other expedients for detecting a conspiracy, had failed to obtain confession, the commander of the forces was enjoined to employ the most trusty officers or soldiers to watch “Master William Spence be turnes, and not to suffer him to sleep by night or by day, and, for that end, to use all effectual meanes for keeping him still awake.” Another was “withhaldin from sleep to the great perturbation of his brayne.” By means of tortures, pricking, watching,</p>
<p>[p. 645] and keeping several women from sleep, James Gillespie, minister of Rind, and some coadjutors, were charged with having obtained false confessions, whereon the innocent had suffered death. Several also, under a guard of drunken fellows at Pittenweem, were kept days and nights awake, which “cruel usage made some of them to be so wise as to acknowledge every question that was asked of them, whereby they found the minister and baillies well pleased, and themselves better treated.” <span class="cited-passage">One was kept five days and nights awake by continual pricking, to the great effusion of her blood: and this kind of torture is alleged to have been protracted even to nine nights.</span></p>
<p>Suspected sorcerers were treated thus in England. Conjoined with other torture it was common on the continent, and esteemed singly as the most effectual inducement to confess. Keeping the suspected awake for the same purpose, is still practised in the Turkish dominions: and it may be traced to ancient date, in Proto Sebastus having been kept awake three days and nights, by his guards pricking him.</p>
</div>
</div>
