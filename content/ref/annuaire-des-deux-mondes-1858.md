abstract: |
  <p>Lecky’s evidence that Protestant intolerance was not dead in his own day: “Only a few years ago, six ladies were exiled from Sweden because they had embraced the Roman Catholic faith.” The <i>Annuaire</i> tells the story. The Swedish government had put two bills before the four estates of the Diet, to abolish exile for apostasy and to establish “a certain tolerance”. The burghers and peasants accepted them; the clergy and the nobility refused. “Bien plus”: a prosecution of six women who had become Catholics, pending for five years, was taken up again immediately after the refusal, and under “the barbarous code that it was wished to preserve” they were exiled. Five of them were mothers of families; two came to ask asylum in France.</p>
  <p>Lecky’s note follows the page closely: the bill “adopted by the Houses of the Middle Class and of the Peasants” and “rejected by those of the Nobles and of the Clergy”. His last sentence, on a “very slight” modification in 1860, is his own addition and is not on this page.</p>

<div class="rendering">
<p>… [we have] given an account of the two bills laid before the four estates by the Swedish government for the abolition of exile and the establishment of a certain tolerance; <span class="cited-passage">but the privileged orders, those of the clergy and the nobility, refused, against the opinion of the burghers and the peasants, to sanction this change in the religious laws. Worse still, a prosecution of six unhappy women who had become Catholics, a prosecution pending for five years, was taken up again immediately after this refusal by the representatives of the nation. Under the terms of the barbarous code that it was wished to preserve, these women, five of whom are mothers of families, have been exiled</span>; two of them have come to ask asylum of France.</p>

<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. IV, text at note 98): “Only a few years ago, six ladies were exiled from Sweden because they had embraced the Roman Catholic faith”; note 98: the bill “adopted by the Houses of the Middle Class and of the Peasants, was rejected by those of the Nobles and of the Clergy”. Lecky’s “ladies” is gentler than the <i>Annuaire</i>’s “malheureuses femmes”, and he leaves out the detail that most of them were mothers. Further down the same page the <i>Annuaire</i> lists what the Diet did pass: the abolition of the edict against conventicles (<i>konventikel-placat</i>) of 12 January 1726, so that Lutherans might meet to pray together without a pastor present (read on the page image at preview size only).</p>
