<section id="water-and-spirit" class="passage">
<h2>“The water and the Spirit in this place signify the same thing”</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: pp. 328–329, <a href="https://archive.org/details/discourseofliber1836tayl/page/n369/mode/2up">scan</a> and <a href="https://archive.org/details/discourseofliber1836tayl/page/n370/mode/2up">scan</a> (chapter “Case of the Anabaptists”).</p>
<figure class="title-page"><a href="../assets/img/ref/taylor-1836-title.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/taylor-1836-title.jpg" alt="Title page: “A Discourse of the Liberty of Prophesying: shewing the unreasonableness of prescribing to other men’s faith; and the iniquity of persecuting differing opinions. By Jeremy Taylor … With an introductory essay by the Rev. R. Cattermole, B.D. London: … Joseph Rickerby … 1836”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Title page, London 1836 (the frontispiece portrait shows through the paper). <span class="credit"><a href="https://archive.org/details/discourseofliber1836tayl/page/n8/mode/2up">Scan: Internet Archive</a> · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>… However, there is no danger that infants should perish for want of this external ministry, much less for prevaricating Christ’s precept of ‘Except a man be born again,’ &amp;c. For, first, <span class="cited-passage">the water and the Spirit in this place signify the same thing; and by water is meant the effect of the Spirit, cleansing and purifying the soul, as appears in its parallel place of Christ baptizing with the Spirit and with fire.</span> For although this was literally fulfilled in Pentecost, yet morally there is more in it, for it is the sign of the effect of the Holy Ghost, and his productions upon the soul … and you may as well conclude that infants must also pass through the fire as through the water. …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Collation with Lecky: Lecky’s “baptising” for Taylor’s “baptizing” and lower-case “spirit”; otherwise identical.</p>
</section>

<section id="elias-fire" class="passage">
<h2>James and John, Elias and the Mosaic law (p. 368)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: p. 368, <a href="https://archive.org/details/discourseofliber1836tayl/page/n409/mode/2up">scan</a> (the end of the chapter on Judaic law and persecution; image = page + 41).</p>
<blockquote>
<p>… For in the case of the apostles and the men of Samaria, <span class="cited-passage">when James and John would have called for fire to destroy them, even as Elias did under Moses’s law, Christ distinguished the spirit of Elias from his own spirit</span>, and taught them a lesson of greater sweetness, and consigned this truth to all ages of the church, that such severity is not consistent with the meekness which Christ by his example and sermons hath made a precept evangelical; at most it was but a judicial law, and no more of argument to make it necessary to us than the Mosaical precepts of putting adulterers to death, and trying the accused persons by the waters of jealousy. …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. IV, note 7): “Taylor, in noticing this argument, finely says that Christ, by refusing to permit his apostles to call down fire like Elias on the misbeliever, clearly indicated his separation from the intolerance of Judaism. (<em>Liberty of Prophesying</em>, sec. 22.)” A fair paraphrase; “sec. 22” is the work’s section number, not read here; the page is in the section on the Old Testament as a ground for persecution.</p>
</section>

<details class="collapse editor-notes"><summary>Editor’s notes on this page</summary>
<p>Lecky’s quotation is accurate (“The water and the spirit in this place signify the same thing; and by water is meant …”). Taylor’s sentence runs across the page break: “For, first, the water and the Spirit in this place signify the same thing; and by water is meant the effect of the Spirit, cleansing and purifying the soul, as appears in its parallel place of Christ baptizing with the Spirit and with fire.” Taylor’s purpose is a defence of the Anabaptists’ objection to infant baptism: he goes on to say that if the “exclusive negative” is read by analogy with Peter’s “not the washing of the flesh”, it cannot prove the necessity of infant baptism.</p>
</details>

<section id="church-of-rome" class="passage">
<h2>How far the Church of Rome is tolerable (Section XX, pp. 358–360)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: 1836 printing, Section XX, “How far the Religion of the Church of Rome is tolerable”, p. 359, <a href="https://archive.org/details/discourseofliber1836tayl/page/n400/mode/2up">scan n400</a> (pp. 358 and 360 from the OCR). Lecky cites “Sec. 22”; in this edition Section XXII is on communicating with churches of other persuasions, so his section number does not fit. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the exceptions Lecky lists.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/taylor-liberty-1836-p359.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/taylor-liberty-1836-p359.jpg" alt="Printed page 359: “Fourthly: but if we consider their doctrines in relation to government and public societies of men … Such doctrines as these—the pope may dispense with all oaths taken to God or man; he may absolve subjects from their allegiance to their natural prince; faith is not to be kept with heretics; heretical princes may be slain by their subjects …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Taylor, <em>The Liberty of Prophesying</em>, 1836 printing, p. 359. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>[p. 358] … so if these men live well though they teach ill, they are to be imitated, not heard: their doctrines by all means, Christian and human, are to [p. 359] be discountenanced, but <span class="cited-passage">their persons tolerated</span> so far (<i>eatenus</i>); their profession and decrees to be rejected and condemned, but the persons to be permitted, because by their good lives they confute their doctrines …</p>
<p>Fourthly: but if we consider their doctrines in relation to government and public societies of men, then, if they prove faulty, they are so much the more intolerable by how much the consequents are of greater danger and malice. Such doctrines as these—<span class="cited-passage">the pope may dispense with all oaths taken to God or man; he may absolve subjects from their allegiance to their natural prince; faith is not to be kept with heretics; heretical princes may be slain by their subjects</span>—these propositions are so depressed, and do so immediately communicate with matter and the interests of men, that they are of the same consideration with matters of fact, and are to be handled accordingly. … [p. 360] Now these opinions are a direct overthrow to all human society and mutual commerce, a destruction of government, and of the laws, and duty, and subordination which we owe to princes; and therefore those men of the church of Rome that do hold them, and preach them, cannot pretend to the excuses of innocent opinions and hearty persuasion …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. IV, note 94): Taylor “desires that they should be absolutely tolerated, unless, indeed, they openly preach such doctrines as the non-observance of faith with heretics, or that a pope can absolve subjects from the oath of allegiance, or that an heretical prince may be slain by his people.” A fair summary, though “absolutely” is stronger than Taylor’s guarded “tolerated so far” (<i>eatenus</i>): he condemns their doctrines “by all means, Christian and human”, and tolerates the persons.</p>
</section>
