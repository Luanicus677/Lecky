abstract: |
  <p>Lecky’s note 101 calls Hutchinson’s chronological table “the most complete authority” on witch-trials, says Hutchinson “restricted himself for the most part to cases of which he had learnt precise particulars, and he carefully gives his authorities”, and gives a count of about 140 English executions. Note 111 cites Hutchinson p. 68 for the claim that more witches perished in England during the Commonwealth than in all the period before and after.</p><p>This one page bears out the style of the table: dated entries with references, and a candid “I have been told” for the Bury figures (“near Forty … and as many more in the County as made up Threescore”). It lists three Commonwealth-era events totalling about 16 + 15 + 60 (Yarmouth, Chelmsford and Manningtree, Bury and Suffolk), which is consistent in size with Lecky’s statement but does not prove it.</p>

<section id="table-37" class="passage">
<h2>The Table of Facts, 1634–1646 (p. 37)</h2>

<p class="passage-source">English: Hutchinson, 1718, p. 37 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/b30500370_0001/page/n64/mode/2up">scan</a>). Long s printed as s; the year column is given in brackets. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the entries for the 1640s.</p>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="en">
<p>… stripp’d naked, search’d for insensible Marks, and put to cruel Pains, while they try’d with an artificial Knife, which Parts of him were sensible, and which not. … After that he was tortur’d till he swooned upon the Rack; and maintaining his Innocence, he was at last inhumanly burnt, without being suffered either to unbosom his Mind to his Confessor, or speak to the People. <em>See the Ways of their Wickedness fully proved in</em> Histoire de Diables de Loudun.</p>
<p>About that Time, Seventeen <em>Pendle-Forest</em> Witches were condemned in <em>Lancashire</em>, by the Contrivance of a Boy and his Father. [1634] See their Vindication in the latter Part of this Book, Ch. 15. Imposture 5.</p>
<p>Dr. <em>Lamb</em> was killed by the Mob, being thought a Conjurer. [1640]</p>
<p>Mother <em>Jackson</em> condemned in <em>London</em>. [1642]</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">Sixteen executed at <em>Yarmouth</em>, discovered by <em>Hopkins</em> the famous Witch-finder of that Time.</span> [1644]</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">Fifteen condemn’d at <em>Chelmsford</em> in <em>Essex</em>, and hang’d, some at <em>Chelmsford</em>, and some at <em>Manningtree</em>. Another died in Goal. Another died as going to Execution.</span> They were condemned at a Sessions by the Earl of <em>Warwick</em>, and some Justices of the Peace. [1645]</p>
<p>One hang’d at <em>Cambridge</em>. She kept a tame Frog, and it was sworn to be her Imp. [1645]</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">Many hang’d at <em>Bury St. Edmunds</em> in <em>Suffolk</em>; I have been told near Forty at the several Times of Executions, and as many more in the County as made up Threescore.</span> [1645–46] See the Notes upon <em>Hudibras</em>, 2d Part, 3d Canto. Mr. <em>Ady</em>, in his Book of Witchcraft, speaks of more.</p>
</div>
</div>

<details class="collapse">
<summary>Notes</summary>
<p>The first paragraph is the end of an entry on a Continental case (the French <em>Loudun</em> reference shows it is the burning of Urbain Grandier, 1634); I did not read the rest of it. The “Pendle-Forest” witches of 1634 were in fact the later, discredited Lancashire accusations (Hutchinson refers to them as a contrivance). The year column is Hutchinson’s, and the “[1645–46]” for Bury is my reading of the two year-marks beside that entry.</p>
</details>

<section id="last-three-1682" class="passage">
<h2>The last three hanged in England (1720 ed., pp. 56–57)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: 1720 edition, p. 56 (bottom) to p. 57 (top), <a href="https://archive.org/details/b30512967_0001/page/n87/mode/2up">scan</a> and <a href="https://archive.org/details/b30512967_0001/page/n88/mode/2up">scan</a>. The long s is printed as s.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>1682. <span class="cited-passage">Susan Edwards, Mary Trembles and Temperance Lloyd, hanged at Exeter, confess’d themselves Witches, but died with good Prayers in their Mouths. I suppose these are the last three that have been hanged in England.</span></p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (text above note 134): “Three witches had been executed in 1682; and others, it is said, endured the same fate in 1712; but these were the last who perished judicially in England.” Hutchinson’s sentence supports “three witches … 1682”, but he hedges (“I suppose”), and nothing on these two pages says anyone was executed in 1712: the 1712 case in the book is that of Jane Wenham (chapter X, p. 153 ff., which I did not read for this page). Lecky’s “others, it is said, endured the same fate in 1712” is not Hutchinson’s statement: it comes from Buckle’s footnote (<a href="buckle-history-of-civilization.html#restoration-revolution">vol. 1, p. 333</a>), which cites Hutchinson’s pp. 56–57 for 1682 and, on Dr. Parr’s authority, five witches hanged at Northampton in 1712. It is left as printed.</p>
</section>

<section id="liberavimus-167" class="passage">
<h2>“Liberavimus animas nostras” (1720 ed., p. 167)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: 1720 edition, p. 167, in chapter X, “The Case of Jane Wenham in Hertfordshire” (pp. 163 ff.), <a href="https://archive.org/details/b30512967_0001/page/n198/mode/2up">scan</a>. Hutchinson is addressing the clergymen who had taken part in the prosecution.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>4. Durst you have us’d her in this manner, if she had been rich; and doth not her Poverty increase, rather than lessen your Guilt in what you did?</p>
<p>And therefore, <span class="cited-passage">instead of closing your Book with a <em>Liberavimus Animas Nostras</em>, and reflecting upon the Court</span>, I ask you, 5. Whether you have not more Reason to give God Thanks that you met with a wise Judge, and a sensible Gentleman, who kept you from shedding innocent Blood, and reviving the meanest and cruellest of all Superstitions amongst us?</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky’s text (above note 135) says that after the judge obtained a remission of the sentence the clergy engaged in the prosecution “drew up a document strongly asserting their belief in the guilt of the accused, animadverting severely upon the conduct of the judge, and concluding with the solemn words, ‘Liberavimus animas nostras.’” Hutchinson’s page confirms the closing words and the reflection on the court, and gives his judgment of it; it does not describe the document itself (it speaks of “your Book”), nor name the judge, on this page. Lecky’s note says of pp. 163–171 “Some noble and liberal remarks”; I read only p. 167. Hutchinson’s chapter title and its 1712 date were seen in the OCR and not read on the image.</p>
</section>

<section id="mohra-1670" class="passage">
<h2>Mohra in Sweden, 1670 (1720 ed., p. 55)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: 1720 edition, p. 55, in the “Chronological Table of Facts”, <a href="https://archive.org/details/b30512967_0001/page/n86/mode/2up">scan</a>.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>1670. <span class="cited-passage">At Mohra in Swedeland, Seventy were condemn’d, and most of them executed. Also Fifteen Children were executed, and 36 ran the Gantlet, and 20 were whipp’d on the Hands.</span> See this answered in Chap. 6.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (text, with note 7): “In 1670, seventy persons were condemned in Sweden”, with the note “Compare Plancey, <em>Dict. Infernale</em>, art. Blokula; Hutchinson on Witchcraft, p. 55; Madden, vol. i. p. 354.” The figure of seventy matches. Hutchinson adds the executions of fifteen children and the lesser punishments; his table ends the entry “See this answered in Chap. 6”, the chapter in which he disputes the Swedish narrative (as printed by Glanvill, translated by Horneck). Lecky’s “condemned” is the narrower statement. Hutchinson’s p. 55 also gives the Rouen <em>arrêt</em> of 1672 releasing accused shepherds (continued on p. 56), which Lecky does not cite here.</p>
</section>

<section id="lowes-90" class="passage">
<h2>Mr. Lowes of Brandeston, the other side of Baxter’s story (1720 ed., p. 90)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: 1720 edition, p. 90, <a href="https://archive.org/details/b30512967_0001/page/n121/mode/2up">scan</a> (chapter “Of the Suffolk Witches”, a letter from the Rev. Mr. Lowes’s neighbour). The long s is printed as s.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Sir, In Answer to your Request concerning Mr. Lowes, my Father was always of the Opinion, that Mr. Lowes suffered wrongfully, and hath often said, that he did believe he was no more a Wizzard than he was. I have heard it from them that watched with him, that <span class="cited-passage">they kept him awake several Nights together, and run him backwards and forwards about the Room, until he was out of Breath</span>: Then they rested him a little, and then ran him again: And thus they did for several Days and Nights together, till he was weary of his Life, and was scarce sensible of what he said or did. They swam him at Framlingham, but that was no true Rule to try him by; for they put in honest People at the same Time, and they swam as well as he.</p>
<p>This walking and running them backward and forward till out of Breath, is so barbarous and senseless, that I should have been apt to think that both Mr. Gaul and Mr. Rivet had taken their Reports from those that had strain’d Things, if I had not now before me … a Book put out by Hopkins himself in the Year 1647. …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (ch. I, note 113): Baxter “relates the whole story with evident pleasure … ‘an old reading parson named Lowis, not far from Framlingham, was one that was hanged, who confessed that he had two imps’ … (<em>World of Spirits</em>, p. 53.) For the other view of the case, see Hutchinson, pp. 88–90.” Page 90 is “the other view”: Lowes suffered wrongfully; he had been kept awake and run about the room for days and nights until, in effect, worn into confessing. Pages 88–89 were not read, and Baxter’s own page is not checked.</p>
</section>
