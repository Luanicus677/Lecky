abstract: |
  <p>Why did accused witches float? A Marburg physician, Scribonius, had an answer, and Binsfeld reports it on Lecky’s p. 315: “water cannot bear witches”, because God destined water for the bath of baptism, and witches, having renounced the Trinity, are rejected by it. This is the belief Lecky describes in his chapter on magic. But Binsfeld, the hard-line suffragan bishop of Trier, does not accept it. He calls it an invention, notes that Bodin condemned the water test as itself magical, and rules that a judge who puts anyone to it “sins mortally”.</p>
  <p>The same book supplies Lecky’s “very amusingly told” story of St Cyprian, then a magician, sending devil after devil against the virgin Justina. The cleverest devil comes to her disguised as another virgin and argues that God told Adam and Eve to “increase and multiply”, so that those who stay virgins “fall into judgment, because we despise the word of God”. On p. 155 Binsfeld lists the devil’s openings, and the eighth, “most frequent in the female sex”, is “desolation, or excessive sadness”: Lecky’s women “whose lives had been clouded by some great sorrow”.</p>

<section id="water" class="passage">
<h2>“Water cannot bear witches”: the cold-water test (pp. 314–315)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin: Trier 1591, “Primum dubium: Quid sentiendum de purgatione vel probatione aquae frigidae?”, pp. 314–315, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_sC0R5IOoPQ8C/page/n344/mode/2up">scans n344</a>–<a href="https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_sC0R5IOoPQ8C/page/n345/mode/2up">n345</a>.</p>
<p>Binsfeld first describes the test as used by “certain judges” in Westphalia and nearby: the suspected witch, bound hand and foot, is thrown into a river or lake; if she floats, she is tortured again; if she sinks, she is acquitted as innocent. He then reports its defender, Wilhelm Adolf Scribonius of Marburg, whose book appeared at the Frankfurt fair in September 1588, against the physicians Johann Ewich and Hermann Neuwalt:</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/binsfeld-1591-p315.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/binsfeld-1591-p315.jpg" alt="Printed Latin: “quia Deus aquam praedestinavit salutari regenerationis lavacro … Nunc pro resolutione dico. Primo: Iudex … qui facit aliquem vel aliquam subire probationem aquae frigidae, mortaliter peccat”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Binsfeld, 1591, p. 315: Scribonius’s theory, Bodin’s objection, and Binsfeld’s ruling. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (Google Books) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>… Causas vero naturales cum reddere nequeat, utpote qui manifestis adversariorum convincitur argumentis, alias confingit; in diabolo quandam levitatem, qua detinentur hujusmodi corpora, ne submergantur: in aqua vero quandam antipathiam, &amp; ut hoc figmentum intelligatur, ponenda breviter, quæ ipse multis profert. <span class="cited-passage">Aqua sagas in se ferre non potest; quia Deus aquam prædestinavit salutari regenerationis lavacro, ut in ea homines abluantur a peccato &amp; regnum Dei ingrediantur; quare odio maleficas aversatur, &amp; reijcit, ut si maxime pro viribus tentent, non tamen submergi possint; cum spreto divino lavacro, toti Trinitati abrenunciaverint.</span> Fatetur tamen &amp; bene dictus author, hanc suam inventionem non esse edicta prætoria. Joannes Bodinus lib. 4. cap. 4. de Magorum Dæmonomania, damnat hoc judicium aquæ frigidæ, tanquam opus quoque magicum.</p>
<p>Nunc pro resolutione dico. Primo: <span class="cited-passage">Judex, vel alius potestate &amp; authoritate fungens, qui facit aliquem vel aliquam subire probationem aquæ frigidæ, mortaliter peccat.</span></p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>… Since he cannot give natural causes, being refuted by his opponents’ plain arguments, he invents others: a kind of lightness in the devil, by which such bodies are held up so that they do not sink, and a kind of antipathy in the water. To make this fiction understood, I must set out briefly what he puts at length. <span class="cited-passage">Water cannot bear witches, because God destined water for the saving bath of regeneration, so that in it men might be washed from sin and enter the kingdom of God; therefore it turns from witches with hatred and rejects them, so that however hard they try they cannot sink, since, despising the divine bath, they have renounced the whole Trinity.</span> Yet the said author admits that this invention of his is no praetor’s edict. Jean Bodin, in book 4, chapter 4 of his <i>Démonomanie</i>, condemns this cold-water trial as itself a magical work.</p>
<p>Now for my resolution. First: <span class="cited-passage">a judge, or anyone else exercising power and authority, who makes any man or woman undergo the cold-water test sins mortally.</span></p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Lecky’s ch. III note 3 gives this as the belief behind “the custom of swimming witches”: “everything tainted with diabolical presence was repelled by” water. The page bears him out as a report of what was believed; it is Scribonius’s theory, not Binsfeld’s, and Binsfeld condemns the test outright. See also the <a href="bodin-demonomanie-des-sorciers.html"><em>Démonomanie</em> page</a> for Bodin.</p>
</section>

<section id="cyprian-justina" class="passage">
<h2>St Cyprian’s devils and the virgin Justina (pp. 464–467)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin: Trier 1591, in the appended <i>Commentarius in titulum Codicis de maleficis et mathematicis</i> (running heads “Comment. in tit. C.” / “De malef. et math.”), pp. 464–467, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_sC0R5IOoPQ8C/page/n494/mode/2up">scans n494</a>–<a href="https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_sC0R5IOoPQ8C/page/n497/mode/2up">n497</a>. Excerpt from pp. 465–466.</p>
<p>A young pagan, in love with the Christian virgin Justina of Antioch, goes to Cyprian, “then still a magician”, and promises him two talents of gold to win her by sorcery. Cyprian sends a demon, then a stronger one; both are put to flight by the sign of the cross. Then he calls up a superior demon, who promises to bring her:</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/binsfeld-1591-p466.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/binsfeld-1591-p466.jpg" alt="Printed Latin page: “dixit: merces autem multa, labor vero modicus. Dixit ei Diabolus: Deus in Paradiso benedixit Adam et Evam, dicens: Crescite et multiplicamini …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Binsfeld, 1591, p. 466: the devil argues for marriage from Genesis. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (Google Books) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>Tunc Diabolus in specie virginis ad Justinam intravit, &amp; sedens super lectum ejus eandem tentando dixit: Hodie missa sum a Christo ad te, vivere tecum in castitate: Multum autem te video vexatam abstinentia. Sancta autem virgo dixit: merces autem multa, labor vero modicus. <span class="cited-passage">Dixit ei Diabolus: Deus in Paradiso benedixit Adam &amp; Evam, dicens: Crescite &amp; multiplicamini. Si ergo in virginitate manserimus, in judicium incidemus, quia verbum Dei contemnimus.</span> Tum virgo turbata surrexit, &amp; per spiritum sensit, quis esset, qui ei loqueretur, <span class="cited-passage">signo crucis se signans: quo facto Dæmon disparuit, &amp; Cypriano confusus apparuit.</span> Qui dixit ei: &amp; tu victus es, ut cæteri subjecti tui? Quomodo enim victi estis ab una virgine Christiana? Dic mihi quæ sit virtus victoriæ ejus. Qui respondit, dicere tibi non possum: quoddam vidi terribile signum Thau. Si autem virtutem hujus signi vis cognoscere, jura mihi quod nunquam velis a me discedere. Qui cum jurasset, ait illi: Vidi signum crucifixi, &amp; Thau: &amp; sicut cera a facie ignis fluxi.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>Then the devil, in the likeness of a virgin, came in to Justina and, sitting on her bed, tempted her, saying: “Today I am sent to you by Christ, to live with you in chastity. But I see you are much worn by abstinence.” The holy virgin said: “The reward is great, and the labour small.” <span class="cited-passage">The devil said to her: “God blessed Adam and Eve in Paradise, saying, Increase and multiply. If then we remain in virginity, we shall fall into judgment, because we despise the word of God.”</span> Then the virgin rose up troubled and perceived in the spirit who it was that spoke to her, <span class="cited-passage">and signed herself with the sign of the cross; at which the demon vanished and appeared, confounded, before Cyprian.</span> Cyprian said to him: “Are you beaten too, like the rest of your underlings? How were you beaten by one Christian virgin? Tell me the power of her victory.” He answered: “I cannot tell you: I saw a terrible sign, the Tau. But if you want to know the power of that sign, swear to me that you will never leave me.” When Cyprian had sworn, the devil said: “I saw the sign of the Crucified, the Tau, and I melted like wax before the fire.”</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Cyprian then renounces the devil and is converted, and later adds martyrdom to his virtue; Binsfeld ends (p. 467, read in the OCR): “Nicephorus Callistus mentions this story, book 5 of his Ecclesiastical History, chapter 27”, Lecky’s “on the authority of Nicephorus” (note 38). Lecky’s retelling in the text, a maiden assailed by “all the resources of archangelic eloquence” in favour of “a young and noble pagan”, follows this version. The legend is that of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprian_and_Justina">Cyprian and Justina</a>.</p>
</section>
