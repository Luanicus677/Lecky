abstract: |
  <p>Lecky mentions, in passing, an ancient wind instrument “which some have placed among the antecedents of the organ, but which seems to have been almost exactly the same as a Scotch bagpipe. I am sorry to say Julian had the bad taste to praise it in one of his epigrams.” His authority is Burney, and Burney is one of the “some”. For Burney the Greek epigram “attributed to the Emperor Julian the Apostate” is “the most ancient proof of an instrument resembling a modern organ blown by bellows, and played with keys”: its reeds of brass are sounded by “a blast that rushes from a leathern cavern beneath their roots”, while “a robust mortal, running with swift fingers over the concordant keys”, makes them sing. The bag of leather may have suggested the bagpipe to Lecky; the keys and the brass pipes do not fit one, and the joke at Julian’s expense is Lecky’s own.</p>

<section id="julian-organ" class="passage">
<h2>The epigram attributed to Julian (vol. II, pp. 65–66)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: London 1782, vol. II, ch. I, pp. 65–66, <a href="https://archive.org/details/10527086bsb/page/n72/mode/2up">scans n72</a>–<a href="https://archive.org/details/10527086bsb/page/n73/mode/2up">n73</a>. Burney prints the Greek in a footnote, which is not transcribed here. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky’s note depends on.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/burney-1782-ii-p65.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/burney-1782-ii-p65.jpg" alt="Printed page: “The most ancient proof of an instrument resembling a modern organ blown by bellows, and played with keys, very different from the Hydraulicon … is a Greek epigram in the Anthologia, attributed to the Emperor Julian the Apostate, who flourished about 364.”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Burney, <em>General History of Music</em>, vol. II, 1782, p. 65. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (Bavarian State Library) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p><span class="cited-passage">The most ancient proof of an instrument resembling a modern organ blown by bellows, and played with keys, very different from the Hydraulicon</span>, which is of much higher antiquity, as has been already shewn in the first volume of this work, p. 512, <span class="cited-passage">is a Greek epigram in the Anthologia, attributed to the Emperor Julian the Apostate, who flourished about 364.</span></p>
<p>I shall here give a literal translation of this epigram, which, though it contain no very beautiful or poetical images, will answer the historical purpose of ascertaining the existence of an instrument in the fourth century, which, in many particulars, resembled a modern organ.</p>
<p>“I see reeds of a new species, the growth of another and a brazen soil; such as are not agitated by our winds, but by a blast that rushes from a leathern cavern beneath their roots; while a <i>robust</i> mortal, running with swift fingers over [p. 66] the concordant <i>keys</i>, makes them, as they smoothly dance, emit melodious sounds.”</p>
<p>Nothing material is omitted in the version of this epigram, or rather enigma, upon the organ, though not a very ingenious one …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky’s ch. III note 82: “There had before been known a water organ, called an hydraulicon. There was also a wind instrument which some have placed among the antecedents of the organ, but which seems to have been almost exactly the same as a Scotch bagpipe. I am sorry to say Julian had the bad taste to praise it in one of his epigrams. (See Burney, <i>Hist. of Music</i>, vol. ii. pp. 65–67.)” Burney’s pages give the hydraulicon and the epigram, but he calls the epigram an “enigma, upon the organ”, says only that it is “attributed” to Julian, and translates its instrument as one with keys. The bagpipe is Lecky’s reading. Burney’s p. 66 goes on to Cassiodorus’s description of the bellows organ, which is probably what Lecky’s “67” covers.</p>
