abstract: |
  <p>Lecky argues that the sense of sin, more than any argument, drove Luther against the indulgences, Wesley and Whitefield against the “frigid moral teaching” of their day, and the Tractarians towards Rome; for “some striking remarks on this” he sends the reader to a novel. In it Froude’s narrator explains why he followed Newman so far and no further. For Newman sin was “a real child of a real devil”, and a heart that felt it so “will surely choose … that one” religion “where the sacraments are most numerous and most constant, and absolution is more than a name”. Hence the aphorism: “If there be any such thing as sin, in proportion to the depth with which men feel it, they will gravitate towards Rome.”</p>
  <p>Lecky’s page numbers match exactly. The book was notorious: William Sewell burned it publicly at Exeter College, and Froude had to resign his fellowship (so the Wikipedia articles on Froude and the novel, citing Ashton and Willey; not from this scan).</p>

<section id="gravitate-towards-rome" class="passage">
<h2>“They will gravitate towards Rome” (pp. 160–161)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: 1849, pp. 160–161, <a href="https://archive.org/details/nemesisoffaith00frou/page/n167/mode/2up">scan n167</a>–n168. The narrator is writing about Newman. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the remarks Lecky points to.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/froude-nemesis-1849-p160.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/froude-nemesis-1849-p160.jpg" alt="Printed page 160: “If there be any such thing as sin, in proportion to the depth with which men feel it, they will gravitate towards Rome. If it be true that the souls even of holy men are as continually contracting infirmity as their bodies are …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Froude, <em>The Nemesis of Faith</em>, 1849, p. 160. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>… The arguments for the Catholicity of the English church continued the same, but he on whom they were to tell was changed. He might have borne her supineness if he could have found the life in her for which he thirsted; but, as his desires deepened with his advances in the real feeling of Christianity, it was natural that his heart should incline where he could find them most fully gratified.</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">If there be any such thing as sin, in proportion to the depth with which men feel it, they will gravitate towards Rome.</span></p>
<p>If it be true that the souls even of holy men are as continually contracting infirmity as their bodies are; if absolution is as constantly necessary for the one as ablution is for the other; as men of cleanly habits of body are more sensitive to the most trifling dirt spot, so men of sensitive consciences are miserable under taints upon a surface which to a vulgar eye seems pure as snow . . . . add to this the conviction that the priest’s voice and hand alone can dispense the purifying stream; and beyond question, where the fountain runs the fullest, thither they will seek to go.</p>
<p>And sin with Newman was real; not a misfortune to be pitied and allowed for; to be talked of gravely in the pulpit, and forgotten when out of it; not a thing to be sentimentally sighed over at the evening tea-party, with complacent feeling that we were pleasing Heaven by calling ourselves children of hell, but in very truth a dreadful monster, a real child of a real devil, so dreadful that at its first [p. 161] appearance among mankind it had convulsed the infinite universe, and that nothing less than a sacrifice, so tremendous that the mind sinks crushed before the contemplation of it, could restore the deranged balance. … To the dry mathematicizing reason, the Catholic, the Anglo-Catholic, the Lutheran, Calvinist, the Socinian, will be equally unacceptable … But sin is of faith, not of mathematics. <span class="cited-passage">And a real human heart, strong enough and deep enough to see it and feel it in its enormity, will surely choose from among the various religions that one where the sacraments are most numerous and most constant, and absolution is more than a name, and confession is possible without episcopal interdictings.</span></p>
<p>For myself I fell off; not because I had determined not to follow, but because I had not yet felt this intensity of hunger and of thirst which could drive me to accept the alternative …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. V, text at note 121) lists the Tractarians last among those driven by a troubled conscience: the sense of sin “urged the first leaders of Tractarianism towards a Church which by authoritative teaching and multiplied absolutions could allay the paroxysms of a troubled conscience.” Froude’s pages make the same point about Newman, in the voice of a character who turned back.</p>
</section>
