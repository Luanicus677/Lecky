abstract: |
  <p>Lecky writes that many Roman mosaics of the ninth to twelfth centuries “exhibit a hideousness which the inexpertness of the artists was quite insufficient to account for, and which was evidently imitated from the emaciation of extreme asceticism”, and refers to Vitet, pp. 268–279. Those pages are Vitet’s summing-up of why the art declined, and his answer is different. The ugliness, he says, was the taste of the barbarians: “the question at issue is a question of barbarism, and precisely the barbarians are forgotten.” Artists in Rome, needing new patrons, learned to please “their half-savage Maecenases”, and the Church let into its chapels “these coarse figures, these haggard eyes … these Scandinavian and Teutonic types, more or less faithful portraits of its formidable allies.”</p>
  <p>Vitet does raise asceticism, earlier, as a question about one mosaic (Saints Cosmas and Damian, p. 245): did the artist mean to express “asceticism, the excess of the spiritual life”, or did he copy the faces of the northern invaders? He answers “On ne saurait le dire” (one cannot say). Lecky takes the first alternative and gives it Vitet’s authority.</p>

<section id="barbarians" class="passage">
<h2>Vitet’s answer: “on oublie les barbares” (pp. 268–279)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: Paris 1864, vol. I, “Les mosaïques chrétiennes de Rome”, part IV, “Fin de la décadence”, pp. 272 and 277, <a href="https://archive.org/details/etudessurlhisto00vitegoog/page/n304/mode/2up">scans n304</a> and <a href="https://archive.org/details/etudessurlhisto00vitegoog/page/n309/mode/2up">n309</a>.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/vitet-1864-p272.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/vitet-1864-p272.jpg" alt="Printed French: “Chose étrange, la question qui s’agite est une question de barbarie, et justement on oublie les barbares. Ce sont eux cependant qui donnent à cette décadence son véritable caractère”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Vitet, vol. I, p. 272. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (Google Books) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>[p. 272] … Seulement on oublie un troisième élément, lequel nous semble le plus visible et le moins contestable de tous. <span class="cited-passage">Chose étrange, la question qui s’agite est une question de barbarie, et justement on oublie les barbares. Ce sont eux cependant qui donnent à cette décadence son véritable caractère, ce qu’elle a d’excessif, d’abrupt, d’incohérent et de désordonné.</span></p>
<p>[p. 277] … elle [l’Église] avait mieux aimé fermer les yeux et laisser prudemment s’introduire, sur les parois de ses chapelles, <span class="cited-passage">ces grossières figures, ces yeux hagards, ces expressions outrées, ces types scandinaves et teutons, portraits plus ou moins fidèles de ses redoutables alliés</span>; puis, cela fait, qu’arriva-t-il? La piété des fidèles prit au sérieux les nouvelles images, les adopta, les consacra …</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>[p. 272] … Only, a third element is forgotten, which seems to us the most visible and least disputable of all. <span class="cited-passage">Strange to say, the question at issue is a question of barbarism, and precisely the barbarians are forgotten. Yet it is they who give this decadence its true character, all that is excessive, abrupt, incoherent and disorderly in it.</span></p>
<p>[p. 277] … the Church had preferred to shut its eyes and prudently let in, on the walls of its chapels, <span class="cited-passage">these coarse figures, these haggard eyes, these exaggerated expressions, these Scandinavian and Teutonic types, more or less faithful portraits of its formidable allies</span>; and then what happened? The piety of the faithful took the new images seriously, adopted them, consecrated them …</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Between these pages (OCR only) Vitet argues that Roman artists, “to please their new patrons, their half-savage Maecenases”, set about unlearning what they knew, and that on p. 278 “barbarism, identifying itself with holiness, soon became almost an article of faith”. His explanation is social and political: the taste of the conquerors, accepted by a Church courting their souls. Lecky’s note 59 puts the decline down to “the emaciation of extreme asceticism”, which is the possibility Vitet left open on p. 245, not the conclusion of the pages Lecky cites.</p>
</section>
