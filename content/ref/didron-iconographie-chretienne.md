abstract: |
  <p>Lecky gives, in quotation marks, as a translation of Didron: “Christ appears more and more melancholy, and often truly terrible. It is, indeed, the <em>rex tremendæ majestatis</em> of our <em>Dies iræ</em>. It is almost the God of the Jews making fear the beginning of wisdom.” The substance is on p. 262, but the English is a free rendering, not a literal one: Didron has “Jésus s’attriste davantage et se montre surtout à sa passion et au dernier jugement. Alors il est vraiment redoutable”. Lecky’s “often truly terrible” turns “at his Passion and the Last Judgment” into “often”; the sentence about the Last Judgment is Didron’s main point.</p>

<section id="christ-redoutable" class="passage">
<h2>“Alors il est vraiment redoutable” (p. 262)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: p. 262, <a href="https://archive.org/details/iconographiechr02didruoft/page/n273/mode/2up">scan</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the sentences Lecky translates.</p>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>… Cependant on ne passe pas sans transition des portraits presque toujours jeunes aux portraits constamment âgés ; dans certaines localités, plus douces de mœurs ou plus en retard sur la coutume régnante, on rencontre quelquefois encore des Jésus souriants et jeunes. <span class="cited-passage">À partir du XII<sup>e</sup> siècle, ces exceptions deviennent de plus en plus rares. Jésus s’attriste davantage et se montre surtout à sa passion et au dernier jugement. Alors il est vraiment redoutable ; c’est bien le « Rex tremendæ majestatis » de notre Dies iræ ; c’est presque le Dieu des Juifs voulant que la crainte soit le commencement de la sagesse</span>¹. Dans les Jugements derniers sculptés aux voussures et peints aux rosaces de nos cathédrales, le Christ semble insensible aux prières de sa mère, qui est placée à sa droite ; de saint Jean évangéliste, son ami, ou de saint Jean-Baptiste, son précurseur, qui sont placés à sa gauche. Il écrase les méchants en leur montrant les trous de ses mains, de ses pieds et de son côté ; il les noie dans le sang qui coule de ses plaies. Les Grecs, plus hébraïsants que les Latins, ont un Christ plus terrible encore. …</p>
<p><small>¹ « Initium sapientiæ timor Domini. » Psaume cx, v. 10.</small></p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>… However, one does not pass without transition from portraits almost always young to portraits constantly aged; in certain localities, milder in manners or further behind the ruling custom, one still sometimes meets smiling and young Jesuses. <span class="cited-passage">From the twelfth century these exceptions become rarer and rarer. Jesus grows sadder and shows himself above all at his passion and at the last judgment. Then he is truly redoubtable; it is indeed the “Rex tremendae majestatis” of our Dies irae; it is almost the God of the Jews, wanting fear to be the beginning of wisdom</span>¹. In the Last Judgments carved in the archivolts and painted in the rose windows of our cathedrals, Christ seems insensible to the prayers of his mother, who is placed at his right; of St John the Evangelist, his friend, or of St John the Baptist, his forerunner, who are placed at his left. He crushes the wicked by showing them the holes of his hands, his feet and his side; he drowns them in the blood that flows from his wounds. The Greeks, more Hebraising than the Latins, have a Christ more terrible still. …</p>
<p><small>¹ “The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom.” Psalm cx [cxi], v. 10.</small></p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Collation with Lecky’s note 52: “‘Christ appears more and more melancholy, and often truly terrible. It is, indeed, the <em>rex tremendæ majestatis</em> of our <em>Dies iræ</em>. It is almost the God of the Jews making fear the beginning of wisdom.’” Lecky renders “Jésus s’attriste davantage” as “Christ appears more and more melancholy” and drops “de plus en plus rares”, “à sa passion et au dernier jugement”; “truly terrible” is Didron’s “vraiment redoutable” (“redoubtable”, “fearsome”), and “often” is Lecky’s. Lecky’s sentence introducing the quotation (“About the twelfth century, this change became almost universal”) is consistent with “à partir du XII<sup>e</sup> siècle, ces exceptions deviennent de plus en plus rares”, a little stronger. The preceding page (p. 261) tells, from Orderic Vitalis, the story of two knights’ dreams, one of a terrible and one of a smiling Christ on the cross; Lecky does not use it.</p>
