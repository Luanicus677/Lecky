abstract: |
  <p>Lecky quotes Montaigne’s outcry at how the question “may a subject rebel against his prince for religion?” was answered by each party according to its fortunes. The passage is Montaigne’s, in the long chapter known as the <em>Apologie de Raimond Sebond</em>. Lecky’s French is slightly modernised and differs in one word, <em>imprudence</em> for <em>impudence</em>, and one repeated <em>et</em>.</p>

<section id="impudence" class="passage">
<h2>The reversals of religious argument (II.12, p. 179)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: p. 179, <a href="https://archive.org/details/19essaispublis03montuoft/page/n192/mode/2up">scan</a>. The passage continues in smaller type at the foot of the page; I have not determined why (the edition’s apparatus is not described in the pages I read). <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: Lecky’s quotation.</p>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>Voyez l’horrible <span class="cited-passage">impudence dequoy nous pelotons les raisons divines et combien irreligieusement nous les avons et rejettées et reprises selon que la fortune nous a changé de place en ces orages publics. Cette proposition si solenne : « S’il est permis au subject de se rebeller et armer contre son prince pour la defense de la religion », souvienne vous en quelles bouches, cette année passée, l’affirmative d’icelle estoit l’arc-boutant d’un parti ; la negative, de quel autre parti c’estoit l’arc-boutant ; et oyez à present de quel quartier vient la voix et instruction de l’une et de l’autre ; et si les armes bruyent moins pour cette cause que pour celle là.</span> Et nous bruslons les gents qui disent qu’il faut faire souffrir à la verité le joug de nostre besoing ; et de combien faict la France pis que de le dire !</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>See the horrible <span class="cited-passage">impudence with which we toss about divine reasons, and how irreligiously we have both rejected them and taken them up again, as fortune has changed our place in these public storms. That proposition so solemn: “Whether it is permitted to a subject to rebel and take up arms against his prince for the defence of religion”: remember in whose mouths, this past year, the affirmative of it was the buttress of one party; the negative, of what other party it was the buttress; and hear now from what quarter comes the voice and teaching of the one and of the other; and whether arms clash less for this cause than for that.</span> And we burn the people who say that truth must be made to suffer the yoke of our need; and how much worse France does than to say it!</p>
</div>

<p>I cannot say whether “imprudence” is Lecky’s slip or the reading of the edition he used: it is not the reading here. Lecky stops at “celle-là” and omits the next sentence about burning those who say that truth must bear the yoke of our need, which is the sharper part.</p>
