<section id="impudence" class="passage">
<h2>The reversals of religious argument (II.12, p. 179)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: p. 179, <a href="https://archive.org/details/19essaispublis03montuoft/page/n192/mode/2up">scan</a>. The passage continues in smaller type at the foot of the page; I have not determined why (the edition’s apparatus is not described in the pages I read). <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: Lecky’s quotation.</p>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>Voyez l’horrible <span class="cited-passage">impudence dequoy nous pelotons les raisons divines et combien irreligieusement nous les avons et rejettées et reprises selon que la fortune nous a changé de place en ces orages publics. Cette proposition si solenne : « S’il est permis au subject de se rebeller et armer contre son prince pour la defense de la religion », souvienne vous en quelles bouches, cette année passée, l’affirmative d’icelle estoit l’arc-boutant d’un parti ; la negative, de quel autre parti c’estoit l’arc-boutant ; et oyez à present de quel quartier vient la voix et instruction de l’une et de l’autre ; et si les armes bruyent moins pour cette cause que pour celle là.</span> Et nous bruslons les gents qui disent qu’il faut faire souffrir à la verité le joug de nostre besoing ; et de combien faict la France pis que de le dire !</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>See the horrible <span class="cited-passage">impudence with which we toss about divine reasons, and how irreligiously we have both rejected them and taken them up again, as fortune has changed our place in these public storms. That proposition so solemn: “Whether it is permitted to a subject to rebel and take up arms against his prince for the defence of religion”: remember in whose mouths, this past year, the affirmative of it was the buttress of one party; the negative, of what other party it was the buttress; and hear now from what quarter comes the voice and teaching of the one and of the other; and whether arms clash less for this cause than for that.</span> And we burn the people who say that truth must be made to suffer the yoke of our need; and how much worse France does than to say it!</p>
</div>
</div>

<p>I cannot say whether “imprudence” is Lecky’s slip or the reading of the edition he used: it is not the reading here. Lecky stops at “celle-là” and omits the next sentence about burning those who say that truth must bear the yoke of our need, which is the sharper part.</p>
</section>

<details class="collapse editor-notes"><summary>Editor’s notes on this page</summary>
<p>Lecky quotes Montaigne’s outcry at how the question “may a subject rebel against his prince for religion?” was answered by each party according to its fortunes. The passage is Montaigne’s, in the long chapter known as the <em>Apologie de Raimond Sebond</em>. Lecky’s French is slightly modernised and differs in one word, <em>imprudence</em> for <em>impudence</em>, and one repeated <em>et</em>.</p>
</details>

<section id="contre-un" class="passage">
<h2>La Boétie’s <i>Servitude volontaire</i>, “rebaptized” <i>Le Contre un</i> (Book I, “De l’amitié”, p. 83)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: Courbet–Royer, vol. 2, Book I ch. XXVIII, p. 83, <a href="https://archive.org/details/19essaispublis02montuoft/page/n96/mode/2up">scan n96</a>. Many editions number this essay I.27, as Lecky does. Spelling as printed. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the renaming Lecky reports.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/montaigne-1900-ii-p83.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/montaigne-1900-ii-p83.jpg" alt="Printed page 83: “… Je me suis advisé d’en emprunter un d’Estienne de la Boitie … C’est un discours auquel il donna nom La Servitude volontaire; mais ceux qui l’ont ignoré l’ont bien proprement depuis rebaptisé Le Contre un. Il l’escrivit par maniere d’essay en sa premiere jeunesse … à l’honneur de la liberté contre les tyrans.”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Montaigne, <em>Essais</em>, ed. Courbet and Royer, vol. 2 (1900), p. 83. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (University of Toronto) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>… Je me suis advisé d’en emprunter un d’Estienne de la Boitie, qui honorera tout le reste de cette besongne. <span class="cited-passage">C’est un discours auquel il donna nom <i>La Servitude volontaire</i>; mais ceux qui l’ont ignoré l’ont bien proprement depuis rebaptisé <i>Le Contre un</i>.</span> Il l’escrivit par maniere d’essay en sa premiere jeunesse, n’ayant pas attaint le dixhuitiesme an de son aage, à l’honneur de la liberté contre les tyrans. Il court pieça és mains des gens d’entendement, <span class="cited-passage">non sans bien grande et meritée recommandation</span>, car il est gentil et plein ce qu’il est possible.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p>… I have thought of borrowing one from Étienne de La Boétie, which will do honour to all the rest of this work. <span class="cited-passage">It is a discourse to which he gave the name <i>Voluntary Servitude</i>; but those who did not know this have since very aptly rebaptized it <i>Against One</i>.</span> He wrote it by way of an essay in his early youth, before he had reached his eighteenth year, in honour of liberty against tyrants. It has long passed through the hands of men of understanding, <span class="cited-passage">not without very great and well-deserved commendation</span>, for it is as graceful and full as can be.</p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation: this edition’s draft.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. V, note 124), on the name <i>Contr’un</i>: “The name was given during the life of Montaigne, who praised it.” Montaigne both reports the new name, approvingly (“bien proprement”), and praises the discourse. He also says La Boétie wrote it before he was eighteen; Lecky’s text adds that La Boétie died “when only in his thirty-second year”, which is not on this page.</p>
</section>

<section id="castalio" class="passage">
<h2>Castellio and Giraldus, dead of want (Book I, ch. 35, p. 160)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: Courbet–Royer, vol. 2, Book I ch. XXXV, “D’un défaut de nos polices”, p. 160, <a href="https://archive.org/details/19essaispublis02montuoft/page/n173/mode/2up">scan n173</a>. The editors’ own note says the chapter is numbered XXXIV in other editions, as in Lecky’s citation. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the “kindly sentences” Lecky mentions.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/montaigne-1900-ii-p160.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/montaigne-1900-ii-p160.jpg" alt="Printed page 160: “J’entens, avec une grande honte de nostre siecle, qu’à nostre veüe deux tres-excellens personnages en sçavoir sont morts en estat de n’avoir pas leur soul à manger: Lilius Gregorius Giraldus en Italie, et Sebastianus Castalio en Allemagne …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Montaigne, <em>Essais</em> I.35, ed. Courbet and Royer, vol. 2, p. 160. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (University of Toronto) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p><span class="cited-passage">J’entens, avec une grande honte de nostre siecle, qu’à nostre veüe deux tres-excellens personnages en sçavoir sont morts en estat de n’avoir pas leur soul à manger: Lilius Gregorius Giraldus en Italie, et Sebastianus Castalio en Allemagne; et croy qu’il y a mil’ hommes qui les eussent appellez avec tres-advantageuses conditions, s’ils l’eussent sceu.</span> Le monde n’est pas si generalement corrompu que je ne sache tel homme qui souhaiteroit de bien grande affection que les moyens que les siens luy ont mis en main se peussent employer … à mettre à l’abry de la necessité les personnages rares et remarquables …</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p><span class="cited-passage">I hear, to the great shame of our age, that before our eyes two men most excellent in learning have died without enough to eat: Lilius Gregorius Giraldus in Italy, and Sebastianus Castalio in Germany; and I believe there are a thousand men who would have invited them on very advantageous terms, had they known of it.</span> The world is not so generally corrupt that I do not know a man who would most heartily wish that the means his family put into his hands could be used … to shelter from want such rare and remarkable persons …</p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation: this edition’s draft.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. IV, text at note 71) says that Castellio, cast out by Protestants and Catholics alike for his plea for toleration, died almost of starvation, and that “a few kindly sentences of Montaigne” were among the rare tributes to him. See also the <a class="popup-ref" href="castellio-traite-des-heretiques.html">Castellio page</a>.</p>
</section>
