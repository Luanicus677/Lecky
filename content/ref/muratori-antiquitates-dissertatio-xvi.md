abstract: |
  <p>Lecky cites Muratori as “a good history of the rise of Christian usurers”. Muratori’s pages explain why medieval Europe called its moneylenders “Lombards” and “Cahorsins”. The Italians, “flocking to so sweet a gain”, lent money across France and England, and the Pope used them to collect his revenues. Dante put the usurers of Cahors in hell next to Sodom (“Sodoma e Caorsa”), and a fourteenth-century commentator explained that Cahors is “a city in France where nearly everyone is a usurer”. The great Florentine companies of the Bardi and Peruzzi, Muratori adds, were ruined because they lent Edward III of England more than he could ever repay.</p>
  <p>Lecky’s next sentence, that the Third Lateran Council of 1179 complained that usury was “almost every where practised” and refused usurers communion and Christian burial, comes straight from the canon Muratori quotes: “Since the crime of usury has taken such root almost everywhere …”. And kings expelled the moneylenders, then let them back: “they were forever condemned, and nevertheless always kept.”</p>

<section id="florentines" class="passage">
<h2>The Florentine companies, and “so sweet a gain” (col. 889)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin: Milan 1738, vol. I, col. 889, <a href="https://archive.org/details/antiquitatesit01mura/page/n483/mode/2up">scan</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky reports.</p>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>Quae a Johanne Villanio appellantur Societates <i>Scalorum</i>, <i>Peruzzorum</i>, <i>Acciajoliorum</i>, <i>Bardorum</i>, <i>Ammanatorum</i> &amp;c. eo prae ceteris spectabant, <span class="cited-passage">ut ex pecunia mutuo data foenebris sibi quaestus redundaret</span>, quum et ipsi ab aliis mutuo acciperent, sed leviorem reddendo usuram. Auctor est idem Villanius, inde manasse earum Societatum ruinam, quod Eduardo III. Angliae Regi immanem pecuniae vim mutuam dedissent: is autem belli impensis prementibus datam fidem liberare nequiverit. … <i>Parmene bene, se non ch’io intendo, che i Fiorentini sono grandi prestatori a usura.</i> Neque tantum Florentini ac Etrusci, sed et alii Italiae Populi ad tam dulce lucrum convolantes, tum in Italia, tum in Gallia et Anglia, mercaturam inierunt …</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>The companies that Giovanni Villani calls those of the <i>Scali</i>, the <i>Peruzzi</i>, the <i>Acciaiuoli</i>, the <i>Bardi</i>, the <i>Ammanati</i> and others aimed above all <span class="cited-passage">at drawing a usurious profit from money lent</span>, since they too borrowed from others, but paid a lighter interest. The same Villani says that the ruin of those companies came from their having lent an enormous sum to Edward III, King of England, who, pressed by the costs of war, could not keep his word. … [A count, mocking a rival’s boast of wealth:] “<i>Very fine, except that I hear the Florentines are great lenders at usury.</i>” And not only the Florentines and Tuscans, but other peoples of Italy too, flocking to so sweet a gain, took up trade both in Italy and in France and England …</p>
</div>
</div>
</section>

<section id="sodoma-caorsa" class="passage">
<h2>“Sodoma e Caorsa”: Dante and the usurers of Cahors (col. 891)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin, with Dante’s Italian: Milan 1738, vol. I, col. 891, <a href="https://archive.org/details/antiquitatesit01mura/page/n484/mode/2up">scan</a>.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/muratori-1738-col891.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/muratori-1738-col891.jpg" alt="Printed Latin column on the expulsion of usurers from England, quoting Matthew Paris on the ‘Caursini’" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Muratori, col. 891: the usurers in England, from Edward the Confessor to Henry III. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (University of Toronto) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>… Dantes Aldigerius Florentinus, qui Cant. XI. Infern. in detestandos Foeneratores invehitur, atque inter cetera scribit:</p>
<p><i lang="it">E però lo minor giron suggella<br>Del segno suo e Sodoma e Caorsa.</i></p>
<p>Idest nefando vitio inquinatos, et Usurarios. En ut non Corsinam gentem, sed Cadurcum Civitatem Galliae, quae Caorsa ab Italis dicebatur, ob foenebre lucrum in Italia quoque famosam, criminis hujus Dantes synchronus arguit. Audi et Benevenutum Imolensem … <i>E Caorsa: idest Usurarios. Caturgium enim est Civitas in Gallia, in qua quasi omnes sunt Foeneratores.</i> …</p>
<p>In Anglia Eduvardus Rex summe pius … <i>Usurarios defendit</i> (idest interdixit) <i>ne remanerent in Regno.</i> … Sunt autem Matthaei Parisii verba ad Annum MCCXXXV.: <i>Invaluit autem his diebus adeo Caursinorum</i> (idest Gallicorum Trapezitarum) <i>pestis abominanda, ut vix esset aliquis in tota Anglia, qui retibus illorum jam non illaquearetur. Etiam ipse Rex debito inaestimabili eis tenebatur obligatus.</i></p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>… Dante Alighieri the Florentine, who in Canto XI of the <i>Inferno</i> inveighs against the detestable usurers, and writes among other things:</p>
<p>“<i>And therefore the smallest round seals with its mark both Sodom and Cahors.</i>”</p>
<p>That is, those stained with the unspeakable vice, and usurers. See how Dante, a contemporary, accuses of this crime not the Corsican people but Cahors, a city of France that the Italians called Caorsa, famous in Italy too for its usurious gain. Hear also Benvenuto da Imola …: “<i>And Cahors: that is, usurers. For Cahors is a city in France in which nearly all are usurers.</i>” …</p>
<p>In England King Edward, a most pious king, … “<i>forbade usurers to remain in the kingdom.</i>” … And these are the words of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Paris">Matthew Paris</a> for the year 1235: “<i>In these days the abominable plague of the Cahorsins</i> (that is, French money-changers) <i>grew so strong that there was hardly anyone in all England who was not already caught in their nets. Even the King himself was bound to them by an incalculable debt.</i>”</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>This is the passage behind Lecky’s “From Italy usury passed to France and England”. Muratori takes the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahorsin">Cahorsins</a> for Frenchmen and the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lombard_banking">Lombards</a> for Italians: “under the name of Lombards came the Italians, the French under that of Cahorsins” (col. 892).</p>
</section>

<section id="lateran-iii" class="passage">
<h2>“Crimen usurarum ita inolevit”: the Third Lateran Council, 1179 (col. 892)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin: Milan 1738, vol. I, col. 892, <a href="https://archive.org/details/antiquitatesit01mura/page/n484/mode/2up">scan</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky reports.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/muratori-1738-col892.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/muratori-1738-col892.jpg" alt="Printed Latin column: “Sed praecipuum contra eos fulmen emersit ex Concilio Generali Lateranensi Tertio, Anno MCLXXIX. Canone XXV”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Muratori, col. 892, with canon 25 of the Third Lateran Council. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (University of Toronto) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>Viden’ ut <i>Lombardi</i> a <i>Caorcinis</i> ubique distinguuntur? Ergo sub <i>Lombardorum</i> nomine Itali, Galli sub <i>Caorcinorum</i> veniebant. <span class="cited-passage">Sed quod Principes suis legibus evertere velle videbantur, improba illa gens aut tuebatur, aut brevi restaurabat oblatione munerum, ita ut perpetuo quidem improbaretur, et semper nihilominus retineretur.</span></p>
<p>Interea grassante eiusmodi peste minime silebant Romani Pontifices, aliique sacri Pastores Ecclesiae. Sed praecipuum contra eos fulmen emersit ex <span class="cited-passage">Concilio Generali Lateranensi Tertio, Anno MCLXXIX. Canone XXV. Ibi Alexander III. Papa, ceterique Patres in haec verba decreverunt: <i>Quia in omnibus fere locis crimen Usurarum ita inolevit, ut multi aliis negotiis praetermissis, quasi licite Usuras exerceant</i> &amp;c. <i>Ideo constituimus, ut Usurarii manifesti nec ad Communionem admittantur Altaris, nec Christianam, si in hoc peccato decesserint, accipiant sepulturam</i></span> &amp;c. Eadem poena contra illos renovata est, aliis insuper adiectis, Anno MCCLXXIV. in Concilio Generali Lugdunensi II. Canone XXVI.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>Do you see how the <i>Lombards</i> are everywhere distinguished from the <i>Cahorsins</i>? So the Italians came under the name of <i>Lombards</i>, the French under that of <i>Cahorsins</i>. <span class="cited-passage">But what princes seemed to wish to overturn by their laws, that shameless race either defended, or soon restored by offering gifts, so that it was forever condemned, and nevertheless always kept.</span></p>
<p>Meanwhile, as this plague raged, the Roman pontiffs and the other sacred pastors of the Church were by no means silent. But the chief thunderbolt against them came from <span class="cited-passage">the Third General Council of the Lateran, in the year 1179, canon 25. There Pope Alexander III and the other Fathers decreed in these words: “<i>Since the crime of usury has taken such root almost everywhere that many, leaving other business aside, practise usury as if it were lawful</i>, etc. <i>We therefore decree that manifest usurers shall not be admitted to the communion of the altar, nor, if they die in this sin, receive Christian burial</i>”</span>, etc. The same penalty was renewed against them, with further additions, in the year 1274, at the Second General Council of Lyon, canon 26.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Lecky’s note 45 adds that the council “is reckoned a general one by the Catholics”, the point of Muratori’s “Concilio Generali”. Lecky’s “should be absolved at the hour of death” is not in the clause Muratori quotes; it may come from the part he cuts with “&amp;c.”, which this edition has not checked against the council’s text.</p>
</section>
