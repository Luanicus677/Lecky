abstract: |
  <p>In 1626, when the government seemed ready to grant Irish Catholics some relief, the Protestant bishops met under Archbishop Ussher and put their objection in writing: “The religion of the Papists is superstitious, and idolatrous; their faith and doctrine, erroneous and heretical; their Church in respect of both, apostatical. To give them therefore a toleration … is a grievous sin.” Lecky quotes it as the voice of “nearly all the Irish Protestant bishops”; his source says “twelve of the Prelates”. He also drops “and idolatrous” without a mark. The bishops’ first reason is the sharpest: toleration would make them accessory “to the perdition of the seduced people, which perish in the deluge of the Catholick apostacy”.</p>

<blockquote>
<p>… which ought to be pursued. Twelve of the Prelates assembled, and drew up a form of protestation, which was as follows:</p>
<p>“The Judgment of divers of the Arch-Bishops, and Bishops of Ireland, concerning Toleration of Religion.</p>
<p>“<span class="cited-passage">The religion of the Papists is superstitious</span>, and idolatrous; <span class="cited-passage">their faith and doctrine, erroneous and heretical; their Church in respect of both, apostatical. To give them therefore a toleration, or to consent that they may freely exercise their religion, and profess their faith and doctrine, is a grievous sin</span>, and that in two respects: For,</p>
<p>“1. It is to make our selves accessary, not only to their superstitions, idolatries, and heresies, and in a word, to all the abominations of Popery; but also (which is a consequent of the former) to the perdition of the seduced people, which perish in the deluge of the Catholick apostacy. …</p>
</blockquote>

<p>The words are Elrington’s print of the bishops’ judgment, with “and idolatrous” silently dropped. Lecky’s “nearly all the Irish Protestant bishops” is his own estimate; Elrington says twelve assembled, and the judgment itself speaks only of “divers” archbishops and bishops.</p>

<section id="war-unjust-239" class="passage">
<h2>“War made by subjects, though really intending such an end, is unjust” (p. 239)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: Dublin 1847, vol. I, p. 239, <a href="https://archive.org/details/wholeworksofmost0001char/page/n492/mode/2up">scan n492</a>: Ussher’s answers, preserved by Dr. Parr, to questions put to him in the Civil War about the duty of subjects. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the doctrine Lecky refers to.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/elrington-ussher-1847-p239.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/elrington-ussher-1847-p239.jpg" alt="Printed page: “To the second. For the discerning of the justness of the cause … For first, the means they use is war maintained against their Sovereign; the end pretended is the defence of religion, laws, liberties: but war made by subjects, though really intending such an end, is unjust. I. It has no warrant of Scripture, but is disallowed Prov. xxx. 31. ‘No rising up against a King.’”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Elrington, <em>Life of Ussher</em>, 1847, p. 239. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (Newberry Library) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>“To the second. “For the discerning of the justness of the cause, we must not look only at the ends pretended (which though never so fair and specious do not justifie a bad cause or unlawful means) nor at the wickedness or evil carriage of instruments imployed in the prosecution; which doth not conclude the cause to be bad and unjust: but we must look at the means used for such ends and then consider the ends, whether intended by those who do pretend them. By these we shall see the cause of the adverse party to the King is unjust.</p>
<p>“For first, <span class="cited-passage">the means they use is war maintained against their Sovereign; the end pretended is the defence of religion, laws, liberties: but war made by subjects, though really intending such an end, is unjust.</span></p>
<p>“I. It has no warrant of Scripture, but is disallowed Prov. xxx. 31. <span class="cited-passage">‘No rising up against a King.’</span> 1 Sam. viii. 18. …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. V, note 89), after citing Jeremy Taylor on passive obedience: “Ussher, who was perhaps still more competent than Taylor to express the sentiments of the Fathers, was at least equally emphatic. See Elrington’s Life of Ussher, vol. i. p. 239.” The page bears him out: even a war genuinely meant to defend religion and liberty is unjust if subjects make it against their king. Ussher argues here from Scripture, not from the Fathers.</p>
</section>
