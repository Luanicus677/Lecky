abstract: |
  <p>Lecky says More “gives a frightful description of the misery and the crimes resulting from the ejectments” that turned English farmland into pasture, and that “he speaks of twenty men hung on one gibbet.” Both things are in Book I, but they are not one picture. The twenty on a gallows come from a lawyer at Cardinal Morton’s table who <em>praises</em> “that strait and rigorous justice”, and wonders that thieves are still everywhere; Hythloday’s answer, a few pages later, is the famous sheep that “eat up and swallow down the very men themselves”.</p>
  <p>The number is not a rule, either. Robinson’s English says the felons were “for the most part .xx. hanged together upon one gallows”; More’s Latin says they were hanged everywhere, “sometimes twenty on one cross” (<i>nonnunquam suspendi uiginti in una cruce</i>), as Lupton’s note on the page points out.</p>

<section id="gallows" class="passage">
<h2>“Twenty hanged together upon one gallows” (p. 43)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Lupton’s edition, p. 43, <a href="https://archive.org/details/theutopiaofsirth00moreuoft/page/n154/mode/2up">scan n154</a>: Robinson’s English (1551) above, More’s Latin (1518) below. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the words behind Lecky’s “twenty men hung on one gibbet”.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/more-utopia-gallows-p43.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/more-utopia-gallows-p43.jpg" alt="Robinson’s English: “It chaunced on a certayne daye, when I sate at hys table, there was also a certayne laye man, cunnynge in the lawes of yowre Realme … were for the moste part .xx. hanged together vpon one gallowes.” Below, More’s Latin: “narrabat nonnunquam suspendi uiginti in una cruce”." loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>More, <em>Utopia</em>, Book I, ed. Lupton (1895), p. 43. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>‘It chaunced on a certayne daye, when I sate at hys table, there was also a certayne laye man, cunnynge in the lawes of yowre Realme. Whyche, I can not tel wherof takyng occasyon, began dyligently and busily to prayse that strayte and rygorous iustice, which at that tyme was there executed upon fellones, who, as he sayde, <span class="cited-passage">were for the moste part .xx. hanged together vpon one gallowes</span>. And, seyng so fewe escapyd punyshement, he sayd he coulde not chewse but greatly wonder and maruell, howe and by what euill lucke it should so cum to passe, that theues neuertheles were in euery place so ryffe and ranke.</p>
</blockquote>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>Forte fortuna quum die quodam in eius mensa essem, laicus quidam legum uestratium peritus aderat. is nescio unde nactus occasionem, coepit accurate laudare rigidam illam iustitiam, quae tum illic exercebatur in fures, quos passim narrabat <span class="cited-passage">nonnunquam suspendi uiginti in una cruce</span>; atque eo uehementius dicebat se mirari, cum tam pauci elaberentur supplicio, quo malo fato fieret, uti tam multi tamen ubique grassarentur.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p>One day, as it happened, when I was at his table, a layman learned in your country’s laws was there. Taking his occasion from I know not what, he began to praise with great care that rigorous justice which was then being done there upon thieves, who, he said, were being hanged everywhere, <span class="cited-passage">sometimes twenty on one cross</span>; and he said he wondered all the more, since so few escaped punishment, by what ill fate it came about that so many were nevertheless at large everywhere.</p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation of the Latin: this edition’s draft.</p>
</div>
</div>

<section id="sheep" class="passage">
<h2>“Your shepe … eate vp and swallow down the very men” (p. 51)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Lupton’s edition, p. 51, <a href="https://archive.org/details/theutopiaofsirth00moreuoft/page/n162/mode/2up">scan n162</a>. Hythloday is answering the lawyer before the Cardinal. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the passage on enclosure that Lecky’s text summarises.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/more-utopia-sheep-p51.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/more-utopia-sheep-p51.jpg" alt="Robinson’s English: “But yet this is not onlye the necessary cause of stealing … your shepe, that were wont to be so myke and tame, and so smal eaters, now … be become so greate deuowerers, and so wylde, that they eate vp and swallow down the very men them selfes.” Below, the Latin: “Oues, inquam, uestrae … ut homines deuorent ipsos”." loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>More, <em>Utopia</em>, Book I, ed. Lupton (1895), p. 51. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>“But yet this is not onlye the necessary cause of stealing. There is an other which as I suppose is proper and peculiare to yow Englishe men alone.” “What is that?” quod the Cardenall. “Forsoth” (quod I), <span class="cited-passage">“your shepe, that were wont to be so myke and tame, and so smal eaters, now, as I heare saie, be become so greate deuowerers, and so wylde, that they eate vp and swallow down the very men them selfes. They consume, destroy, and deuoure hole fieldes, howses, and cities.</span></p>
</blockquote>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>Neque haec tamen sola est furandi necessitas. Est alia magis, quantum credo, peculiaris uobis. Quaenam est ea? inquit Cardinalis. <span class="cited-passage">Oues, inquam, uestrae, quae tam mites esse, tamque exiguo solent ali, nunc (uti fertur) tam edaces atque indomitae esse coeperunt, ut homines deuorent ipsos; agros, domos, oppida uastent ac depopulentur.</span></p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p>Yet this is not the only thing that drives men to steal. There is another, I think, more peculiar to you. What is that? said the Cardinal. <span class="cited-passage">Your sheep, I said, which used to be so mild and to feed on so little, have now (so it is said) begun to be so greedy and so wild that they devour men themselves; they lay waste and depopulate fields, houses and towns.</span></p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation of the Latin: this edition’s draft.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Hythloday goes on, over the next pages (read in the OCR only, except the line quoted here), to describe noblemen and abbots enclosing all into pasture, “leauyng nothing stondynge but only the churche, to make of it a shepehowse”, and the evicted tenants driven to beg or steal, and then hanged.</p>
