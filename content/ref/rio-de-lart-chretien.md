abstract: |
  <p>Lecky’s note on oil painting (“generally said to have been invented in the beginning of the fifteenth century by Van Eyck, who died in 1440; but the claim of Van Eyck is not undisputed. It was introduced into Italy about 1452 by a Sicilian painter named Antonello”) condenses these two pages of Rio. Rio’s story: <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonello_da_Messina">Antonello da Messina</a> saw a painting by “Jean de Bruges” at Naples and travelled to Flanders to learn its secret; and in a footnote, oil painting “was known in Italy long before this time … <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_van_Eyck">Jan van Eyck</a> only perfected the process”.</p>
  <p>Lecky’s “about 1452” is Rio’s “at the very time we are speaking of”, which on the previous page is the year Finiguerra’s engraved pax appeared at Florence, “at the very time when Faust and Gutenberg printed their first Latin Bible at Mainz”. And Rio, a devout critic, cannot forgive Antonello his reasons for living at Venice; he cites Vasari: “being a person much given to pleasures, and wholly venereal, he resolved to live always in Venice”.</p>

<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>Une autre découverte, non moins importante, et qui influa, non pas sur la quantité, mais sur la qualité et sur la durée des produits, fut celle de la Peinture à l’huile, <span class="cited-passage">introduite en Italie, précisément à l’époque dont nous parlons, par un peintre Sicilien nommé Antonello da Messina</span>, qui, après avoir vu un tableau de Jean de Bruges à Naples, avait fait le voyage de Flandres pour apprendre de lui son secret. Que cet apprentissage ait eu lieu par des rapports directs avec <span class="cited-passage">l’inventeur, mort en 1440</span>, ou qu’il ait eu lieu, comme cela est vraisemblable, par l’intermédiaire de Roger de Bruges, qui visita l’Italie à l’occasion du jubilé de 1450, il est certain qu’Antonello da Messina s’appropria l’invention de l’artiste ultramontain avec un bonheur extraordinaire. … Si son biographe Vasari ne nous disait pas l’ignoble motif qui lui fit préférer le séjour de Venise à tous les autres, nous serions tenté de croire qu’il cherchait dans ce patriciat, si riche en grands citoyens, des types majestueux et héroïques pour son pinceau; mais hélas! il y cherchait tout simplement la facile satisfaction des penchants qui lui étaient communs avec son contemporain Lippi.</p>
<p class="note">(1) <span class="cited-passage">La peinture à l’huile était connue en Italie longtemps avant cette époque, comme cela est prouvé par plusieurs documents incontestables. Jean Van Eyck ne fit que perfectionner ce procédé</span>, dont on s’était peu occupé avant lui. … (2) <i lang="it">Per essere persona molto dedita ai piaceri et tutta venerea, si risolvè abitar sempre in Vinezia.</i> Vasari, <i>Vita di Antonello da Messina</i>.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>Another discovery, no less important, which affected not the quantity but the quality and durability of the works, was that of oil painting, <span class="cited-passage">brought into Italy, at the very time we are speaking of, by a Sicilian painter named Antonello da Messina</span>, who, having seen a picture by John of Bruges at Naples, had made the journey to Flanders to learn its secret from him. Whether this apprenticeship came about through direct dealings with <span class="cited-passage">the inventor, who died in 1440</span>, or, as is likely, through Roger of Bruges, who visited Italy for the jubilee of 1450, it is certain that Antonello da Messina made the transalpine artist’s invention his own with extraordinary success. … If his biographer Vasari did not tell us the ignoble motive that made him prefer living in Venice to anywhere else, we should be tempted to think he sought in that patriciate, so rich in great citizens, majestic and heroic types for his brush; but alas! what he sought there was simply the easy satisfaction of the inclinations he shared with his contemporary Lippi.</p>
<p class="note">(1) <span class="cited-passage">Oil painting was known in Italy long before this time, as several incontestable documents prove. Jan van Eyck only perfected the process</span>, which had been little attended to before him. … (2) “Being a person much given to pleasures, and wholly venereal, he resolved to live always in Venice.” Vasari, <i>Life of Antonello da Messina</i>.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>The “époque dont nous parlons” is fixed on p. 380 by <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maso_Finiguerra">Maso Finiguerra</a>’s famous engraved <i>pax</i>, which “appeared at Florence in 1452”: hence Lecky’s “about 1452”. “Jean de Bruges” is Rio’s name for Jan van Eyck, as was common at the time.</p>
