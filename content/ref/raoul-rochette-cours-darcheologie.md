abstract: |
  <p>Lecky’s sentences on Greek art (“The gods, it was emphatically said, were naked. To represent an emperor naked, was deemed the highest form of flattery, because it was to represent his apotheosis. The athletic games … furnish the most admirable models to the sculptors”) follow these two pages of Raoul-Rochette closely, down to the quotation from Seneca, <i>Deus nudus est</i>, which Lecky gives in note 56.</p>
  <p>Raoul-Rochette’s argument: all Greek gods, heroes, athletes and famous men were shown naked, because the gymnasium gave artists their models and the laws demanding athletes’ statues freed art “from every veil as from every fetter”. Goddesses were the exception, for women were kept out of public life; even Praxiteles’ naked Venus of Cnidus was at first “a reprehensible innovation”. The Romans kept the custom without its reasons, and showed even Pompey and Agrippa naked.</p>

<section id="nudity" class="passage">
<h2>“Dieu est nu”: the law of nudity (pp. 268–270)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">French: Paris 1828, ninth lesson, pp. 268–270, <a href="https://archive.org/details/coursdarcheolog00roch/page/n273/mode/2up">scans n273</a>–<a href="https://archive.org/details/coursdarcheolog00roch/page/n275/mode/2up">n275</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky draws on.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/raoul-rochette-cours-1828-pp269-270.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/raoul-rochette-cours-1828-pp269-270.jpg" alt="Two printed French pages: “Or, cette nudité, principe certain …” and “de pourpre; car Dieu est nu”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Raoul-Rochette, pp. 269 (misnumbered 169) and 270. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="fr">
<p>Ainsi tous les dieux, de quelque ordre qu’ils fussent, tous les demi-dieux ou génies, tous les héros, les athlètes, les hommes célèbres, de toute condition, étaient représentés absolument nus, ou avec une partie de vêtement insignifiante. Il n’y eut guère d’exceptions à ce principe général, que celle qui concerna les déesses, représentées toujours vêtues, si ce n’est Vénus …</p>
<p>Or, cette <i>nudité</i>, principe certain, incontestable de la perfection imitative à laquelle s’éleva l’art grec … <span class="cited-passage">de quelle autre source put-elle dériver, que de ces habitudes gymnastiques qui offrirent d’abord aux artistes les plus beaux modèles</span>, et des lois en rapport avec les mêmes institutions, qui, demandant sans cesse à l’art des statues athlétiques, avaient ainsi émancipé complètement l’imitation, l’avaient affranchie de toute voile, aussi bien que de toute entrave … Ce principe se trouvait, d’ailleurs, conforme aux idées que les anciens se faisaient de la divinité, et des hommes qui lui ressemblent; <span class="cited-passage">ils se représentaient <i>Dieu nu</i>, comme l’être qui a tout donné, et qui n’a besoin de rien. C’est une idée que Sénèque exprime plusieurs fois, et, entre autres, dans ce passage d’une de ses épîtres: <i>ce n’est pas la richesse qui rend semblable à Dieu; car Dieu n’a rien; et ce n’est pas non plus un vêtement de pourpre; car Dieu est nu.</i></span> …</p>
<p>Car les Romains, qui n’avaient déjà plus, ni les mêmes mœurs, ni les mêmes idées, imitèrent encore les Grecs en ce point; <span class="cited-passage">ils représentèrent entièrement <i>nus</i>, non-seulement leurs <i>empereurs</i>, à qui cette nudité convenait d’ailleurs comme un signe d’apothéose, comme un trait de ressemblance avec la Divinité</span>; non-seulement des personnages déifiés, tels qu’<i>Antinoüs</i>, mais de simples héros qui n’avaient pas joui d’un pareil honneur, tels que <i>Pompée</i> ou <i>Agrippa</i> …</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>So all the gods, of whatever rank, all the demigods or genii, all the heroes, the athletes, the famous men of every condition, were represented entirely naked, or with some insignificant piece of clothing. There was hardly any exception to this general principle except the goddesses, who were always shown clothed, apart from Venus …</p>
<p>Now this <i>nudity</i>, the certain and incontestable principle of the imitative perfection to which Greek art rose … <span class="cited-passage">from what other source could it come than from those habits of the gymnasium that first offered artists the finest models</span>, and from the laws connected with the same institutions, which, constantly demanding athletic statues from art, had thus completely emancipated imitation, freed it from every veil as from every fetter … This principle agreed, moreover, with the ideas the ancients had of the deity, and of the men who resemble it: <span class="cited-passage">they pictured <i>God naked</i>, as the being who has given everything and needs nothing. It is an idea Seneca expresses several times, among others in this passage of one of his letters: <i>it is not riches that make one like God, for God has nothing; nor is it a purple robe, for God is naked.</i></span> …</p>
<p>For the Romans, who no longer had the same customs or the same ideas, still imitated the Greeks on this point: <span class="cited-passage">they represented entirely <i>naked</i> not only their <i>emperors</i>, to whom this nudity was in any case fitting as a sign of apotheosis, as a mark of resemblance to the Divinity</span>; not only deified persons such as <i>Antinous</i>, but mere heroes who had not enjoyed such an honour, such as <i>Pompey</i> or <i>Agrippa</i> …</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Raoul-Rochette’s footnote gives the Seneca reference as “Epist. 31”, the letter Lecky cites in note 56 (“Seneca, Ep. xxxi”). Lecky’s “the highest form of flattery” is his own gloss on “a sign of apotheosis”.</p>
