abstract: |
  <p>Lecky sends readers wanting “Kant’s influence on German Rationalism” to the English divine Hugh James Rose, and the pages he gives are exactly Rose’s treatment of the subject. Rose is careful: he speaks of Kant’s influence “in encreasing the tendency to Rationalism”, and calls it absurd to say that Kant’s philosophy “gave rise to that state of religious opinion”, since Kant’s first great work appeared only in 1781, after Basedow, Steinbart and Bahrdt; and the Prussian king’s edict of 1788 complained of a licence among Protestant ministers that he had “long” observed. The following pages show how Kant’s <i>Religion within the Limits of Reason Alone</i> kept the words of Christian doctrine while giving them “quite new meanings”, and taught readers to interpret Scripture by its moral use.</p>

<h2>The influence of Kant (1829 edition, p. 183)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: 1829, p. 183, <a href="https://archive.org/details/a606734800roseuoft/page/n220/mode/2up">scan n220</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the subject Lecky refers to.</p>

It has been sometimes absurdly said that his philosophy gave rise to that state of religious opinion …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Rose, <em>The State of Protestantism in Germany</em>, 1829, p. 183. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (University of Toronto) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>… But there is one point connected with the history of that progress on which it seems necessary to say a few words. <span class="cited-passage">I mean the influence of Kant, in encreasing the tendency to Rationalism.</span> It has been sometimes absurdly said that his philosophy gave rise to that state of religious opinion. But his first work appeared only in 1781, while the works of Basedow, Steinbart, Bahrdt, and many others had been published previously. If we look indeed to the famous ‘Religions-edikt’ published by Frederick William II. of Prussia, in 1788, we find the king saying that he had <i>long</i> observed the strange licentiousness of the Protestant ministers in matters of doctrine, their rejection of many Christian [doctrines] …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (ch. II, text at note 21): “In Germany, Kant from the beginning pronounced this mode of reasoning to be unphilosophical; … On Kant’s influence on German Rationalism, see Rose <i>On Protestantism in Germany</i>, pp. 183–190.” The reference is exact; Rose’s qualification, that Kant strengthened a movement already under way, is worth having beside Lecky’s sentence. (“Encreasing” is Rose’s spelling.)</p>
