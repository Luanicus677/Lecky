abstract: |
  <p>Lecky says Bossuet observes that “Abimelech”, a name common to all the kings of Palestine, means “my father king”. The remark is in the <em>Defensio</em>: Bossuet writes that from the very beginning of the human race kings were held in the place of parents, as even the name Abimelech common to the Palestinian kings shows, for “Abimelech” is Latinised <em>Pater meus Rex</em>.</p>

<section id="abimelech" class="passage">
<h2>“Pater meus Rex” (p. 128)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin: p. 128, <a href="https://archive.org/details/defensiodeclarat01boss/page/n193/mode/2up">scan</a>, second paragraph, citing Chrysostom, Hom. 23 on Romans. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the sentence behind Lecky’s note.</p>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>… nec immeritò: ab ipso enim generis humani exordio, <span class="cited-passage">Reges parentum loco habitos, vel illud Regibus Palæstinis commune Abimelechi nomen ostendit. Abimelech enim Latinè vertitur, <em>Pater meus Rex</em></span>; quæ nota &amp; obvia argumentis confirmare non est animus.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>… and not undeservedly: for from the very beginning of the human race, <span class="cited-passage">kings were held in the place of parents, as even the name Abimelech, common to the kings of Palestine, shows. For Abimelech is translated in Latin as <em>My father the king</em></span>; these well-known and obvious things I do not intend to confirm by arguments.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Lecky’s English: “‘Abimelech,’ which was a name originally common to all the kings of Palestine, signifies ‘My father king’”. “Originally” is Lecky’s; Bossuet says only “common”. Lecky’s “lib. i. c. 3” is not this page’s chapter (see the status note above).</p>
</section>
