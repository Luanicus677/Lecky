abstract: |
  <p>Did Constantine forbid the pagan sacrifices? Lecky says the prohibition “is expressly and unequivocally asserted by both Eusebius, Sozomen, and Theodoret”, and refers to Milman for Eusebius’s repeated claims. Milman’s pages are more cautious than that: Eusebius’s “strong assertions” are confirmed “to a certain extent” by a law of Constantine’s son, but the same Eusebius copies out letters in Constantine’s own hand that promise the pagans “the most impartial toleration”: “Let all enjoy the same peace … let those who withhold their obedience from Thee have their temples of falsehood if they think right.”</p>
  <p>Milman’s footnote prints the law of Constans (<em>Codex Theodosianus</em> XVI.10.2, “Cesset superstitio, sacrificiorum aboleatur insania”), which Lecky quotes in his note 21.</p>

<section id="donatists" class="passage">
<h2>The Donatists and the Circumcellions (vol. II, pp. 372–375)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: London 1840, vol. II, ch. I, p. 375, <a href="https://archive.org/details/historyofchrist02milm/page/n394/mode/2up">scan n394</a>; the account of the schism begins on p. 372 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/historyofchrist02milm/page/n391/mode/2up">n391</a>).</p>
<blockquote>
<p>… duty of slaying by thousands the renegades, or the unbelievers. In vain Constantine at length published an edict of peace: the afflicted province was rent asunder till the close of his reign, and during that of his son, by this religious warfare. <span class="cited-passage">For, on the other hand, the barbarous fanaticism of the Circumcellions involved the Donatist party in the guilt of insurrection, and connected them with revolting atrocities, which they were accused of countenancing, of exciting, if not actually sanctioning by their presence.</span> That which in the opulent cities, or the well-ordered communities, led to fierce and irreconcileable contention, grew up among the wild borderers on civilisation into fanatical frenzy.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky’s note 14 supports his sentence that “any blood that was at this time shed seems to have been due to the excessive turbulence of the Circumcelliones”. Milman’s margin dates Constantine’s edict of peace to A.D. 321.</p>
</section>

<section id="eusebius-sacrifice" class="passage">
<h2>Eusebius’s “strong assertions” and Constantine’s own toleration (vol. II, pp. 460–464)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: London 1840, vol. II, ch. IV, p. 461, <a href="https://archive.org/details/historyofchrist02milm/page/n480/mode/2up">scan n480</a>, with Milman’s footnote.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/milman-1840-v2-p461.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/milman-1840-v2-p461.jpg" alt="Printed page: “These strong assertions of Eusebius are, to a certain extent, confirmed by expressions in the laws of his successors … On the other hand, Eusebius himself inserts … documents … proclaiming the most impartial toleration to the Pagans”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Milman, 1840, vol. II, p. 461. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>… who forced the priests to surrender up the statues of their gods, which, having been despoiled of their ornaments, were melted or destroyed. <span class="cited-passage">These strong assertions of Eusebius are, to a certain extent, confirmed by expressions in the laws of his successors, especially one of Constans, which appeals to an edict of his father Constantine, which prohibited sacrifice.</span>*</p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">On the other hand, Eusebius himself inserts, and ascribes to a date posterior to some of these laws, documents, which he professes to have seen in Constantine’s own hand, proclaiming the most impartial toleration to the Pagans, and deprecating compulsion in religious matters.</span> “Let all enjoy the same peace; let no one disturb another in his religious worship; let each act as he thinks fit; let those who withhold their obedience from Thee (it is an address to the Deity), have their temples of falsehood if they think right.” He exhorts to mutual charity, and declares, “It is a very different thing willingly to submit to trials for the sake of immortal life, and to force others by penalties to embrace one faith.” These generous sentiments, if Constantine was issuing edicts to close the temples, and prohi[bit] …</p>
<p class="note">* Cesset superstitio, sacrificiorum aboleatur insania. Nam quicunque contra legem divi Principis, parentis nostri, et hanc nostræ mansuetudinis jussionem ausus fuerit sacrificia celebrare, competens in eum vindicta, et præsens sententia exseratur. Cod. Theodos. xvi. 10. 2.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Milman’s footnote translates: “Let superstition cease; let the madness of sacrifices be abolished. For whoever, against the law of the deified emperor our father and this command of our clemency, dares to celebrate sacrifices, let fitting punishment and immediate sentence be executed upon him.” Lecky quotes the same law in his note 21. For Constantine’s law on the temples see also the <a href="codex-theodosianus-xvi-jews-and-temples.html"><em>Codex Theodosianus</em> XVI page</a>.</p>
</section>
