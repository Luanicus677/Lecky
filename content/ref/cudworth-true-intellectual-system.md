abstract: |
  <p>Cudworth was a philosopher of reason, not a credulous man. Yet in a chapter on the phenomena that “confute atheism”, he lists apparitions, witches and demoniacs as proofs that spirits exist, and says that those who now “explode” witches “can hardly escape the suspicion of having some hankering towards atheism”. Lecky quotes the sentence accurately except for a dropped phrase (“in a peculiar manner”). Cudworth’s very next sentence raises a question that troubled believers: why were there so many demoniacs in Christ’s time, “and hardly any, or none, in this present age of ours”?</p>

<section id="hankering" class="passage">
<h2>“Some hankering towards atheism” (Book I, ch. V)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: London 1743, vol. II, p. 702, <a href="https://archive.org/details/trueintellectual02cudw_0/page/n75/mode/2up">scan</a>. Long s modernised. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: Lecky’s quotation.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/cudworth-1743-p702.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/cudworth-1743-p702.jpg" alt="A page of Cudworth with long s: “To this phaenomenon of apparitions might be added those two others of magicians or wizards, daemoniacks or Energumeni”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Cudworth, <i>True Intellectual System</i>, 2nd ed. (1743), vol. II, p. 702. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>To this phænomenon of apparitions might be added those two others of magicians or wizards, dæmoniacks or <i>Energumeni</i>; both of these proving also the real existence of spirits, and that they are not mere phancies, and imaginary inhabitants of men’s brains only, but real inhabitants of the world. As also, that among those spirits there are some foul, unclean, and wicked ones, (though not made such by God, but by their own apostacy) which is some confirmation of the truth of Christianity, the Scripture insisting so much upon these evil dæmons or devils, and declaring it to be one design of our Saviour Christ’s coming into the world, to oppose these confederate powers of the kingdom of darkness, and to rescue mankind from the thraldom and bondage thereof. <span class="cited-passage">As for wizards and magicians, persons who associate and confederate themselves</span> in a peculiar manner <span class="cited-passage">with these evil spirits, for the gratification of their own revenge, lust, ambition, and other passions; besides the Scriptures, there hath been so full an attestation given to them by persons unconcerned in all ages, that those our so confident exploders of them, in this present age, can hardly escape the suspicion of having some hankering towards atheism.</span> But as for the dæmoniacks and <i>Energumeni</i>, it hath been wondered, that there should be so many of them in our Saviour’s time, and hardly any, or none, in this present age of ours.</p>
</blockquote>
</section>

<section id="psellus-bodies" class="passage">
<h2>Psellus and St Basil: devils have bodies, or they could not burn (vol. II, p. 702)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: London 1743, vol. II, p. 702, in the notes, <a href="https://archive.org/details/trueintellectual02cudw_0/page/n75/mode/2up">scan n75</a>. Cudworth quotes Psellus’s dialogue <i>On the operation of demons</i> in Greek and translates it; the Greek is not transcribed here. Long <i>s</i> normalised; Cudworth’s italics kept. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky uses.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/cudworth-1743-ii-p702-psellus.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/cudworth-1743-ii-p702-psellus.jpg" alt="Printed English: “But you are to know, that dæmons or devils are not altogether incorporeal … the divine Basil contending, that there are bodies, not only in devils, but also in the pure angels themselves … that they shall be punished with fire; which (saith he) were a thing impossible, were they all of them incorporeal.”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Cudworth, <em>True Intellectual System</em>, 1743, vol. II, p. 702. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>[Thus Psellus in his Dialogue …] <i>But you are to know, that <span class="cited-passage">dæmons or devils are not altogether incorporeal</span>, but that they are joined to bodies, and so converse with bodies; which may be learned also from the fathers, <span class="cited-passage">the divine</span></i> <span class="cited-passage">Basil</span> <i><span class="cited-passage">contending, that there are bodies, not only in devils, but also in the pure angels themselves</span>, as certain subtile, airy, defecate spirits.</i> Where afterwards he shows, how the σύμφυες ἀγγέλοις σῶμα, that <i>body which is connate with angels</i>, differs from that, which devils are united to, in respect of the radiant splendour of the one, and the dark fuliginous obscurity of the other. <span class="cited-passage">Moreover, that devils are not without bodies, he endeavours further to confirm from the words of our Saviour, that they shall be <i>punished with fire</i>; which (saith he) were a thing impossible, were they all of them incorporeal.</span></p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (ch. I, text and note 48): Psellus “lays it down as a fundamental position that all demons have bodies. This, he says, is the necessary inference from the orthodox doctrine that they endure the torment of fire”; and in the note, “This was a very old notion. St. Basil seems to have maintained it very strongly. Cudworth’s Int. System, vol. ii. p. 648.” Both points are on this page of the 1743 edition; Lecky’s page number belongs to another edition, as with his note 127.</p>
</section>
