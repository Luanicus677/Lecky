abstract: |
  <p>Lecky quotes only the second half of the canon, “ne quod colitur et adoratur in parietibus depingatur”, and adds that Catholics explain it as a measure “elicited by the persecution” to keep Christian images from pagan profanation. Hefele, himself a Catholic bishop and historian, gives two other Catholic readings that limit the ban: Binterim thought it forbade only images hung up at anyone’s fancy, Aubespine only images of God (because the canon says <i>adoratur</i>). Hefele rejects both: the canon also says <i>colitur</i>, “and the prohibition is conceived in very general terms”.</p>

<p><i>Canon 36. That there be no pictures in church.</i></p>

</div>
</div>
<p>Hefele’s comment (p. 151):</p>
<blockquote>
<p>These canons are easy to understand: we have elsewhere explained why the ancient Church did not tolerate images. Binterim and Aubespine do not believe in a complete exclusion: they think that the Church in general, and the Synod of Elvira in particular, wished to proscribe only a certain kind of images. Binterim believes that this Synod forbade only one thing,—namely, that any one might hang images in the Church according to his <i>fancy</i>, and often therefore inadmissible ones. Aubespine thinks that our canon forbids only images representing God (because it says <i>adoratur</i>), and not other pictures, especially those of saints. But the canon also says <i>colitur</i>, and the prohibition is conceived in very general terms.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Neither of the readings Hefele reports is the one Lecky attributes to “the Catholics” (a measure against pagan profanation in time of persecution); that explanation is not on this page.</p>
