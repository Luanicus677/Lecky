abstract: |
  <p>In 357 and 358 the emperor <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantius_II">Constantius II</a> issued a series of laws against divination. Lecky treats them as the point where the Christian empire began to turn the old Roman laws against magic against paganism itself, and quotes two of them in Latin: law 4, a general ban on consulting diviners on pain of death, and law 6, which allows torture of anyone caught practising divination in the imperial retinue.</p><p>Lecky’s Latin agrees with the 1738 text in every word. The only differences are in punctuation. See <a href="#lecky-quotation">the comparison below</a>.</p>

<section id="law-4" class="passage">
<h2>Law 4: Divination Forbidden (357)</h2>

<p class="passage-source">Latin: Leipzig, 1738, vol. III, p. 128 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/BRes1409273/page/n143/mode/2up">scan</a>). Spelling regularised: <em>u</em>/<em>v</em> and <em>i</em>/<em>j</em> normalised and capitals reduced; the editors’ footnote letters omitted. The edition’s heading reads “De divinandi curiositate omni prohibita, Constantii lex”; the law is addressed to the people of Rome. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the words Lecky quotes.</p>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p><em>Imp. Constantius A. et Iulian. Caes. ad populum.</em></p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">Nemo haruspicem consulat aut mathematicum, nemo hariolum. Augurum et vatum prava confessio conticescat. Chaldaei ac magi et ceteri, quos maleficos ob facinorum magnitudinem vulgus appellat, nec ad hanc partem aliquid moliantur. Sileat omnibus perpetuo divinandi curiositas: etenim supplicium capitis feret gladio ultore prostratus quicunque iussis obsequium denegaverit.</span> Dat. VIII. Kalendas Febr. Mediol. Constantio A. IX. et Iuliano Caes. II. Coss.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p><em>The Emperor Constantius Augustus, and Julian Caesar, to the people.</em></p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">Let no one consult a haruspex, or an astrologer; let no one consult a soothsayer. Let the wicked utterance of augurs and seers fall silent. Let the Chaldeans and magi and the rest, whom the common people call “<em>malefici</em>” (evildoers) for the magnitude of their crimes, attempt nothing in this direction either. Let the curiosity to divine be silent for all, for ever; for whoever refuses obedience to these commands shall be laid low by the avenging sword and suffer the penalty of death.</span> Given on the eighth day before the Kalends of February, at Milan, in the ninth consulship of Constantius Augustus and the second of Julian Caesar.</p>
</div>
</div>

<details class="collapse">
<summary>Date, and the edition’s notes on the text</summary>
<p>The 1738 edition prints the date as 357. By Roman reckoning the “eighth day before the Kalends of February” is 25 January; this conversion is this edition’s own, not the book’s. The edition’s apparatus notes two variants in this law: a manuscript it calls “G” reads “<em lang="la">hariolum, aut augurem, aut vatem</em>” for the sentence about augurs, and the editor notes that Tribonian’s version of the law (in the Justinian Code) reads “<em lang="la">supplicio capitis feretur</em>” where this text has <em lang="la">supplicium capitis feret</em>, though he judges the printed reading to be the lawful one. Lecky follows the printed reading.</p>
</details>
</section>

<section id="law-6" class="passage">
<h2>Law 6: Torture for Diviners in the Emperor’s Retinue (358)</h2>

<p class="passage-source">Latin: Leipzig, 1738, vol. III, pp. 133–134 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/BRes1409273/page/n148/mode/2up">scan</a>), conventions as above. The heading reads “De divinandi curiositate omni in comitatu suo et Iuliani C. prohibita Constantii lex” and the law is addressed to Taurus, praetorian prefect. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the words Lecky quotes (the end of the law).</p>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p><em>Idem A. ad Taurum P. P.</em></p>
<p>Etsi excepta tormentis sunt corpora honoribus praeditorum (praeter illa videlicet crimina, quae legibus demonstrantur), etsi omnes magi, in quacunque sint parte terrarum, humani generis inimici credendi sunt, tamen quoniam qui in comitatu nostro sunt, ipsam pulsent propemodum maiestatem, si quis magus, vel magicis contaminibus adsuetus (qui maleficus vulgi consuetudine nuncupatur), aut haruspex, aut hariolus, aut certe augur, vel et mathematicus, aut narrandis somniis occultans artem aliquam divinandi, aut certe aliquid horum simile exercens, in comitatu meo, vel Caesaris, fuerit deprehensus, praesidio dignitatis cruciatus et tormenta non fugiat. <span class="cited-passage">Si convictus ad proprium facinus detegentibus repugnaverit pernegando, sit eculeo deditus, ungulisque sulcantibus latera perferat poenas proprio dignas facinore.</span> Dat. III. Non. Iul. Arimini, Daciano et Cereale Coss.</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p><em>The same Augustus, to Taurus, praetorian prefect.</em></p>
<p>Although the persons of those endowed with honours are exempt from torture (except, that is, for the crimes the laws specify), and although all magi, in whatever part of the world they may be, are to be reckoned enemies of the human race, nevertheless, since those who are in our retinue all but strike at majesty itself: if any magus, or anyone accustomed to magical spells (whom common usage calls a <em>maleficus</em>), or a haruspex, or a soothsayer, or an augur, or an astrologer as well, or anyone who conceals some art of divination in the telling of dreams, or who practises anything like these, is caught in my retinue or the Caesar’s, let the protection of his rank not save him from racking and torment. <span class="cited-passage">If, though convicted, he persists in flatly denying his own crime against those who expose it, let him be handed over to the rack, and let him endure, with his sides furrowed by the claws, punishments worthy of his own crime.</span> Given on the third day before the Nones of July, at Rimini, in the consulship of Datianus and Cerealis.</p>
</div>
</div>

<details class="collapse">
<summary>The rack and the claws; the date</summary>
<p>The <em lang="la">eculeus</em> (literally “little horse”) was a rack on which the victim was stretched; the <em lang="la">ungulae</em> were iron claws used to tear the flesh of the sides. Gothofredus and Ritter print notes on both on p. 134, immediately after the law; these are the “Commentary on the law” to which Lecky’s note directs the reader, along with Beugnot. The edition dates the law 358; “the third day before the Nones of July” is 5 July by Roman reckoning, this edition’s own conversion. A note in the edition records that Tribonian’s version of the law (in the Justinian Code) reads <em lang="la">si vero convictus fuerit</em> (“but if he is convicted”) here; the editor prefers <em lang="la">nam si convictus</em> or <em lang="la">igitur si convictus</em>. Lecky’s text, like the one printed here, has <em lang="la">si convictus</em>.</p>
</details>
</section>
