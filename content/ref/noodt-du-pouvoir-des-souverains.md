abstract: |
  <p>Lecky recommends two Leiden lectures, “Noodt On the Power of Sovereigns, and Gronovius On the Royal Law, both of which were translated into French by Barbeyrac — the first in 1707, and the second in 1714”, and then calls them “rather dismal performances”. The title page of Barbeyrac’s second edition bears out the publishing history: printed at Amsterdam in 1714, it is “revised and enlarged with several notes, as also with the Discourse of Jean Frederic Gronovius on the Royal Law”, so Gronovius indeed joined Noodt only in 1714. (The date of the first edition is not on this page.)</p>
  <p>The copy scanned belonged to John Adams, second President of the United States. Its title page is inscribed “Bôt of Jer. Gridley”, that is, bought from the library of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremiah_Gridley">Jeremiah Gridley</a>, the Boston lawyer who sponsored the young Adams at the bar (so Wikipedia; the inscription and ownership are from the Boston Public Library’s catalogue record). The allegorical frontispiece includes a book labelled “De la Loi Roiale”.</p>

<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. V, note 120) adds that both were “lectures delivered near the end of the seventeenth century before the University of Leyden”. Gronovius died in 1671 (Wikipedia), so his lecture at least is earlier than “near the end” of the century; the title page gives no dates for the lectures. Lecky also notes that Noodt attacked “the theological superstitions about usury”; see the usury pages from <a href="../rationalism/vol2-ch6.html">Chapter VI</a>.</p>
