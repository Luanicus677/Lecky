abstract: |
  <p>Lecky says it was “early remarked” that in Holland, while religious liberty was unusually great, “the power accorded to the clergy was unusually small”, and cites Temple. Temple’s chapter on religion gives four reasons why the Dutch settled on the Reformed faith, and the last two are political: by it “all Rights and Jurisdiction of the Clergy or Hierarchy being suppressed, There was no Ecclesiastical Authority left to rise up and trouble or fetter the Civil Power”; and in Holland the clergy, unlike everywhere else in Christendom, had never been one of the Estates, so the province chose the profession “which gave least pretence of Power or Jurisdiction to the Clergy”.</p>
  <p>The toleration follows directly. “The great Care of this State has ever been, To favour no particular or curious Inquisition into the Faith or Religious Principles of any peaceable man, who came to live under the protection of their Laws, And to suffer no Violence or Oppression upon any Mans Conscience.” Only the Catholics were excepted, as subjects of a foreign jurisdiction; and even they, Temple adds, worshipped freely by the connivance of officers paid a regular sum by every family.</p>

<h2>“Least pretence of Power or Jurisdiction to the Clergy” (ch. V, pp. 174–176)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: London 1673, ch. V “Of their Religion”, pp. 174–176, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_observations-upon-the-un_temple-sir-william_1673/page/n207/mode/2up">scan n207</a>–n209. Long s modernised; Temple’s spelling kept. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the point Lecky draws from Temple.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/temple-united-provinces-1673-p175.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/temple-united-provinces-1673-p175.jpg" alt="Printed page 175: “There might perhaps be added one Reason more, which was particular to one of the Provinces: For whereas in most, if not all other parts of Christendom, the Clergy composed one of the Three Estates of the Countrey … That Order never made any part of the Estates in Holland … which gave least pretence of Power or Jurisdiction to the Clergy … Since this Establishment, as well as before, the great Care of this State has ever been, To favour no particular or curious Inquisition into the Faith or Religious Principles of any peaceable man …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Temple, <em>Observations upon the United Provinces</em>, 1673, p. 175. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (EEBO microfilm) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>[p. 174] … And lastly, Because by this Profession, <span class="cited-passage">all Rights and Jurisdiction of the Clergy or Hierarchy being suppressed, There was no Ecclesiastical Authority left to rise up and trouble or fetter the Civil Power</span>; And all the Goods and Possessions of Churches and Abbies, were seized wholly into the hands of the State, [p. 175] which made a great encrease of the publique Revenue, A thing the most necessary for the support of their Government.</p>
<p>There might perhaps be added one Reason more, which was particular to one of the Provinces: For whereas in most, if not all other parts of Christendom, the Clergy composed one of the Three Estates of the Countrey, And thereby shar’d with the Nobles and Commons in their Influences upon the Government; <span class="cited-passage">That Order never made any part of the Estates in <i>Holland</i>, nor had any Vote in their Assembly</span>, which consisted only of the Nobles and the Cities, and this Province bearing always the greatest sway in the Councils of the Union, was most enclined to the settlement of that Profession, <span class="cited-passage">which gave least pretence of Power or Jurisdiction to the Clergy</span>, and so agreed most with their own ancient Constitutions.</p>
<p>Since this Establishment, as well as before, <span class="cited-passage">the great Care of this State has ever been, To favour no particular or curious Inquisition into the Faith or Religious Principles of any peaceable man, who came to live under the protection of their Laws, And to suffer [p. 176] no Violence or Oppression upon any Mans Conscience</span>; whose Opinions broke not out into Expressions or Actions of ill consequence to the State. A free Form of Government either making way for more freedom in Religion, Or else having newly contended so far themselves for Liberty in this point, they thought it the more unreasonable for them to oppress others. …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. IV, text at note 54) puts Temple’s two observations together, liberty “unusually great” and clerical power “unusually small”. Temple connects them less directly: he gives the weakness of the clergy as a reason for the choice of the Reformed religion, and the toleration as the policy of a free state recently at war for its own liberty.</p>
