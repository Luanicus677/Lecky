  <p>Lecky’s two quotations are accurate, with small changes. In the first, Hallam writes “ought not to be burned alive, or otherwise put to death”, and “Thus, at the end of the sixteenth century”; Lecky has “should not be burned alive or otherwise put to death” and drops “Thus”. In the second, the changes are punctuation only. The context matters: Hallam is speaking of the 1590s (the first sentence follows his notice of Koornhert, who wrote against the death penalty for heresy) and of the Reformation period generally (the second).</p>

<section id="simple-proposition" class="passage">
<h2>“The simple proposition” (<em>Literature of Europe</em>, ch. II, p. 116)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: 1837 ed., vol. 2, p. 116, <a href="https://archive.org/details/introtolitofeur02halliala/page/n137/mode/2up">scan</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: Lecky’s quotation.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>… known by a treatise against capital punishment for heresy, published in Latin after his death. It is extremely scarce, and I have met with no author, except Bayle and Brandt, who speaks of it from direct knowledge.* <span class="cited-passage">Thus, at the end of the sixteenth century, the simple proposition, that men for holding or declaring heterodox opinions in religion ought not to be burned alive, or otherwise put to death, was itself little else than a sort of heterodoxy; and, though many privately must have been persuaded of its truth, the Protestant churches were as far from acknowledging it as that of Rome. No one had yet pretended to assert the general right of religious worship, which, in fact, was rarely or never conceded to the Romanists in a Protestant country, though the Huguenots shed oceans of blood to secure the same privilege for themselves.</span></p>
<p><small>* Bayle, Biogr. Univ. Brandt, Hist. de la Reformation des Provinces Unies, i. 435. …</small></p>
</blockquote>
<p>The page read here begins mid-sentence, with the end of an account of a man “known by a treatise against capital punishment for heresy”; Hallam’s footnote names Koornhert and Brandt’s history, so I take it to be Koornhert, but the previous page was not read. Collation with Lecky: “ought not” → “should not”; “Thus, at the end” → “At the end”; punctuation; otherwise identical.</p>
</section>

<section id="deadly-original-sin" class="passage">
<h2>“Persecution is the deadly original sin” (<em>Constitutional History</em>, ch. II)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: Gutenberg 39711, vol. 1, chapter II (the reign of Edward VI and the confiscation of church property).</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Tolerance in religion, it is well known, so unanimously admitted (at least verbally) even by theologians in the present century, was seldom considered as practicable, much less as a matter of right, during the period of the Reformation. The difference in this respect between the Catholics and Protestants was only in degree, and in degree there was much less difference than we are apt to believe. <span class="cited-passage">Persecution is the deadly original sin of the reformed churches; that which cools every honest man’s zeal for their cause, in proportion as his reading becomes more extensive.</span> …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky: “Persecution is the deadly original sin of the Reformed churches, that which cools every honest man’s zeal for their cause in proportion as his reading becomes more extensive.” Same words; Lecky capitalises “Reformed” and uses commas for Hallam’s semicolon and comma.</p>
</section>
