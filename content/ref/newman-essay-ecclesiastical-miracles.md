abstract: |
  <p>Lecky calls Newman’s essay “essentially an apology for the ecclesiastical miracles”, the miracles reported by the Fathers and the lives of saints after the age of the Apostles. Its second section, “On the Antecedent Probability of the Ecclesiastical Miracles”, opens the case in so many words: “there is no presumption whatever against miracles <i>generally</i> in the ages after the Apostles, though there may be and is a certain antecedent improbability in <i>particular</i> miracles.” The reason is Scripture itself: inspiration, by establishing its own miracles “in fact, has disproved their impossibility in the abstract.” Newman wrote it in 1842, three years before he became a Catholic; Lecky’s remark on the “miracles of the English saints” refers to the later <em>Lives of the English Saints</em> and is not checked here.</p>

<h2>“On the antecedent probability of the ecclesiastical miracles” (Section II, p. xv)</h2>

<blockquote>
<p><span class="cited-passage">Now it may be asserted that there is no presumption whatever against miracles <i>generally</i> in the ages after the Apostles, though there may be and is a certain antecedent improbability in <i>particular</i> miracles.</span></p>
<p>There is no presumption against Ecclesiastical miracles generally, because inspiration has stood the brunt of any such antecedent objection, whatever it be worth, by its own supernatural histories, and in establishing their certainty in fact, has disproved their impossibility in the abstract. …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (ch. II, note 16): “Dr. Newman’s very able essay (prefixed to Fleury’s History) is essentially an apology for the ecclesiastical miracles”. The opening of its argument bears that out.</p>
