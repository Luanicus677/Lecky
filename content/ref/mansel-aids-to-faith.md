abstract: |
  <p><em>Aids to Faith</em> was the Church of England’s answer to <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essays_and_Reviews"><em>Essays and Reviews</em></a> (1860), and Lecky cites its first two essays as the voice of the “small body of theologians” still defending Christianity by its miracles. Mansel’s argument is the one Lecky summarises: a man who throws a stone sets material laws working without breaking any of them, and no law of matter explains why the stone was thrown at all. Admit free will in man, says Mansel, and you have “the experience of a power, analogous, however inferior,” to the one that works a miracle; deny it, and nothing in religion “is worth contending about”.</p>
  <p>Lecky says these writers “complain bitterly” that the old evidential method has been abandoned. The second essay opens with exactly the man Lecky blames for that, Coleridge (“Evidences of Christianity! … I am weary of the word”), and concedes “a general distaste” for the apologetic literature of the last century. But its tone is regretful, not bitter: Bishop Fitzgerald explains the distaste as a natural reaction against an earlier generation’s excess. Mansel’s own complaint (p. 30) is narrower, aimed at the attempt in <em>Essays and Reviews</em> “to disparage the value of the miracles as evidences”.</p>

<section id="free-will" class="passage">
<h2>Mansel: free will as a precedent for miracle (pp. 19–20)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Essay I, “On Miracles as Evidences of Christianity”, pp. 19–20, <a href="https://archive.org/details/a609561000thomuoft/page/n30/mode/2up">scan n30</a>–<a href="https://archive.org/details/a609561000thomuoft/page/n31/mode/2up">n31</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the argument Lecky summarises at note 23.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/aids-to-faith-1861-p19-mansel.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/aids-to-faith-1861-p19-mansel.jpg" alt="Printed page 19: “Deny the existence of a free will in man; and neither the possibility of miracles, nor any other question of religion or morality, is worth contending about. Admit the existence of a free will in man; and we have the experience of a power, analogous, however inferior, to that which is supposed to operate in the production of a miracle …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Mansel, <em>Aids to Faith</em>, 1861, p. 19. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/aids-to-faith-1861-p20-mansel.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/aids-to-faith-1861-p20-mansel.jpg" alt="Printed page 20: “… Such interferences, whether produced by human or by superhuman will, are not contrary to the laws of matter; but neither are they the result of those laws. … If a man, of his own free will, throws a stone into the air …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Mansel, p. 20. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>The most rigid prevalence of law and necessary sequence among purely material phenomena may be admitted without apprehension by the firmest believer in miracles, so long as that sequence is so interpreted as to leave room for a power indispensable to all moral obligation and to all religious belief—the power of Free Will in man. Deny the existence of a free will in man; and neither the possibility of miracles, nor any other question of religion or morality, is worth contending about. <span class="cited-passage">Admit the existence of a free will in man; and we have the experience of a power, analogous, however inferior, to that which is supposed to operate in the production of a miracle, and forming the basis of a legitimate argument from the less to the greater.</span> In the Will of man we have the solitary instance of an Efficient Cause in the highest sense of the term, acting among and along with the physical causes of the material world, and producing results which would not have been brought about by any invariable sequence of physical causes left to their own action. We have evidence, also, of an <i>elasticity</i>, so to speak, in the constitution of nature, which [p. 20] permits the influence of human power on the phenomena of the world to be exercised or suspended at will, without affecting the stability of the whole. <span class="cited-passage">We have thus a precedent for allowing the possibility of a similar interference of a higher will on a grander scale</span>, provided for by a similar elasticity of the matter subjected to its influence. Such interferences, whether produced by human or by superhuman will, are not contrary to the laws of matter; but neither are they the result of those laws. They are the work of an agent who is independent of the laws, and who, therefore, neither obeys them nor disobeys them. If a man, of his own free will, throws a stone into the air, the motion of the stone, as soon as it has left his hand, is determined by a combination of purely material laws; partly by the attraction of the earth; partly by the resistance of the air; partly by the magnitude and direction of the force by which it was thrown. But by what <i>law</i> came it to be thrown at all? What law brought about the circumstance through which the aforesaid combination of material laws came into operation on this particular occasion and in this particular manner? …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (ch. II, text at note 23): “The idea of interference can present no difficulty to anyone who admits human liberty; for those who acknowledge that liberty must hold that man has a certain power of guiding and controlling the laws of matter …” That is a fair summary of Mansel. Lecky’s preceding sentence, that miracles are “performed simply by the application of natural means guided by supernatural knowledge”, is not Mansel’s language: Mansel says such interferences are “not the result” of material laws but the work of an agent “independent of the laws”. Lecky’s sentence covers a wider school (note 24 adds the <em>Edinburgh Review</em>; see <a class="popup-ref" href="edinburgh-review-the-supernatural.html">that page</a>).</p>
<p>A footnote on p. 19 answers “an attempt … recently made to prove the non-existence of free will, by means of statistical calculations”: averages hold of masses, Mansel replies, not of individuals. He names no one; the target is presumably Buckle’s <em>History of Civilization</em> (1857), whose statistical argument against free will was then notorious. That identification is ours.</p>
</section>

<h2>Mansel: miracles “disparaged” as evidences (p. 30)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Essay I, § 25, p. 30, <a href="https://archive.org/details/a609561000thomuoft/page/n41/mode/2up">scan n41</a>. Mansel’s footnote points to “Essays and Reviews, p. 143”.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/aids-to-faith-1861-p30-mansel.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/aids-to-faith-1861-p30-mansel.jpg" alt="Printed page 30: “25. With the question of the possibility of miracles is intimately connected that of their value as evidences. … we find it coupled with an attempt to disparage the value of the miracles as evidences, even supposing their reality.”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Mansel, p. 30. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>25. With the question of the <i>possibility</i> of miracles is intimately connected that of their <i>value as evidences</i>. … Nevertheless, as if the conclusiveness of the former argument were, after all, somewhat doubtful in the eyes of its advocates, we find it coupled with <span class="cited-passage">an attempt to disparage the value of the miracles as evidences, even supposing their reality</span>. It is intimated that they are not so much <i>evidences</i> as <i>objects</i> of faith, invested with sanctity and exempted from criticism by virtue of the religious mysteries with which they are connected …</p>
</blockquote>

<section id="weary-of-the-word" class="passage">
<h2>Fitzgerald: “a general distaste” for the evidences (p. 43)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Essay II, “On the Study of the Evidences of Christianity”, § 1, p. 43, <a href="https://archive.org/details/a609561000thomuoft/page/n54/mode/2up">scan n54</a>.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/aids-to-faith-1861-p43-fitzgerald.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/aids-to-faith-1861-p43-fitzgerald.jpg" alt="Printed page 43: “ON THE STUDY OF THE EVIDENCES OF CHRISTIANITY. 1. ‘Evidences of Christianity!’ exclaims the late Mr. Coleridge … ‘I am weary of the word. …’ There can be little doubt, I think, that these words express the prevailing sentiments of a very considerable number of Christians at the present day …”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Fitzgerald, <em>Aids to Faith</em>, 1861, p. 43. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>1. “<span class="sc">Evidences</span> of Christianity!” exclaims the late Mr. Coleridge in one of the most popular of his prose-works; “I am weary of the word. Make a man feel the want of it … and you may safely trust it to its own evidence.”</p>
<p>There can be little doubt, I think, that these words express the prevailing sentiments of a very considerable number of Christians at the present day; and it cannot be denied that, for many years back, <span class="cited-passage">there has been a general distaste for that <i>apologetic</i> religious literature which was popular in the last century</span>.</p>
<p>2. This has doubtless been greatly owing to a Reaction from the disproportionate attention paid to such literature by the Divines of a former age, and has taken place in virtue of that general rule which seems to ordain that an over value of any branch of knowledge in one generation shall be attended by an unjust depreciation of it in the next. …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (ch. II, text at note 22): the defenders of the evidential method “complain bitterly that they are labouring in a wilderness, and that the old method has been on all sides abandoned and neglected”. Fitzgerald supports the fact (“a general distaste”, “an unjust depreciation”) but not the bitterness; and he opens with the very sentence of Coleridge that Lecky, a paragraph earlier, credits with bringing the method “into complete disrepute”. (Fitzgerald does not name Coleridge’s book; the dots in the quotation are his.)</p>
</section>
