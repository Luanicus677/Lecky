abstract: |
  <p>Lecky argues in Chapter III that the early Fathers thought of the soul as material, and cites Tertullian: “Corporalitas animæ in ipso evangelio relucebit. Dolet apud inferos anima cujusdam, et punitur in flammâ et cruciatur in linguâ et de digito animæ felicioris implorat solatium roris.” These are the first sentences of chapter 7 of <em>De anima</em>, where Tertullian turns from the philosophers to the Christians’ own proof, the story of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16).</p><p>Lecky’s Latin is the passage with one word dropped and one added: he omits “quibus” (“to whom”, joining the sentence to the previous one) and has an extra “et” before “punitur”. The sense is unchanged. See <a href="#comparison">the comparison</a>.</p>

<section id="ch7" class="passage">
<h2>Chapter 7: The Corporeality of the Soul in the Gospel</h2>

<p class="passage-source">Latin: Migne, <em>PL</em> 2, col. 656 (right column, foot, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_patrologiae-cursus-completus-_1844_2/page/n329/mode/2up">scan</a>), <em>De anima</em> VII. Spelling regularised. The second line is the start of a new chapter (“Caput VII”). <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the words Lecky quotes.</p>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>Quantum ad philosophos, satis haec; quia quantum ad nostros, ex abundanti, quibus <span class="cited-passage">corporalitas animae in ipso Evangelio relucebit. Dolet apud inferos anima cuiusdam, punitur in flamma, et cruciatur in lingua, et de digito animae felicioris implorat solatium roris.</span> Imaginem existimas exitum illum pauperis laetantis …</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>So much for the philosophers; as for our own people, more than enough, to whom <span class="cited-passage">the corporeality of the soul will shine out in the Gospel itself. The soul of a certain man grieves in the underworld, is punished in the flame, and is tormented in the tongue, and begs for the solace of a drop of dew from the finger of a happier soul.</span> Do you suppose that the departure of that poor man rejoicing was only an image …</p>
</div>
</div>

<details class="collapse">
<summary>Doubtful points</summary>
<p>“Imaginem existimas exitum illum pauperis laetantis” is cut by the page edge; I give its start only. “Ex abundanti” I take as “over and above”. Lecky’s reference is “cap. vii.”, which is right for this edition. The surrounding argument (that the soul’s torment in the tongue requires a body) is Tertullian’s own, but I have only read these lines.</p>
</details>
</section>

<section id="comparison" class="passage">
<h2>Lecky’s Quotation Compared</h2>

<div class="table-wrap">
<table class="collation">
<thead><tr><th>Lecky, 1865</th><th>Migne</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td lang="la">‘Corporalitas animæ in ipso evangelio relucebit. Dolet apud inferos anima cujusdam <strong>et</strong> punitur in flammâ et cruciatur in linguâ et de digito animæ felicioris implorat solatium roris.’</td><td lang="la">… quibus corporalitas animae in ipso Evangelio relucebit. Dolet apud inferos anima cuiusdam, punitur in flamma, et cruciatur in lingua, et de digito animae felicioris implorat solatium roris.</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>

<p>Lecky starts the sentence at “Corporalitas”, omitting the relative “quibus” (so his version reads as an independent statement), and has “et punitur” where Migne has an asyndeton, “punitur”. His “flammâ”, “linguâ” are the ablative circumflexes of older printings. Otherwise the words are identical.</p>
</section>
