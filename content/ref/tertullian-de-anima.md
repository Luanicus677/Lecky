abstract: |
  <p>Lecky’s quotation is almost exact: set against Migne’s text it drops “quibus” and adds “et” before “punitur”, leaving the sense unchanged. Tertullian is arguing that the soul is corporeal, and takes his proof from the Gospel story of the rich man and Lazarus. The soul of a certain man “grieves in the underworld, is punished in the flame, and is tormented in the tongue, and begs for the solace of a drop of dew from the finger of a happier soul.”</p>
  <p>Lecky’s woman “who had seen a soul” is in chapter 9: a sister in Tertullian’s congregation, gifted with revelations, who fell into ecstasy during the Sunday service. The soul was shown to her “bodily”, “tender, and lucid, and of the colour of air, and in form human in every respect”. Tertullian adds a detail Lecky leaves out: the vision came just after he himself had been preaching on the soul.</p>

<section id="ch7" class="passage">
<h2>Chapter 7: The Corporeality of the Soul in the Gospel</h2>

<p class="passage-source">Latin: Migne, <em>PL</em> 2, col. 656 (right column, foot, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_patrologiae-cursus-completus-_1844_2/page/n329/mode/2up">scan</a>), <em>De anima</em> VII. Spelling regularised. The second line is the start of a new chapter (“Caput VII”). <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: the words Lecky quotes.</p>
<figure class="title-page"><a href="../assets/img/ref/pl2-title.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/pl2-title.jpg" alt="Title page: “Patrologiae cursus completus … Series prima … Tomus secundus”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Title page of Migne’s <em>Patrologia Latina</em>, tome II (Tertullian). <span class="credit"><a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_patrologiae-cursus-completus-_1844_2/page/n2/mode/2up">Scan: Internet Archive</a> · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>

<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>Quantum ad philosophos, satis haec; quia quantum ad nostros, ex abundanti, quibus <span class="cited-passage">corporalitas animae in ipso Evangelio relucebit. Dolet apud inferos anima cuiusdam, punitur in flamma, et cruciatur in lingua, et de digito animae felicioris implorat solatium roris.</span> Imaginem existimas exitum illum pauperis laetantis …</p>
</div>
<div class="rendering" lang="en">
<p>So much for the philosophers; as for our own people, more than enough, to whom <span class="cited-passage">the corporeality of the soul will shine out in the Gospel itself. The soul of a certain man grieves in the underworld, is punished in the flame, and is tormented in the tongue, and begs for the solace of a drop of dew from the finger of a happier soul.</span> Do you suppose that the departure of that poor man rejoicing was only an image …</p>
</div>
</div>

<details class="collapse">
<summary>Doubtful points</summary>
<p>“Imaginem existimas exitum illum pauperis laetantis” is cut by the page edge; I give its start only. “Ex abundanti” I take as “over and above”. Lecky’s reference is “cap. vii.”, which is right for this edition. The surrounding argument (that the soul’s torment in the tongue requires a body) is Tertullian’s own, but I have only read these lines.</p>
</details>
</section>

<section id="comparison" class="passage">
<h2>Lecky’s Quotation Compared</h2>

<div class="table-wrap">
<table class="collation">
<thead><tr><th>Lecky, 1865</th><th>Migne</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td lang="la">‘Corporalitas animæ in ipso evangelio relucebit. Dolet apud inferos anima cujusdam <strong>et</strong> punitur in flammâ et cruciatur in linguâ et de digito animæ felicioris implorat solatium roris.’</td><td lang="la">… quibus corporalitas animae in ipso Evangelio relucebit. Dolet apud inferos anima cuiusdam, punitur in flamma, et cruciatur in lingua, et de digito animae felicioris implorat solatium roris.</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>

<p>Lecky starts the sentence at “Corporalitas”, omitting the relative “quibus” (so his version reads as an independent statement), and has “et punitur” where Migne has an asyndeton, “punitur”. His “flammâ”, “linguâ” are the ablative circumflexes of older printings. Otherwise the words are identical.</p>
</section>

<section id="ch9" class="passage">
<h2>Chapter 9: a soul seen in a vision</h2>
<p class="passage-source">Latin: Migne, PL 2, <em>De anima</em> c. IX, cols. 659–660, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_patrologiae-cursus-completus-_1844_2/page/n331/mode/2up">scan n331</a>; the facsimile joins the foot of col. 659 to the top of col. 660. Migne’s note letters and figures omitted. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky’s text gives.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/tertullian-de-anima-9-pl2-col660.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/tertullian-de-anima-9-pl2-col660.jpg" alt="Migne columns: “Est hodie soror apud nos revelationum charismata sortita … ostensa est mihi anima corporaliter, et spiritus videbatur, sed non inanis et vacuæ qualitatis, imo quæ etiam teneri repromitteret, tenera, et lucida, et aerei coloris, et forma per omnia humana.”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Tertullian, <em>De anima</em> 9, Migne, PL 2, cols. 659–660. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<div class="parallel">
<div class="original" lang="la">
<p>Est hodie soror apud nos revelationum charismata sortita, quas in ecclesia inter dominica solemnia per ecstasin in spiritu patitur; conversatur cum angelis, aliquando etiam cum Domino, et videt et audit sacramenta, et quorumdam corda dignoscit, et medicinas desiderantibus submittit. Jam vero, prout Scripturæ leguntur, aut Psalmi canuntur, aut adlocutiones proferuntur, aut petitiones delegantur, ita inde materiæ visionibus subministrantur. Forte nescio quid de anima disserueramus, cum ea soror in spiritu esset. Post transacta solemnia, dimissa plebe, quo usu solet nobis renuntiare quæ viderit (nam et diligentissime digeruntur, ut etiam probentur): Inter cætera, inquit, <span class="cited-passage">ostensa est mihi anima corporaliter, et spiritus videbatur, sed non inanis et vacuæ qualitatis, imo quæ etiam teneri repromitteret, tenera, et lucida, et aerei coloris, et forma per omnia humana.</span></p>
</div>
<div class="rendering">
<p>There is among us today a sister who has been granted gifts of revelation, which she experiences in the spirit, in ecstasy, in church during the Sunday rites. She converses with angels, sometimes even with the Lord; she sees and hears mysteries, reads the hearts of some, and gives remedies to those who want them. And as the Scriptures are read, or psalms sung, or sermons preached, or prayers offered, so matter is supplied to her visions. It happened that we had been discoursing on the soul, I forget what, when this sister was in the spirit. After the rites were over and the people dismissed, she told us, as she usually does, what she had seen (for these things are most carefully examined, so that they may also be tested). Among other things, she said, <span class="cited-passage">a soul was shown to me in bodily form; it seemed a spirit, but not of an empty and hollow quality; rather, one that promised it could even be held: delicate, and lucid, and the colour of air, and in form human in every respect.</span></p>
<p class="editorial-note">Translation: this edition’s draft.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Lecky’s “a transparent and lucid figure in the perfect form of a man” is a free rendering of the last clause; “transparent” stands for “of the colour of air”. In his note 167 he remarks that the book was written after Tertullian became a Montanist; the sister’s ecstatic gifts are of the kind the Montanists prized.</p>
</section>

<details class="collapse editor-notes"><summary>Editor’s notes on this page</summary>
<p>Lecky argues in Chapter III that the early Fathers thought of the soul as material, and cites Tertullian: “Corporalitas animæ in ipso evangelio relucebit. Dolet apud inferos anima cujusdam, et punitur in flammâ et cruciatur in linguâ et de digito animæ felicioris implorat solatium roris.” These are the first sentences of chapter 7 of <em>De anima</em>, where Tertullian turns from the philosophers to the Christians’ own proof, the story of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16).</p><p>Lecky’s Latin is the passage with one word dropped and one added: he omits “quibus” (“to whom”, joining the sentence to the previous one) and has an extra “et” before “punitur”. The sense is unchanged. See <a href="#comparison">the comparison</a>.</p>
</details>
