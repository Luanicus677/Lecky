abstract: |
  <p>Lecky quotes Hooker as an early voice for government by consent: a prince who makes laws on his own authority, without commission from God or the people’s consent, exercises “mere tyranny”. The quotation is close to the text. What Lecky leaves out is the turn Hooker takes next. Consent, he says, can be given by others in our name, as in parliament, and it can be silent: an absolute monarch’s edict has the force of law whether his subjects like it or not, and old custom binds us though nobody ever asked our consent. Hooker is defending the established order, not challenging it; it was Locke who later turned these words into a theory of resistance.</p>

<section id="mere-tyranny" class="passage">
<h2>“It is no better than mere tyranny” (Book I, ch. X § 8)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: p. 59, <a href="https://archive.org/details/oflawsofecclesia0000hook_s0l3/page/n94/mode/2up">scan</a>. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: Lecky’s quotation.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/hooker-polity-i-p59.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/hooker-polity-i-p59.jpg" alt="A printed page headed “X. 8. Book I. 59” with the words “it is no better than mere tyranny”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Hooker, <i>Ecclesiastical Polity</i>, Book I, ch. X § 8 (p. 59 of this edition). <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>… That which we spake before concerning the power of government must here be applied unto the power of making laws whereby to govern; which power God hath over all: and by the natural law, whereunto he hath made all subject, <span class="cited-passage">the lawful power of making laws to command whole politic societies of men belongeth so properly unto the same entire societies, that for any prince or potentate of what kind soever upon earth to exercise the same of himself, and not either by express commission immediately and personally received from God, or else by authority derived at the first from their consent upon whose persons they impose laws, it is no better than mere tyranny.</span></p>
<p><span class="cited-passage">Laws they are not therefore which public approbation hath not made so.</span> But approbation not only they give who personally declare their assent by voice, sign, or act, but also when others do it in their names by right originally at the least derived from them. As in parliaments, councils, and the like assemblies, although we be not personally ourselves present, notwithstanding our assent is, by reason of others, agents there in our behalf. … As for example, when an absolute monarch commandeth his subjects that which seemeth good in his own discretion, hath not his edict the force of a law whether they approve or dislike it? Again, that which hath been received long sithence and is by custom now established, we keep as a law which we may not transgress; yet what consent was ever thereunto sought or required at our hands?</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky’s wording differs slightly: “belonging” for “belongeth”, “political” for “politic”, “not by express commission” for “not either by express commission”, and “from authority” for “by authority”.</p>
