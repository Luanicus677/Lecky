abstract: |
  <p>Lecky enjoys the irony: Elizabeth had encouraged the Scots against their queen, and in return she was “immediately favoured in reply with a long dissertation on the manifest superiority of nations to their sovereigns; which, as Camden assures us, and as we can readily believe, she received with extreme indignation.” Camden tells it in 1571. Ordered to give their reasons for deposing Mary, the Earl of Morton and his fellow commissioners “produced a large Discourse” proving “That the Scottish People are above their Kings; yea and by the Authority of Calvin, That popular Magistrates are constituted every-where to bridle the Lust of Kings”, and may imprison and depose bad ones. “This Discourse Queen Elizabeth read, (not without Indignation,) and tacitly condemned, as written in Affront and Wrong to Kings.” Camden’s “not without indignation” is Lecky’s “extreme indignation”.</p>

<section id="scots-discourse-1571" class="passage">
<h2>The Scots’ discourse against the royal power, 1571 (1688 English version, pp. 154–155)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: London 1688, Book II, pp. 154–155, <a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_the-history-of-the-most-_camden-william_1688/page/n171/mode/2up">scans n171</a>–<a href="https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_the-history-of-the-most-_camden-william_1688/page/n172/mode/2up">n172</a>; the margin note reads “A Writing of the Scots against the Regal Power.” Long <i>s</i> normalised. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky reports.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/camden-1688-pp154-155.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/camden-1688-pp154-155.jpg" alt="Printed English with the side-note “A Writing of the Scots against the Regal Power”: “For there were now come about Scottish matters … the Earl of Morton … produced a large Discourse … That the Scottish People are above their Kings … This Discourse Queen Elizabeth read, (not without Indignation,) and tacitly condemned, as written in Affront and Wrong to Kings.”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Camden, <em>History of … Elizabeth</em>, London 1688, pp. 154–155. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>For there were now come about Scottish matters, in the Name of the King of Scots, the Earl of Morton, Petcarne Abbat of Dunfermelin, and James Mac-Gilly: who, being commanded by Queen Elizabeth to deliver their Reasons positively for their Deposing the Queen, and to shew the Justice of them, <span class="cited-passage">produced a large Discourse</span>, wherein, with insolent Liberty and Sharpness of words, they went about to prove by an Ancient Right of the Kingdom of Scotland, by outworn Examples and new ones picked up here and there, “<span class="cited-passage">That the Scottish People are above their Kings</span>; yea and by the Authority of Calvin, That popular Magistrates are constituted every-where to bridle the Lust of Kings, and that it is lawfull for them to restrain bad Kings by Imprisonment, and to depose them.” They boasted much of their Civility and Lenity towards the deposed Queen, in permitting her to substitute her Son in her room, and to appoint him Guardians; “That it was more out of the Peoples Mercy than her own Innocency that she had her Life:” and many other like things, which tumultuous Spirits insolently devise against the Royal Majesty. <span class="cited-passage">This Discourse Queen Elizabeth read, (not without Indignation,)</span> and tacitly condemned, as written in Affront and Wrong to Kings. …</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Lecky (vol. II, ch. V, text at note 84): “she was immediately favoured in reply with a long dissertation on the manifest superiority of nations to their sovereigns; which, as Camden assures us, and as we can readily believe, she received with extreme indignation.” Camden says “not without indignation”, and adds that she condemned it only “tacitly”. The English here is the 1688 translation; Lecky read Camden’s Latin.</p>
