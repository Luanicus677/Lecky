abstract: |
  <p>Lecky says that “Warton has shown that on at least one occasion in the fifteenth century, Adam and Eve were brought upon the stage strictly in their state of innocence. In the next scene the fig-leaves were introduced.” The passage, on Malone’s pp. 15–16, is Warton’s, quoted by Malone; Warton gives no date for it, so Lecky’s “fifteenth century” is his own inference. Warton is less shocked than Lecky: the spectators watched “with great composure”, since “they had the authority of scripture for such a representation”. Malone adds a footnote that the same thing was revived under James I, at an Oxford pastoral before the King and Queen.</p>

<section id="naked" class="passage">
<h2>“Adam and Eve are both exhibited on the stage naked” (pp. 15–16)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: p. 15 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/10747532bsb/page/n20/mode/2up">scan</a>) and p. 16 (<a href="https://archive.org/details/10747532bsb/page/n21/mode/2up">scan</a>). Long s modernised. <span class="cited-passage">Highlighted</span>: what Lecky reports.</p>
<figure class="facsimile"><a href="../assets/img/ref/malone-pp15-16.jpg"><img src="../assets/img/ref/malone-pp15-16.jpg" alt="Two strips of an eighteenth-century printed page with long s, describing Adam and Eve exhibited naked in a mystery play" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Malone, quoting Warton, pp. 15–16 of the 1800 printing. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek) · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>
<blockquote>
<p>… In these Mysteries I have sometimes seen gross and open obscenities. In a play of <i>The Old and New Testament</i>, <span class="cited-passage">Adam and Eve are both exhibited on the stage naked, and conversing about their nakedness; this very pertinently introduces the next scene; in which they have coverings of fig-leaves.</span> This extraordinary spectacle was beheld by a numerous assembly of both sexes with great composure: they had the authority of scripture for such a representation, and they gave matters just as they found them in the third chapter of <i>Genesis</i>. It would have been absolute heresy to have departed from the sacred text in personating the primitive appearance of our first parents, whom the spectators so nearly resembled in simplicity; and if this had not been the case, the dramatists were ignorant what to reject and what to retain.</p>
<p>[Malone’s footnote:] This kind of primitive exhibition was revived in the time of King James the First, several persons appearing almost entirely naked in a pastoral exhibited at Oxford before the King and Queen, and the ladies who attended her. It is, if I recollect right, described by Winwood.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The quotation closes with a reference to “Warton’s <i>History of English Poetry</i>, Vol. I. pp. 242, &amp;c.”</p>
</section>

<section id="chester" class="passage">
<h2>The Chester plays, guild by guild (p. 11, note)</h2>
<p class="passage-source">English: p. 11, footnote, <a href="https://archive.org/details/10747532bsb/page/n16/mode/2up">scan</a>. Not cited by Lecky; given here because it shows how the mysteries were staged.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>“Exhibited at Chester in the year 1327, at the expense of the different trading companies of that city. <i>The Fall of Lucifer</i>, by the Tanners. <i>The Creation</i>, by the Drapers. <i>The Deluge</i>, by the Dyers. <i>Abraham, Melchisedech, and Lot</i>, by the Barbers. … <i>The Shepherds feeding their Flocks by Night</i>, by the Painters and Glaziers. <i>The three Kings</i>, by the Vintners. … <i>The last Supper</i>, by the Bakers. … <i>Descent into Hell</i>, by the Cooks and Innkeepers. <i>The Resurrection</i>, by the Skinners. <i>The Ascension</i>, by the Taylors. … <i>Antichrist</i>, by the Clothiers. <i>Day of Judgement</i>, by the Websters.” The reader will perhaps smile at some of these combinations. This is the substance and order of the former part of the play. God enters creating the world: he breathes life into Adam, leads him into Paradise, and opens his side while sleeping. Adam and Eve appear naked, and not ashamed, and the old serpent enters lamenting his fall. He converses with Eve. She eats of the forbidden fruit, and gives part to Adam. They purpose, according to the stage-direction, to make themselves <i lang="la">subligacula a foliis quibus tegamus pudenda</i> [aprons of leaves to cover our shame]. Cover their nakedness with leaves, and converse with God. God’s curse. The serpent <i>exit</i> hissing. They are driven from Paradise by four angels and the cherubim with a flaming sword. Adam appears digging the ground, and Eve spinning.</p>
</blockquote>
</section>
