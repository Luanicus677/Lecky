This edition fixes the mistakes of the scanning software and nothing else. Where the 1865 printing itself has an odd word, a strange spelling or a wrong date, the text here keeps it. In November 2026 a machine reader was asked to flag likely errors in the transcription; each flag was then checked against a photograph of the printed line. Most of the “errors” turned out to be in the book. The best of them are shown below. Click a strip to open the full page of the scan.

## Where the sense wants another word

**“Accordingly wept to sleep again”** (vol. I, p. 66). Luther, Lecky says, grew so used to the Devil making a noise in the cloisters that, woken by the sound one night, “he perceived that it was *only* the Devil, and accordingly wept to sleep again”. The joke needs “went”. In the scanned copy there is a pencil tick in the margin beside these lines, perhaps an earlier reader’s.

**“No priest had embraced Christianity”** (vol. II, p. 25). Lecky is giving the persecutors’ case: if the New Testament shows no apostle using force, “this was simply because in their time no priest had embraced Christianity”. The sense seems to want “prince”, a ruler with force to use; the page says “priest”.

<figure class="facsimile"><a href="https://archive.org/details/rationalismineur02leckuoft/page/n40/mode/2up"><img src="../assets/img/rationalism/slips/priest.jpg" alt="Printed lines reading “this was simply because in their time no priest had embraced Christianity”" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption>Vol. II, p. 25. <span class="credit">Scan: Internet Archive · public domain</span></figcaption></figure>

**“To subdue men’s bodies of their souls and spirits”** (vol. I, p. 340). Lecky quotes an English law of 1548 that kept the fish-days after the Reformation, partly for the good of the soul and partly, as it frankly says, so that fishermen “may thereby the rather be set on work”. Abstinence, in his quotation, is “a mean to virtue, and to subdue men’s bodies of their souls and spirits”. Hallam’s <a href="../ref/hallam-literature-and-constitutional-history.html"><i>Constitutional History</i></a>, which Lecky cites for these laws, gives the statute’s words as “to subdue men’s bodies to their soul and spirit” (ch. VII, in a note; read in the Project Gutenberg text, not on a scan).

## A century out

**Aquinas, “the ablest writer of the fourteenth century”** (vol. I, p. 72). Thomas Aquinas died in 1274, so the thirteenth century is meant. Whether the slip is Lecky’s or the printer’s cannot be told from the page.

## A name misspelled four times

**“Wagstaafe”** (vol. I, p. 130, and the table of contents). The Oxford sceptic about witchcraft whom Lecky calls “a deformed, dwarfish scholar” is usually spelled <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wagstaffe">Wagstaffe</a>. The 1865 edition prints “Wagstaafe” every time: once in the contents, once in the text and twice in the footnote, so it is probably not a compositor’s one-off slip.

## Others left as printed

Not pictured: “interpretetion” and “one the most common” (vol. I, ch. III); “it all difficult”, “Protestanism” (ch. II); “Voltarian”, “Lammenais”, “deepseated”, “developement”; “his named erased from the list of advocates” (vol. II, p. 350); “Tertullian and other of the Fathers” (vol. II, ch. V); “the Marshes” in a list of Italian regions, where the Marches seem to be meant (vol. II, p. 121); “the Starchamber” (p. 129) and “a crime of the deepest die” (p. 181), probably spellings of the time; a quotation from “a great modern historian” that opens but never closes (vol. II, p. 50, at “that ever took place”); a bracket that never closes after “tom. i. p. 113” and a quotation mark that never closes after “Curious Tracts on Witchcraft” (vol. I, ch. I, notes).

Page numbers on this page are worked out from the scan’s image numbers (vol. I: image minus 29; vol. II: image minus 15); only p. 25 of vol. II was read from the page head.
