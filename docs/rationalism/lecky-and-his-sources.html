Lecky and his sources

Where the footnotes and the books disagree

This page is editorial. It gathers what has turned up in checking Lecky’s footnotes against the books themselves, on the source pages of this edition. Lecky’s text is never altered; the disagreements are recorded here and on the source pages, where each passage is transcribed from a scan and translated.

Lecky was, for a historian of 1865, an unusually careful one. Most of what has been checked so far stands up: his quotations are usually exact, and his page numbers usually lead to the right page of the edition he used. Concina, Naudé, Binsfeld, Bédarride, Buckle, Durand, Garinet, Michelet’s La Sorcière and Hallam’s Literature of Europe, for instance, match him page for page. But checking several hundred of his footnotes has also turned up places where the book does not say what Lecky says it says. They fall into a few kinds, and they are listed below. Only part of his notes have been checked so far, so this is a sample, not a verdict.

The source says something else

  • Ugly saints and the monks. Lecky says the hideous figures in Roman mosaics of the dark ages were “evidently imitated from the emaciation of extreme asceticism”, citing Vitet, pp. 268–279 (ch. III, note 59). On those pages Vitet blames the barbarians: “the question at issue is a question of barbarism, and precisely the barbarians are forgotten.” Asceticism is only a question he raised earlier and left open. Vitet
  • Witches, apples and orchards. Lecky credits De Lancre with the “brilliant suggestion” that witchcraft about Bordeaux was connected with orchards, the Devil having a special power over apples (ch. I, note 3). The passage in Garinet that he cites says nothing of orchards or Bordeaux; De Lancre’s point is Eve and the apple. Garinet
  • Snow and tempests. Constantine’s law of 321, in Lecky’s summary, protected harvests “from hail, snow, or tempests” (ch. I, note 27). The law speaks of rains and hail; snow and tempests are not in it. Codex Theodosianus
  • The bull of Cuenca. A bull that had killed seven men, Lecky says, “became the object of the highest reverence, and the people were so gratified that a painting … was immediately executed” (ch. III, note 132). His source, Concina quoting Mariana, says the bull was “consecrated to immortality” by a painting in a public place, and calls it “a monument of madness and savagery”; the delighted crowd and the haste are Lecky’s. Concina
  • An English consul who was Venetian. Lecky’s “earliest notice” of an English consul (1346) is, in Macpherson, a consul of the Venetian merchants at Bruges, mentioned in an English record (ch. VI, note 94). Macpherson
  • The rebellion that wasn’t. Lecky says the witch-burnings near Brescia in 1518 provoked “an absolute rebellion” (ch. I, note 5). The Venetian records show the Council of Ten ordering the inquisitors to stop, and delegates from the valley later asking for the inquisition to go on. Madden
  • Agrippa’s black dog. “It is related that, on his death-bed, he drew off from his neck a black dog, which was a demon” (ch. I, note 87). Garinet, whom Lecky cites, tells the story only to file it among “the lies of history”, and in his French Agrippa takes a magic collar off the dog’s neck; Lecky has him pull the dog off his own. Garinet
  • Things not on the page. Newton’s last sentence in Lecky’s quotation (“The history of those ages is very imperfect”) is not in Brewster’s text (ch. II, note 8; Brewster). Hogarth does not mention the Cardinal Gonzaga at Politian’s Orfeo (ch. VI, note 131; Hogarth). Pecchio’s page has the maritime code of Amalfi, not Lecky’s manuscript of Roman laws found there (ch. VI, note 90; Pecchio).
  • Julian’s bagpipe. “Julian had the bad taste to praise” a wind instrument “almost exactly the same as a Scotch bagpipe” (ch. III, note 82). Burney, whom Lecky cites, calls the epigram “attributed” to Julian an “enigma, upon the organ”, and translates its instrument as one played with keys. Burney
  • The monk who would not wash. Lecky’s saint who “had therefore scrupulously abstained from washing since his conversion” and stood “in despair upon the banks of a bridgeless stream” (ch. II, note 2): Socrates says only that Ammon would not see himself naked, and once prayed to cross a river without undressing. The washing, the despair and the missing bridge are Lecky’s. Socrates

The right words, the wrong speaker

  • Jerome and Vigilantius. “As St. Jerome says”, Lecky writes, the souls of the martyrs love their ashes (ch. III, note 34). The words are Vigilantius’s, quoted by Jerome in order to mock them. Jerome
  • Naudé and the demonologists. Lecky has Naudé “observe” that nearly all heresies before the Reformation were accompanied by an outburst of sorcery (ch. I, note 63). Naudé reports it as the axiom of “our Catholic doctors, and principally Delrio and Maldonat”, and passes on without endorsing it. Naudé
  • Calvin and Dumoulin. The view that usury is wrong only when oppressive or taken from the poor is Calvin’s in Lecky (ch. VI, note 49); in Concina, whom he cites, it is Charles Dumoulin’s, and Calvin’s argument is a different one. Concina
  • Augustine on Christ’s face. Lecky’s quotation joins Augustine’s remark that nobody knows what Christ looked like to a sentence that, in Augustine, is about St Paul (ch. III, note 28). Augustine

Stronger than the source

  • Augustine’s peacock. Augustine tested for himself that a peacock’s flesh did not rot, and Lecky says he “seemed to regard [it] as a miracle” (ch. III, note 8). Augustine gives it as a natural example, beside the salamander and Etna. Augustine
  • Constantine’s ban on sacrifice. Lecky says the prohibition of temple worship is “expressly and unequivocally asserted” by Eusebius (ch. IV, note 18). Eusebius does assert a ban on sacrifice, but his English translator noted “that is, private sacrifices”, and Milman, whom Lecky also cites, sets beside it Constantine’s own letters promising the pagans toleration. Eusebius, Milman
  • Catherine’s interest law. “Catherine of Russia” lowered the general rate of interest and so raised it (ch. VI, note 59): in Storch the example is one province, Livonia, in 1786. Storch
  • A terrible Christ. Didron confines the sternness of Christ in art to the Passion and the Last Judgment; Lecky makes it a general rule (ch. I, note 52). Didron
  • Aliturus. Lecky’s sentence on the actor who interceded for the Jews claims more than Gibbon’s note does (ch. VI, note 103). Gibbon
  • “It is said.” In three places Lecky states as fact what Dalyell only reports as hearsay about Scottish witch trials. Dalyell
  • Both burnt alive? Of two priests who lived for decades with demon mistresses, Lecky says “they were both burnt alive” (ch. I, note 21). Bodin, retelling Pico della Mirandola, says the first “was burnt alive” and the second only “was also burnt”. Bodin
  • No impostors in Scotland. “The very remarkable fact, that no cases of imposture have been detected in Scotch witch-trials, is noted by Buckle” (ch. I, note 145). Buckle’s footnote quotes the jurist David Hume, who says only that none of the trials “proceeds upon the notion of a vain or cheating art”: no one was charged with witchcraft as a fraud. That is a fact about the indictments, not evidence that no witch was an impostor. Buckle
  • Zeal for God’s honour. Quoting Newman on the Greek Fathers’ “just causes” for an untruth, Lecky prints “zeal for God’s honour” in italics and concludes that it “would include all of what are commonly termed pious frauds” (ch. IV, note 38). Newman prints the words in plain type and draws no such conclusion; his next lines give the just causes as defence of life and honour, keeping a secret, and the good of others. Newman

Dates, figures and names

  • Salem. “Twenty-seven persons were executed” (ch. I, note 130); Hutchinson, his source, counts nineteen hanged and one pressed to death, with eight more condemned. Lecky’s figure is the hanged plus the condemned. Hutchinson
  • The last witch burnt. “I believe, in Switzerland, in 1782” (ch. I, note 50): Michelet has Spain in 1782 and Switzerland in 1781. Elsewhere Lecky dates the Spanish burning 1780, where his source, Buckle, says 1781 (ch. I, note 4). Michelet, Buckle
  • Leith. Lecky dates the burnings at Leith 1664; Dalyell has 1644 (ch. I, at note 159). Dalyell
  • Grillandus was not “an Italian inquisitor of the fifteenth century” (ch. I, note 1): he judged sorcery cases from 1517 and published in the sixteenth. Grillandi
  • Six general councils. Lecky counts 28 councils against usury, “six of them regarded by the Church of Rome as general” (ch. VI, note 47). He counted Concina’s list correctly, but Concina’s list also includes Nicaea and Lyon I, which Rome counts as general too. Concina
  • Bull-baiting. Macaulay’s joke is that the Puritan hated bear-baiting “not because it gave pain to the bear, but because it gave pleasure to the spectators”; Lecky makes it bull-baiting (ch. III, note 132). Macaulay
  • “Soon afterwards.” Oxford’s decree of 1683 for passive obedience, Lecky says, was one “which the House of Lords soon afterwards committed to the flames” (ch. V, at note 91). Hallam, whom he cites, dates the burning 1709: twenty-six years later. Hallam
  • Bamberg and Würzburg. “Six hundred by a single bishop of Bamberg, and eight hundred in a single year in the bishopric of Wurtzburg” (ch. I, note 2, citing Michelet p. 10). Michelet gives two different counts: p. x has Würzburg 800 “almost in one batch” and Bamberg 1,500; p. 206 has Bamberg 600 and Würzburg 900. Lecky took one figure from each page, and “in a single year” is his. Michelet
  • Mazarin’s letter to the Bishop of Évreux on the nuns of Louviers is dated 1642 by Lecky (ch. I, note 98); the letter as Garinet prints it is dated 21 September 1643. Garinet
  • Seville, not Andalusia for Mariana’s two thousand burnt in the first year of the Inquisition (ch. IV, note 37); and 1443, not 1440, for the ransom of Moors for slaves (ch. VI, note 160). Llorente, Macpherson

Wrong chapter, wrong page

These are slips of reference rather than of substance: the passage exists, but not where Lecky says.

  • Eusebius, Life of Constantine, “lib. i. c. 16” and “lib. iii. caps. 5, 6” (ch. I, note 26; ch. III, note 75): the passages are II.45 and III.54. Eusebius
  • Malleus Maleficarum “Pars I. Quæst. vii.” for St Serenus (ch. I, note 78): it is Pars II, Quaest. I. Malleus
  • Augustine, City of God, the Sibyl’s acrostic “cap. 20” (ch. III, note 14): it is chapter 23. Augustine
  • Riccoboni, Histoire du théâtre italien, “p. 89” for the Harlequin Cecchini ennobled by the Emperor (ch. VI, note 123): he is on pp. 42 and 57, although Lecky’s other pages in the same volume fit. Riccoboni
  • Concina, Adversus usuram, “p. 14” (ch. VI, note 56): the words are on p. xiv. Concina
  • Pages that do not fit any edition found: Mackay, “vol. ii. pp. 286–297” (ch. VI, note 115; Mackay); Milman, “vol. iii. p. 60” (ch. IV, note 31; Milman); Storch, “tom. iii. p. 403”, a page tome III does not have (ch. VI, note 111; Storch); Beugnot “p. 148” for p. 143 (ch. I, note 29; Beugnot).

What this does not show

Many apparent mismatches are not Lecky’s fault. Where his page numbers fail, the usual reason is that the edition he used is not online and a later or earlier printing has been read instead (Rio, Ford, Neal, Sarpi, Lamet and Fromageau, among others); the source pages say so where it applies. Slips of the 1865 printer, as distinct from Lecky’s, are on a separate page: As printed. And the books he gets right far outnumber those listed here.
