A Cabinet of Curiosities

What lies at the bottom of Lecky’s footnotes

Lecky’s Rationalism in Europe rests on eight hundred and fifty footnotes, and behind them are hundreds of forgotten books: demonologies, sermons, law codes, lives of saints, treatises on usury and the theatre. This edition has been tracking those books down, page by page, and transcribing the passages Lecky used. Here are some of the strangest things found there. Each item links to the passage itself, translated, with a picture of the old printed page. Hover a link to preview it; from any source page, the “rabbit hole” box at its foot leads on to the books Lecky cites beside it.

The Devil and his servants

  • The Devil as a mature student. Seventeenth-century Scottish divines taught that Satan’s cunning grew with age: “having been studying for more than five thousand years, he had now attained to unexampled dexterity”, as Buckle summed them up. One of them called him “an advancing student, and still learning”. Buckle →
  • Lord of the flies. Why was Beelzebub the god of flies? Because, says Jean Bodin, there was not a single fly in his temple, “as they say that in the Palace at Venice there is not one fly, and in the Palace at Toledo only one”. Bodin →
  • Agrippa’s black dog. The magician Cornelius Agrippa, the story went, took a collar covered with magic signs off the neck of his black dog as he lay dying, and told it: “Be gone, wretched beast; you are the cause of my ruin.” The dog was a demon. Garinet, who tells the story, files it among “the lies of history”. Garinet →
  • The nightmare was a witch. In Valois and Picardy, Bodin heard, there were witches called cochemares. A rich farmer told him that whenever one had sat on him in the night, the old woman he feared came round next morning to ask for fire. Bodin →
  • Forty years with a demon. Gianfrancesco Pico, Prince of Mirandola, wrote that he had seen an eighty-year-old priest, Benoist Berne, who said he had lived for more than forty years with a demon in woman’s shape, whom he called Hermione. He was burnt alive. A second priest, aged seventy, confessed to more than fifty years of the same. Bodin →
  • Where “the Deuce” comes from. Alfred Maury derived it from the Dusii, night spirits of the ancient Celts, whom St Augustine had already heard of. Old Nick, he adds, is the water spirit Nix. Maury →
  • The sect that spat at demons. The Messalians believed themselves besieged by demons at every moment: they blew their noses and spat without end, and shot arrows at the demons around them. Maury →
  • Why witches float. Because water, consecrated to baptism, “cannot bear witches”, said a physician of Marburg. The hard-line Bishop Binsfeld disagreed, and ruled that a judge who used the water test “sins mortally”. Binsfeld →
  • Werewolves get no mercy. The witch-judge Henri Boguet’s rules: ordinary sorcerers should be strangled and then burnt, but “werewolves must be burnt alive”. Garinet →
  • When the Devil turned ugly. The oldest known picture of the Devil in human form, on an ivory cover of the Gospels of Charles the Bald, shows a horned figure with a shepherd’s crook. He took on the hideous form artists gave him afterwards only around the thirteenth century. Maury →
  • A council against dancing bears. The Council in Trullo (692) excommunicated, in one breath, fortune-tellers, cloud-chasers, amulet-makers and “those who lead about bears or similar beasts for the sport and ruin of simple folk”. Wier →
  • Ventriloquists speak by the Devil. Anyone who speaks otherwise than through the mouth works by the Devil’s help: so “the first Christians knew very well”, said the abbé Fiard, on the authority of “five or six forgotten doctors”, chief among them Nicholas of Lyra (d. 1340). Garinet is mocking him; Lecky reports it straight. Garinet →

Saints, pictures and legends

  • The stag that sucks snakes. Why was the stag a symbol of Christ? Because, Origen reported, it smells out where serpents hide, puts its mouth to the hole and draws them out with its breath; after eating them it is so thirsty that if it goes three hours without water it dies. Hence “As the hart panteth after the water brooks”. Maury →
  • Conceived through the ear. Painters showed the Holy Spirit as a dove flying into the Virgin’s ear, meaning a purely spiritual union. The picture was taken literally: “God spoke by the angel,” runs a sermon printed under Augustine’s name, “and the Virgin was made pregnant through the ear.” Maury →
  • Half a beard in hell. On the First Crusade a priest reported that the dead Bishop Adhémar had appeared to him: he was in heaven, but for having doubted the Holy Lance he had first been taken to hell, “where, as you see, they cut off the right side of my beard and hair”. Maury →
  • The ugly saints of Rome. Lecky says the haggard figures in Roman mosaics of the dark ages were imitated from starving ascetics. The art critic he cites blames the barbarians: Roman artists learned to please “their half-savage Maecenases”, and painted “Scandinavian and Teutonic types”. Vitet →
  • Augustine’s incorruptible peacock. Augustine tested it himself at Carthage: roast peacock did not rot. He offered it as one of nature’s marvels, beside the salamander and Etna. Augustine →
  • God with a great nose. The Hebrew phrase for “patient” is literally “long of nose”. Bodin, writing on what faces reveal, took it word for word: God calls himself “Big-nose”, and large noses therefore mean prudence and patience. Bodin →
  • The sun is hell. An Anglican clergyman calculated that the sun is large enough and hot enough to hold the damned of all ages, and concluded that it is “the Tartarus or Local Hell”. Swinden →
  • The old gods as rag dolls. In the fifth century, persecution notwithstanding, peasants still carried Jupiter, Minerva and Venus about as “poor little dolls of linen or of flour”; in the eighth century they were still doing it. Michelet →
  • Improving the evidence. Quoting Josephus on the death of King Agrippa, the church historian Eusebius quietly replaced an owl, a bird of ill omen, with the avenging angel of the Christian legend. Maury →

Laws, money and punishments

  • The price of eating a man. Under the Salic law, a witch convicted of having devoured a man was fined eight thousand deniers, “which make two hundred sous”. Garinet →
  • Burnt for eating meat on Friday. At Angers in 1539, Bodin reports, those who ate meat on a Friday and would not repent were burnt alive; those who repented were hanged. Bodin →
  • “The people cannot live without loans.” So St Louis’s counsellors told him, urging that the Jews, “already damned”, might as well do the damnable trade of lending. Louis answered: “Let them give up usury, or leave my land.” Troplong →
  • Senators at work, artisans at rest. Augustine scolded monks who had left poor trades for the cloister to be fed for nothing: “In the cells there will be senators working with their hands and artisans at rest!” Champagny →
  • A hospital of the dead. At the Dublin Foundling Hospital, in the six years ending with 1797, 12,786 children were admitted and 12,561 died. M’Culloch →
  • A horoscope behind a statistic. Lecky repeats that 72,000 people were executed under Henry VIII. His source doubted it, and the figure goes back to the astrologer Cardano’s horoscope of Edward VI, where it is hearsay from a French bishop. Barrington →
  • Why comets kill princes. Because the great have bodies “weaker, more delicate” than the rest of us, and comets inflame their bile, as the common explanation ran; the sceptics answered that plenty of popes and kings had died with no comet in sight. Roccamora →
  • Why did Abel need to guard his sheep? If Adam’s family were alone on earth, asked Isaac La Peyrère, “What thieves should he fear, beside his Father, Mother, and Brother?” La Peyrère →
  • A Harlequin ennobled. Pier Maria Cecchini, who played Harlequin, was ennobled by the Emperor Matthias; the actor-historian Riccoboni kept a printed copy of the letters. Riccoboni →

Where Lecky slipped

  • Two counts of the dead. Michelet gives Bamberg 1,500 burnings in one place and 600 in another, and Würzburg 800 and 900. Lecky took one figure from each page. Michelet →
  • Salem. “Twenty-seven persons were executed”, says Lecky. His source counts nineteen hanged and one pressed to death. Hutchinson →
  • “Soon afterwards.” Oxford’s decree for passive obedience (1683) was, says Lecky, “soon afterwards” burned by the House of Lords. It was burned in 1709. Hallam →
  • A letter a year out. Mazarin’s letter congratulating the Bishop of Évreux on the possessed nuns of Louviers is dated 1643; Lecky says 1642. Garinet →
