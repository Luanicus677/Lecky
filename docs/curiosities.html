A Cabinet of Curiosities

What lies at the bottom of Lecky’s footnotes

Lecky’s Rationalism in Europe rests on eight hundred and fifty footnotes, and behind them are hundreds of forgotten books: demonologies, sermons, law codes, lives of saints, treatises on usury and the theatre. This edition has been tracking those books down, page by page, and transcribing the passages Lecky used. Here are some of the strangest things found there. Each item links to the passage itself, translated, with a picture of the old printed page. Hover a link to preview it; from any source page, the “rabbit hole” box at its foot leads on to the books Lecky cites beside it.

The Devil and his servants

  • The Devil as a mature student. Seventeenth-century Scottish divines taught that Satan’s cunning grew with age: “having been studying for more than five thousand years, he had now attained to unexampled dexterity”, as Buckle summed them up. One of them called him “an advancing student, and still learning”. Buckle →
  • Lord of the flies. Why was Beelzebub the god of flies? Because, says Jean Bodin, there was not a single fly in his temple, “as they say that in the Palace at Venice there is not one fly, and in the Palace at Toledo only one”. Bodin →
  • Agrippa’s black dog. The magician Cornelius Agrippa, the story went, took a collar covered with magic signs off the neck of his black dog as he lay dying, and told it: “Be gone, wretched beast; you are the cause of my ruin.” The dog was a demon. Garinet, who tells the story, files it among “the lies of history”. Garinet →
  • The nightmare was a witch. In Valois and Picardy, Bodin heard, there were witches called cochemares. A rich farmer told him that whenever one had sat on him in the night, the old woman he feared came round next morning to ask for fire. Bodin →
  • Forty years with a demon. Gianfrancesco Pico, Prince of Mirandola, wrote that he had seen an eighty-year-old priest, Benoist Berne, who said he had lived for more than forty years with a demon in woman’s shape, whom he called Hermione. He was burnt alive. A second priest, aged seventy, confessed to more than fifty years of the same. Bodin →
  • Where “the Deuce” comes from. Alfred Maury derived it from the Dusii, night spirits of the ancient Celts, whom St Augustine had already heard of. Old Nick, he adds, is the water spirit Nix. Maury →
  • The sect that spat at demons. The Messalians believed themselves besieged by demons at every moment: they blew their noses and spat without end, and shot arrows at the demons around them. Maury →
  • Why witches float. Because water, consecrated to baptism, “cannot bear witches”, said a physician of Marburg. The hard-line Bishop Binsfeld disagreed, and ruled that a judge who used the water test “sins mortally”. Binsfeld →
  • Werewolves get no mercy. The witch-judge Henri Boguet’s rules: ordinary sorcerers should be strangled and then burnt, but “werewolves must be burnt alive”. Garinet →
  • When the Devil turned ugly. The oldest known picture of the Devil in human form, on an ivory cover of the Gospels of Charles the Bald, shows a horned figure with a shepherd’s crook. He took on the hideous form artists gave him afterwards only around the thirteenth century. Maury →
  • A council against dancing bears. The Council in Trullo (692) excommunicated, in one breath, fortune-tellers, cloud-chasers, amulet-makers and “those who lead about bears or similar beasts for the sport and ruin of simple folk”. Wier →
  • Ventriloquists speak by the Devil. Anyone who speaks otherwise than through the mouth works by the Devil’s help: so “the first Christians knew very well”, said the abbé Fiard, on the authority of “five or six forgotten doctors”, chief among them Nicholas of Lyra (d. 1340). Garinet is mocking him; Lecky reports it straight. Garinet →
  • Eyes shut against Christ. A devil appeared to a holy man in the form of Christ: “I am Christ, visiting you in person, because you are worthy.” The holy man covered his eyes with both hands: “I do not wish to see Christ here; it is enough if I see him in glory.” Gerson →
  • An ambassador to the Devil. The Puritan Parliament, jeered Samuel Butler, sent “a Ledger to the Devil”, a resident envoy. An eighteenth-century editor named him: Matthew Hopkins, the witch-finder, who hanged sixty suspected witches in Suffolk in one year. Butler →
  • A pitch-barrel at Dornoch. In June 1727, by Captain Burt’s account, an old woman condemned for witchcraft in Sutherland was burnt in a pitch-barrel at Dornoch. Burt →
  • Ghosts as proof of the soul. Origen took the “shadow-like phantasms of the dead, that have appeared to many about graves and monuments” as souls in their luminous bodies. Even doubting Thomas, he argued, never doubted that a dead man’s soul could be seen; what he doubted was the risen body. Cudworth →

Saints, pictures and legends

  • The stag that sucks snakes. Why was the stag a symbol of Christ? Because, Origen reported, it smells out where serpents hide, puts its mouth to the hole and draws them out with its breath; after eating them it is so thirsty that if it goes three hours without water it dies. Hence “As the hart panteth after the water brooks”. Maury →
  • Conceived through the ear. Painters showed the Holy Spirit as a dove flying into the Virgin’s ear, meaning a purely spiritual union. The picture was taken literally: “God spoke by the angel,” runs a sermon printed under Augustine’s name, “and the Virgin was made pregnant through the ear.” Maury →
  • Half a beard in hell. On the First Crusade a priest reported that the dead Bishop Adhémar had appeared to him: he was in heaven, but for having doubted the Holy Lance he had first been taken to hell, “where, as you see, they cut off the right side of my beard and hair”. Maury →
  • The ugly saints of Rome. Lecky says the haggard figures in Roman mosaics of the dark ages were imitated from starving ascetics. The art critic he cites blames the barbarians: Roman artists learned to please “their half-savage Maecenases”, and painted “Scandinavian and Teutonic types”. Vitet →
  • Augustine’s incorruptible peacock. Augustine tested it himself at Carthage: roast peacock did not rot. He offered it as one of nature’s marvels, beside the salamander and Etna. Augustine →
  • God with a great nose. The Hebrew phrase for “patient” is literally “long of nose”. Bodin, writing on what faces reveal, took it word for word: God calls himself “Big-nose”, and large noses therefore mean prudence and patience. Bodin →
  • The sun is hell. An Anglican clergyman calculated that the sun is large enough and hot enough to hold the damned of all ages, and concluded that it is “the Tartarus or Local Hell”. Swinden →
  • The old gods as rag dolls. In the fifth century, persecution notwithstanding, peasants still carried Jupiter, Minerva and Venus about as “poor little dolls of linen or of flour”; in the eighth century they were still doing it. Michelet →
  • Improving the evidence. Quoting Josephus on the death of King Agrippa, the church historian Eusebius quietly replaced an owl, a bird of ill omen, with the avenging angel of the Christian legend. Maury →
  • Counting the saints. How many lives of saints are there? Guizot counted one month of the Bollandists’ collection, April, day by day: 141 saints for the twelfth alone, 1,472 for the month. From that he estimated “more than 25,000”. Guizot →
  • Gravediggers of the desert. When Paul the first hermit died, Jerome says, two lions ran out of the desert, roared over the body “so that you would understand that they were mourning”, dug a grave with their paws, and then came to Antony with bowed heads, licking his hands, to beg his blessing. Jerome →
  • Ferried by an angel. The desert monk Ammon held that “it became not a monk to see his own person exposed”. Once, unwilling to undress to cross a river, he prayed, “and immediately an angel transported him to the other side”. On his wedding night he had read his bride St Paul’s Epistle to the Corinthians, and talked her into chastity. Socrates →
  • Mice from filth. The humanist Juan Luis Vives taught that some mice are born of parents and some of filth. Just before, he reports that women at Naples and in Flanders often give birth to “a beast of no single form” instead of a child. Vives →

Players and the stage

  • The devil in the lettuce. A nun ate a lettuce in the convent garden without first making the sign of the cross, and was possessed. When the abbot Equitius came, the devil protested through her mouth: “What have I done? What have I done? I was sitting there upon the lettice, and she came and did eat me.” Gregory →
  • Found in its own house. A Christian woman went to the theatre and came home possessed. Rebuked at the exorcism for attacking a believer, the demon answered: “And quite rightly; I found her in my own domain.” Another woman dreamed of a shroud after hearing a tragic actor, says Tertullian, and was dead within five days. Tertullian →
  • Burnt for absolution. On his deathbed the composer Lulli was refused absolution until he burnt the opera he was writing. He did. When he rallied, a young prince came to see him: “What, Baptiste, have you been such a fool as to burn your opera?” “Hush, my lord,” whispered Lulli, “I have got a copy of it.” Hogarth →
  • A little earth for Molière. All that could be obtained for Molière when he died, wrote the Oratorian Le Brun, was “a little earth”, and that only by prayer. The actor Rosimond (d. 1691) was buried without clergy, candles or prayers, in the corner of the cemetery kept for unbaptised children. Le Brun →
  • Actors in mortal sin. In 1694 six doctors of the Sorbonne ruled that actors, “by reason of their profession”, are in a state of mortal sin, and must be refused absolution unless they promise to leave the stage. Lamet and Fromageau →
  • Back to the stage, for life. Christian emperors let an actress leave the stage if she asked for baptism. A law of 381 says that if she then returns to her old life she is to be dragged back and kept there “until she is a ridiculous old woman”, and not released even then. Codex Theodosianus →
  • Burnt on the great staircase. In 1761 a lawyer who defended actors and attacked the refusal of Christian burial to them had his book torn up and burnt by the executioner “at the foot of the great staircase of the Palace”, and was struck from the roll of advocates. Desprez de Boissy →
  • Adam and Eve, unclothed. In an English mystery play Adam and Eve appeared on the stage naked, the fig-leaves coming in the next scene. The spectators watched “with great composure”, wrote Thomas Warton, since “they had the authority of scripture for such a representation”. Malone →

Inquisitors and persecutors

  • Why miracles stopped. In the apostles’ day, argued the Jesuit Muzzarelli, God punished heretics himself: Simon Magus, flying by the devils’ help, fell and broke both legs. Once the emperors were baptised, “Omnipotence little by little seemed to withdraw its foot”, and the Church had to take up severity. Muzzarelli →
  • God the Father’s nose. Philip II’s fallen secretary Antonio Pérez once said that if God the Father stood in his way he would cut off his nose. The Inquisition’s theologian ruled this heresy, since it implied that God has a body, and warned that it could not be excused on the ground that Christ, made man, has a nose. Mignet →
  • Enlightened by scandal. During the Great Schism two popes, and their successors for forty years, excommunicated each other. The result, wrote the Abbé de Mably, was that they “enlightened the faithful by dint of scandalising them” and made excommunication ridiculous. Mably →
  • The cardinal’s errand. Richelieu wanted to read La Boétie’s Voluntary Servitude and sent a gentleman down the booksellers’ street to buy it. Nobody knew it, until one bookseller offered a “collector’s” unbound copy for five pistoles, slipped out the back door, and came back with the sheets he had just unstitched from his own. Tallemant →
  • A President’s copy. The scan of Barbeyrac’s 1714 translation of Noodt’s Leiden lectures on sovereign power and liberty of conscience, one of the books in Lecky’s notes, is John Adams’s own copy, inscribed “Bôt of Jer. Gridley”. Noodt →
  • Not by killing but by dying. Writing as the last great persecution ended, the Christian rhetorician Lactantius held that “religion cannot be compelled” and must be defended “not by killing, but by dying”: “nothing is so voluntary as religion.” Lactantius →
  • God the first inquisitor. The official historian of the Spanish Inquisition began his history in Eden: God, the first inquisitor, drove Adam out of Paradise stripped of all his goods, “whence inquisitors confiscate the goods of heretics”. Páramo →
  • Pulled from the fire. At Barcelona a priest at the stake, “already somewhat scorched on one side”, cried out that he wished to abjure, and was taken from the fire. Fourteen years later he was found to have remained a heretic all along, and was burnt. Eymeric →
  • Converted by results. Augustine first held that “no one should be coerced into the unity of Christ”. What changed his mind, he told a Donatist bishop, was not argument but example: his own town, Hippo, once wholly Donatist, had become Catholic “by fear of the imperial laws”. Augustine →
  • Worse than an invasion. When Mary Queen of Scots heard mass in her own chapel, Knox told his congregation that “one mass was more fearfull unto him, than if ten thousand armed enemies were landed in ony parte of the realme”. His biographer quoted it to excuse it: “let us be just as well as liberal.” M’Crie →
  • A boatload of relics. After Jacques Clément killed Henri III in 1589, Leaguers dug up the earth soaked with the assassin’s blood to make a shrine in Paris. They overloaded their boat; a gale sank it “with the relics and the pilgrims”. In Rome, Sixtus V compared the murder to the Incarnation. De Thou →
  • A nation sentenced in three lines. In 1568 the Holy Office condemned the whole population of the Netherlands as heretics, and Philip II confirmed it. “Three millions of people”, wrote Motley, “were sentenced to the scaffold in three lines.” Motley →

Laws, money and punishments

  • The price of eating a man. Under the Salic law, a witch convicted of having devoured a man was fined eight thousand deniers, “which make two hundred sous”. Garinet →
  • Burnt for eating meat on Friday. At Angers in 1539, Bodin reports, those who ate meat on a Friday and would not repent were burnt alive; those who repented were hanged. Bodin →
  • “The people cannot live without loans.” So St Louis’s counsellors told him, urging that the Jews, “already damned”, might as well do the damnable trade of lending. Louis answered: “Let them give up usury, or leave my land.” Troplong →
  • Senators at work, artisans at rest. Augustine scolded monks who had left poor trades for the cloister to be fed for nothing: “In the cells there will be senators working with their hands and artisans at rest!” Champagny →
  • A hospital of the dead. At the Dublin Foundling Hospital, in the six years ending with 1797, 12,786 children were admitted and 12,561 died. M’Culloch →
  • A horoscope behind a statistic. Lecky repeats that 72,000 people were executed under Henry VIII. His source doubted it, and the figure goes back to the astrologer Cardano’s horoscope of Edward VI, where it is hearsay from a French bishop. Barrington →
  • Why comets kill princes. Because the great have bodies “weaker, more delicate” than the rest of us, and comets inflame their bile, as the common explanation ran; the sceptics answered that plenty of popes and kings had died with no comet in sight. Roccamora →
  • Why did Abel need to guard his sheep? If Adam’s family were alone on earth, asked Isaac La Peyrère, “What thieves should he fear, beside his Father, Mother, and Brother?” La Peyrère →
  • A Harlequin ennobled. Pier Maria Cecchini, who played Harlequin, was ennobled by the Emperor Matthias; the actor-historian Riccoboni kept a printed copy of the letters. Riccoboni →
  • Three per cent is still usury. Asked in 1677 whether a lender who took only three per cent a year was free of usury, a doctor of the Sorbonne said no: “any gain … however small, makes usury”, because Ezekiel “makes no distinction of greater or lesser gain”. Lamet and Fromageau →
  • The classics as rabies. Among the chief causes of rebellion, said Hobbes, is the reading of Greek and Roman political books: from them men learn that a king may be killed, provided they first call him a tyrant. He compared the disease to hydrophobia, caught from the bite of a mad dog. Hobbes →
  • Botany banned for indecency? Someone told Queen Ulrika Eleonora of Sweden that Linnaeus’s system, “based on the sex of plants”, was indecent and would corrupt the young. She was about to get a decree against him from the king, says Gioja, “had not, by a rare chance, powerful men defended the botanist at court”. Gioja →
  • Two hundred thousand billion to one. Laplace calculated that the odds against the planets all moving the same way by chance are more than two hundred thousand billion to one. Lecky quotes it as the argument for design; Laplace concluded only that “a primitive cause” was at work, and went on to look for a natural one. Laplace →

Where Lecky slipped

  • Two counts of the dead. Michelet gives Bamberg 1,500 burnings in one place and 600 in another, and Würzburg 800 and 900. Lecky took one figure from each page. Michelet →
  • Salem. “Twenty-seven persons were executed”, says Lecky. His source counts nineteen hanged and one pressed to death. Hutchinson →
  • “Soon afterwards.” Oxford’s decree for passive obedience (1683) was, says Lecky, “soon afterwards” burned by the House of Lords. It was burned in 1709. Hallam →
  • A letter a year out. Mazarin’s letter congratulating the Bishop of Évreux on the possessed nuns of Louviers is dated 1643; Lecky says 1642. Garinet →
  • A thousand examined, not burnt. Lecky says a thousand people were executed at Como in a single year. Spina says more than a thousand were examined and “more than a hundred” burnt in almost every year; only a marginal note says a thousand were burnt. Spina →
  • An emperor’s bad taste. “I am sorry to say”, writes Lecky, that the Emperor Julian praised a bagpipe in one of his epigrams. Lecky’s source reads the same epigram as the oldest description of an organ: brass pipes blown from “a leathern cavern beneath their roots”, while “a robust mortal” runs his fingers over the keys. Burney →
