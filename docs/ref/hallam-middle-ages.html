Hallam, View of the State of Europe during the Middle Ages, vol. 1

The Church and manumission (p. 221)

Author
Henry Hallam (1777–1859). Lecky cites it as “Hallam, Middle Ages, vol. i. p. 221” in Chapter VI and also cites pp. 217–218 for the causes of serfdom (note 18).
Edition used
The 1822 edition, vol. 1: archive.org/details/stateofeuropedur01hall. Printed p. 221 is image n244 (p + 23). The page number matches Lecky’s; the chapter is Chap. II, Part II (the feudal system, “Gradual abolition of villenage”).
Cited in
Rationalism, ch. VI, note 19. Notes 18 and 20 are on this page as well (pp. 217–218 and 222).
Status
The sentence Lecky quotes (p. 221) and the passages behind notes 18 (pp. 217–218) and 20 (p. 222 read; p. 223 not read), transcribed from page images and compared.

In note 19 Lecky puts a sentence of Hallam’s in quotation marks. It is Hallam’s, with three small changes: “enforced it” becomes “enforced manumission”, “equally ready” becomes “as ready”, and “church lands” becomes “the Church lands”. Hallam’s “it is said” marks the claim as hearsay, and Lecky keeps it.

“The clergy, and especially several popes” (p. 221)

English, from the page image. Highlighted: the words Lecky quotes.

… This, as is well known, was an exceedingly common practice with the Romans; and is mentioned, with certain ceremonies prescribed, in the Frankish and other early laws. The clergy, and especially several popes, enforced it as a duty upon laymen; and inveighed against the scandal of keeping Christians in bondage.* But they were not, it is said, equally ready in performing their own parts: the villeins upon church lands were among the last who were emancipated.† As society advanced in Europe, the manumission of slaves grew more frequent.‡ …

Lecky, 1865Hallam, 1822
Objectenforced manumission as a dutyenforced it as a duty (“it” = manumission, from the sentence before)
Readinessas ready in performingequally ready in performing
Punctuation… bondage; but they were not … parts. The villeins upon the Church lands were …… bondage. But they were not … parts: the villeins upon church lands were …

The changes do not alter the sense. “It” has no antecedent without the sentence before, so Lecky’s “manumission” is a sensible gloss, but he should have used brackets. The Church’s own record on emancipating villeins comes from the second sentence, which Hallam qualifies with “it is said”; Lecky’s “it is probable that this last fact in some degree lessened her zeal” (in his text, before note 19) goes a step beyond Hallam on this page.

How freemen became serfs (pp. 217–218)

English: 1822 ed., vol. 1, pp. 217–218, scan and scan.

… Where the small proprietors lost their lands by mere rapine, we may believe that their liberty was hardly less endangered. … as the labour either of artizans or of free husbandmen was but sparingly in demand, they were often compelled to exchange their liberty for bread.* In seasons also of famine, not unfrequent, many freemen sold themselves into slavery. … Many were reduced into this state through inability to pay those pecuniary compositions for offences, which were numerous and sometimes heavy in the barbarian codes of law; and many more by neglect of attendance on military expeditions of the king, the penalty of which was a fine called Heribann, with the alternative of perpetual servitude. A source of loss of liberty which may strike us as more extraordinary was superstition; men were infatuated enough to surrender themselves, as well as their properties, to churches and monasteries, in return for such benefit as they might reap by the prayers of their new masters.

Lecky (ch. VI, text above note 18): “The rapacity of the nobles, and the famines that were so frequent during the middle ages, induced the poor to exchange their liberty for protection and for bread; and the custom of punishing all crimes by fines, with the alternative of servitude in case of non-payment, still further increased the evil. At the same time the mildness of the ecclesiastical rule, and also the desire to obtain the advantage of the prayers of the monks, induced many to attach themselves as serfs to the monasteries.” Note 18: “See on all these causes Hallam’s Middle Ages, vol. i. pp. 217–218.” Every cause is on these two pages: rapine, famine, fines and the Heribann, and surrender to monasteries for their prayers. “The mildness of the ecclesiastical rule” is Lecky’s addition; Hallam attributes the surrender to superstition. “Protection” (Hallam: vassals “for the sake of his protection”) is Lecky’s wording for the lord’s part.
