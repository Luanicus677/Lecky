Paramo, De origine et progressu officii sanctae inquisitionis

Why the dead are tried for heresy

Author
Luis de Páramo (c. 1545–1608), inquisitor in Sicily (Lecky calls him “a Sicilian Inquisitor”). Lecky uses him in Chapter IV, Part II as the Inquisition’s own apologist.
Edition used
De origine et progressu officii sanctae inquisitionis, eiusque dignitate et utilitate, a 1598 printing: archive.org/details/bub_gb_mOToVY5wvVYC. Printed p. 588 (Book III, “De ordin. iudiciar. S. Officii”) is image n706 (p + 118). Lecky cites “[Madrid, 1598], p. 588”, which matches. A second 1598 copy is bub_gb_867nf0ato7UC. The translation is this edition’s own draft.
Cited in
Rationalism, ch. IV (continued), note 43.
Status
One passage transcribed from the page image, translated and compared. Not checked: Lecky’s statements in the same note that Paramo justifies confiscation by saying the heretic’s impurity falls on his relatives, and calls God “the First Inquisitor” who deprived Adam of Eden; I did not find those passages. Lecky’s second Latin quotation in that note (Farinacius, De delictis et poenis) is not on a source page: I found Farinacius scans but could not locate the passage in their poor OCR.

Lecky says the Inquisition’s trials of dead men were not “impotent malice” but aimed to rob the heirs. Paramo’s sentence, on p. 588, confirms this: one reason for proceeding against the dead heretic is that his goods may be taken “through the fisc” from his heirs or any other possessors. The Latin is Paramo’s, with one small change; Lecky leaves out the third reason, that the dead man’s body may be exhumed and burned.

“Juste enim proceditur contra defunctos hæreticos” (Book III, p. 588)

Latin: p. 588, left column, scan, after the marginal note 84 (“cui vivo communicare non licet, nec mortuo licebit”). Highlighted: Lecky’s words.

… Justè enim proceditur contra defunctos hæreticos. Primo ut memoria ejus damnetur. Secundo ut bona illius per fiscum ab hæredibus defuncti, seu à quibuslibet aliis possessoribus auferantur. Tertio, ut defunctus exhumetur, & extra locum sacrum projiciatur & comburatur.

… For it is justly proceeded against dead heretics. First, that his memory may be condemned. Second, that his goods may be taken away, through the fisc, from the heirs of the deceased or from any other possessors. Third, that the dead man may be exhumed and cast out of sacred ground and burned.

Lecky’s “damnatur” (indicative) for Paramo’s subjunctive “damnetur” is a small slip or a variant reading; I could not tell which. The omitted third reason does not weaken his point: the same page (marginal notes 83–88) says the action can be brought at any time, however long after death, that the heirs are notified, and that the dead man’s statue is paraded and handed to the secular court. The confiscation is plainly a main purpose.
