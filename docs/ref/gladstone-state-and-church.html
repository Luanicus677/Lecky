Gladstone, The State in its Relations with the Church

The future Liberal prime minister’s defence of a Church-and-State Toryism

Author
W. E. Gladstone (1809–1898), then a 28-year-old Tory MP, later four times Liberal prime minister. Macaulay’s review of this book called him “the rising hope of those stern and unbending Tories”.
Edition used
London, 1838, the first edition (Internet Archive stateinitsrelati00glad), chapter VI § 19, p. 188 = n203, matching Lecky’s page.
Cited in
Rationalism, ch. V, note 27 (“Gladstone, on Church and State, p. 188”).
Status
One passage read on the page image and transcribed.

Lecky, a Liberal writing in 1865, quotes “one of the ablest modern books in defence of the Tory theory” without naming its author in his text, and only the citation reveals it: the young Gladstone. Gladstone argues that the established Church is bound up with belief in a fixed religious truth, so that wherever Protestantism slides into latitude and toleration it turns against the union of Church and State. Lecky quotes the sentence accurately except for one word: Gladstone wrote “an immutable objective truth”. By the time Lecky quoted him, Gladstone was moving towards the Liberal leadership; four years later he disestablished the Church of Ireland.

“Where Protestantism becomes vicious” (ch. VI § 19, p. 188)

English: London 1838, p. 188, scan. Highlighted: Lecky’s quotation.

19. To speak of the influence of Protestantism as such upon the principle of union between the church and the state, to some may appear visionary or unintelligible—under many circumstances it may have been latent; but upon examination we shall find it to have been both direct and very substantial. Its character has indeed, at different stages, been very different: at first it would seem to have operated in England very favourably to this principle; and we may find that more strict regard has been paid to it, in some instances at least, by Protestant than by Romish governments. But at the point where Protestantism becomes vicious, where it receives the first tinge of latitudinarianism, and begins to join hands with infidelity, by superseding the belief of an immutable objective truth in religion necessary for salvation, at that very spot it likewise assumes an aspect of hostility to the union of church and state.
