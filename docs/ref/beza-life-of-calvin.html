Beza, Life of Calvin

Who objected to burning Servetus? More people than Lecky says

Author
Theodore Beza (1519–1605), Calvin’s successor at Geneva. His life of Calvin first appeared in French in 1564; the Latin Vita Calvini (1575) is the fuller version translated here.
Edition used
“Life of John Calvin, by Theodore Beza”, in Tracts relating to the Reformation by John Calvin, vol. I, Edinburgh: Calvin Translation Society, 1844: Internet Archive tractsrelatingto01calv; p. lxv = n70, p. lxvi = n71. Lecky cites “Beza, Vita Calvini” with no page; the passage is our identification. The Latin was not checked.
Cited in
Rationalism, vol. II, ch. IV, note 72.
Status
Read on the page images and transcribed.

Lecky: “Some years after the murder of Servetus, Beza, in relating its circumstances, declared that Castellio and Socinus were the only men who had opposed it.” Beza’s Life of Calvin does not say that. It says that as soon as Servetus’s ashes were cold, questions were raised about punishing heretics: some held that heretics “ought indeed to be coerced, but could not justly be put to death”; others that they “ought to be left to the judgment of God only”, an opinion “defended even by some good men”, who feared to seem to sanction “the cruelty of tyrants against the godly”. Castellio and Laelius Socinus were “the chief abettors of that opinion (and they were thereby pleading their own cause)”.

So Beza names Castellio and Socinus as the leaders of the opposition, not as the whole of it; he concedes that good men shared it. Lecky’s point, that open protest was rare, survives, but his “the only men” is stronger than his source. A reader of this copy of the 1844 translation underlined the passage in pencil.

“The chief abettors of that opinion” (pp. lxv–lxvi)

English: Calvin Translation Society, Edinburgh 1844, vol. I, pp. lxv–lxvi, scan n70–n71. Highlighted: what Lecky reports.

Scarcely, therefore, were the ashes of that unhappy man cold when questions began to be agitated concerning the punishment of heretics—some maintaining that they ought indeed to be coerced, but could not justly be put to death; others, as if the nature of heresy could not be clearly ascertained from the Word of God, or as if it were lawful to judge in academic fashion of all the heads of religion, maintaining that heretics ought to be left to the judgment of God only. This opinion was defended even by some good men, who were afraid that if a different view were [p. lxvi] adopted they might seem to sanction the cruelty of tyrants against the godly. The chief abettors of that opinion (and they were thereby pleading their own cause) were Sebastian Castellio and Lælius Socinus; the latter, indeed, more secretly, but the former more openly, having in a certain treatise, which he prefixed to his translation, or rather perversion, of the sacred books, plainly studied to deprive the Divine Word of clear authority …

Beza goes on to say that the anonymous book of “Martin Bellius” was Castellio’s own work, “although he afterwards swore it was not”, and that he, Beza, answered it to spare Calvin the trouble (his De haereticis of 1554). This printing dates Calvin’s refutation of Servetus “in the beginning of the year 1544”, a slip for 1554 in the translation or its source; not checked against the Latin.
