Pascal, Pensées

“Que de damner éternellement un enfant incapable de volonté” (Section VII)

Author
Blaise Pascal (1623–1662). Lecky cites “Pensées, cap. iii. § 8” (Chapter IV, note 12), the numbering of an older edition (Faugère or Havet).
Edition used
A text, not a scan: the Brunschvicg edition on French Wikisource, Section VII (“Morale et doctrine”), fragment of original sin. The fragment’s number in Brunschvicg I did not read from the page; the translation is this edition’s own draft.
Cited in
Rationalism, ch. IV, note 12.
Status
Text compared; no page image seen. Lecky’s other statements in the note (Sarpi on the Council of Trent; St Bernard’s “Nihil ardet in inferno nisi propria voluntas”) are not checked.

Lecky’s quotation agrees with the Brunschvicg text apart from older spelling (“paroît”), lighter punctuation and a different reading of the clause “sans ce mystère le plus incompréhensible de tous nous sommes incompréhensibles à nous-mêmes” (Brunschvicg sets the middle phrase off by commas). Lecky’s version ends there; Pascal continues that the knot of our condition takes its folds in this abyss, and that man is more inconceivable without the mystery than the mystery to man.

The infant damned for a sin of six thousand years ago

French: Brunschvicg ed., Section VII (French Wikisource). Highlighted: what Lecky quotes.

… Cet écoulement ne nous paraît pas seulement impossible, il nous semble même très injuste ; car qu’y a-t-il de plus contraire aux règles de notre misérable justice que de damner éternellement un enfant incapable de volonté, pour un péché où il paraît avoir si peu de part, qu’il est commis six mille ans avant qu’il fût en être ? Certainement rien ne nous heurte plus rudement que cette doctrine ; et cependant, sans ce mystère, le plus incompréhensible de tous, nous sommes incompréhensibles à nous-mêmes. Le nœud de notre condition prend ses replis et ses tours dans cet abîme ; de sorte que l’homme est plus inconcevable sans ce mystère que ce mystère n’est inconcevable à l’homme. …

… This flowing down does not merely seem impossible to us, it seems to us very unjust; for what is more contrary to the rules of our wretched justice than to damn eternally a child incapable of will, for a sin in which it appears to have had so little part that it was committed six thousand years before it was in being? Certainly nothing shocks us more roughly than this doctrine; and yet, without this mystery, the most incomprehensible of all, we are incomprehensible to ourselves. The knot of our condition takes its folds and turns in this abyss; so that man is more inconceivable without this mystery than this mystery is inconceivable to man. …

Lecky introduces it as Pascal “stating the doctrine in a very repulsive form”, which is how the passage reads; Pascal’s purpose is to show that the doctrine, hard as it is, explains man to himself.
