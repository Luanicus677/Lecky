Cudworth, The True Intellectual System of the Universe, with Mosheim’s notes

“Not one of them entertained the same opinion …” (vol. 3, p. 325)

Author
Ralph Cudworth (1617–1688), with the notes of J. L. von Mosheim, translated in the edition of J. Harrison (London, 1845). Lecky: “Note by Mosheim to Cudworth’s Intell. System (Harrison’s ed.), vol. iii. p. 325” (Chapter III, note 164).
Edition used
A Google scan of the 1845 Harrison edition (volume 3, “Soul after death” on the running head): Internet Archive trueintellectua00moshgoog; p. 325 = image n334 (image = page + 9). Lecky’s page number matches.
Cited in
Rationalism, ch. III, note 164.
Status
p. 325 read on the image. The text is Mosheim’s section III of the note; the first page of the note is p. 324.

Lecky’s long quotation is accurate in substance, with ellipses. The page reads “Not one of them entertained the same opinion as the majority of Christians do at the present day, that it is perfectly simple, and entirely destitute of all body, figure, form, and extension”; Lecky has “that the soul is perfectly simple”. Mosheim’s “For some contend that there are two things in the soul, spirit and a very thin and subtle body in which this spirit is clothed” and the Platonist/anti-Platonist division agree with Lecky’s version. Mosheim’s “those who follow Plato and the Platonists, for example Clement, Origen, and their disciples” is Lecky’s “(i.e. Clement, Origen, and their disciples)”.

The Fathers on the corporeity of the soul

English: vol. 3, p. 325, scan. Highlighted: what Lecky quotes.

III. But as Dr. Cudworth in the sequel again appeals to the testimony of the ancient Christian fathers, it may be worth while to give a brief exposition of their sentiments generally on the nature of the soul. Not one of them entertained the same opinion as the majority of Christians do at the present day, that it is perfectly simple, and entirely destitute of all body, figure, form, and extension. On the contrary, they all acknowledge it to contain something corporeal, although of a different kind and nature from the bodies of this mortal sphere. But yet they are divided into two opinions. For some contend that there are two things in the soul, spirit and a very thin and subtle body in which this spirit is clothed: while others hold it to be altogether corporeal, and nothing more than a certain subtle and aerial body. … For those who follow Plato and the Platonists, for example Clement, Origen, and their disciples, adopt the Platonic doctrine respecting the soul also, and pronounce it to be most simple in itself, but yet always invested with a subtle body. But the others who keep farther aloof from Plato, and consider his philosophy to be prejudicial to Christian principles, repudiate this doctrine of his as well, and maintain that the soul altogether is nothing more than a most subtle body. … They very frequently assail the Platonists with bitter invectives, for inculcating that the soul is a nature most simple in itself, and devoid of all concretion …

Lecky’s version: “‘Not one of them (the early Fathers) entertained the same opinion as the majority of Christians do at the present day, that the soul is perfectly simple …’” with “(the early Fathers)” and “the soul” inserted for Mosheim’s “it”; “Platonists (i.e. Clement, Origen, and their disciples)” is Lecky’s parenthesis. The sentence “I am aware that some of them pronounce the soul to be a mere spirit …” is passed over by Lecky.
