Pitcairn, Ancient Criminal Trials in Scotland

Confessions taken before presbyteries (vol. 3, p. 598)

Author
Robert Pitcairn (1793–1855), editor. Lecky: “Pitcairn’s Criminal Trials of Scotland, a vast repository of original documents … (Vol. iii. p. 598.)” (Chapter I, note 149) and “Pitcairn, vol. iii. p. 598” (note 162).
Edition used
Edinburgh, 1833, vol. 3 (the Appendix to part II, “Witchcraft”): Internet Archive ancientcrim43pt203pitcuoft; p. 598 = image n249 (image = page − 349). Lecky’s page number matches.
Cited in
Rationalism, ch. I, note 149; note 162 cites the same page (see below).
Status
p. 598 read on the image. Lecky’s “Pitcairn gives numbers of these confessions” is not checked.

Lecky’s quotation (“The confessions were commonly taken before presbyteries, or certain special commissioners, who usually ranked among their number the leading clergy of those districts where their hapless victims resided”) is Pitcairn’s sentence from the editor’s introduction to the Confessions of Isobel Gowdie and Janet Breadhead, with “Such declarations” changed to “The confessions” and Lecky’s lower-case. The same page also says that matters were “no better in England”, under Charles I, Charles II and the Puritans, with Hopkins the Witch-finder (Pitcairn’s footnote refers to Scott’s Letters on Demonology and Witchcraft, 1830).

Note 162 cites p. 598 for a sentence about the Earl of Mar being “bled to death” (that sentence cites Scott’s Demonology, letter ix.). Nothing about the Earl of Mar is on this page; the note’s “Pitcairn, vol. iii. p. 598” probably belongs to the note that came before it.

Confessions taken before presbyteries (p. 598)

English: Appendix, p. 598, scan. Highlighted: Lecky’s quotation.

… The Editor has been induced, in compliance with the repeated desire of literary friends, contrary to his own private opinion, to annex to this work the Confessions of these unhappy women, and a few other similar Examinations. Such declarations were commonly taken before Presbyteries, or certain Special Commissioners, who usually ranked among their number the leading Clergy of those districts where their hapless victims resided. The details of all such cases, and, in particular, those which are now annexed, are repulsive and shocking to every well-regulated mind; but, as recording the superstitions and crimes common at the period in question to all the civilized …

Lecky (ch. I, note 149): “The confessions were commonly taken before presbyteries, or certain special commissioners, who usually ranked among their number the leading clergy of those districts where their hapless victims resided.” Matches except for the opening words and capitals. On the same page, before this passage, Pitcairn quotes a Scottish chronicle on witches burnt alive and says it is hardly necessary to remind the reader that matters were no better in England.
