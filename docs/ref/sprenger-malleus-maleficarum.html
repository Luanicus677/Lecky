Sprenger, Malleus Maleficarum

Malleus maleficarum, attributed to Jakob Sprenger and Heinrich Kramer

Work
Malleus maleficarum (first printed 1486), the inquisitors’ handbook on witchcraft, in three parts (Pars). Lecky calls it “Sprenger” and cites it as “Mall. Mal.”. Early editions credit Jakob Sprenger and Heinrich Kramer; this page follows Lecky in saying “Sprenger” only where he does.
Edition used
Lyon, 1620, vol. I (Pars I–III) (scan); the edition Lecky’s volume and page numbers fit (see the edition note on the Grillandus page). The printed page numbers below are from the page images.
Cited in
Rationalism in Europe, ch. I, note 21 (the “angels” and the hair) and note 22 (exorcism at baptism); also note 68 (etymologies), note 78 (Serenus and Equitius) and note 79 (Silvanus); other Malleus citations are in notes 37, 80 and 82–84 (not yet transcribed)
Status
Eight passages transcribed and translated (the first three and the five added for notes 68, 78 and 79), plus the Cologne approbation. Translation is this edition’s own draft.

The Malleus is the best-known manual of the witch-hunters, and Lecky uses it as his chief witness for what inquisitors believed. In notes 21 and 22 he cites it for two details: that St. Paul’s instruction that women cover their heads “because of the angels” was read as a reference to incubi, and that exorcism before baptism was a later addition to the rite.

Two of Lecky’s three statements are close to the print. The third attributes to the Malleus a claim it does not make in so many words, and the first cites the wrong question number for this edition. See the comparisons below.

Notes 68, 78 and 79 add the etymologies of femina, maleficus and diabolus, the story of St. Silvanus, and the angels’ treatment of Serenus and Equitius. The etymologies are word for word; the Silvanus story is retold with one invented detail (the Devil is “dragged out”); and the Serenus and Equitius stories are in Nider’s text at the end of the volume, not in the place Lecky cites. See the second comparison.

Pars I, Quaestio III, p. 32: “Because of the Angels”

Latin: Lyon, 1620, vol. I, Pars I, Quaestio III (“An per Daemones detur procreatio” in the running title), p. 32 (scan). Spelling regularised: u/v and æ normalised and abbreviations expanded. The right-hand margin carries the note “Mulieres velantur propter Incubos” (“Women are veiled because of incubi”). Highlighted: the sentence Lecky reports.

Insuper illud Apostoli, 1. Cor. 11, “Mulier debet habere velamen super caput suum propter Angelos”, multi Catholici exponunt quod sequitur “propter Angelos”, id est Incubos. Ad idem est Beda in Historiis Angelorum. Item Guliel. in lib. de universo, par. ultima, tractat. 6, multipliciter. Praeterea hoc determinat S. Doctor prima par. q. 25, et in secundo scripto di. 8, et Quodlibet. 6, quo atque super Esaiam c. 13 et 14. Unde talia negare, dicit S. Tho., imprudentiae est. Id enim quod multis videtur, non potest omnino falsum esse, secundum Philosophum, de somno et vigilia, in fine, et in 2. Ethicorum. Sileo de historiis multis et authenticis tam Catholicorum quam Ethnicorum, qui Incubos esse palam asserunt.

Moreover, as to the Apostle’s saying, 1 Cor. 11, “A woman ought to have a veil upon her head because of the angels”, many Catholics explain the words that follow, “because of the angels”, as meaning incubi. Bede, in his Histories of the Angels, is to the same effect. So too William, in the book On the Universe, last part, treatise 6, at some length. The Holy Doctor also decides this in the first part, q. 25, in the second Scriptum, dist. 8, and in Quodlibet 6, and in his commentary on Isaiah 13 and 14. Hence to deny such things, says St. Thomas, is a mark of imprudence; for what seems so to many cannot be wholly false, according to the Philosopher, in the end of On Sleep and Waking and in book 2 of the Ethics. I say nothing of the many authentic histories, of Catholics as of pagans, which openly assert that there are incubi.

Doubtful readings and untraced references

The phrase “quo atque super Esaiam” is read from a dark page and may be wrong. “Beda in Historiis Angelorum” is as printed; no work of that title by Bede has been identified here. The Thomist and Aristotelian citations (“S. Doctor”, “the Philosopher”) are given as printed and have not been traced to their texts. The question number is taken from the running heads of the two preceding pages, which read “Quaestio III”; the page itself carries only the shorter title “An per Daemones detur proc. &c.”.

Pars II, Quaestio II, Cap. I, p. 273: Incubi and Women’s Hair

Latin: Lyon, 1620, vol. I, Pars II, Quaestio II, cap. I, p. 273 (scan), conventions as above. The writer is not Sprenger: the sentence reports “Guilhelmus” (William of Paris, as the same text is credited in the Formicarius printed later in the volume). The margin note reads “Puellae pulchros crines habentes magis sentiunt incubos” (“Girls with beautiful hair feel incubi the more”). Highlighted: the clause that bears on Lecky’s sentence.

Notatur etiam a Guilhelmo quod Incubi plus vexare videntur mulieres et puellas pulchros crines habentes, ideo quia aut curae aut ornatui crinium incumbunt huiusmodi, aut quia per crines viros inflammare cupiunt vel consueverunt, aut quia in eis inaniter gloriantur, aut quia divina bonitas hoc permittit, ut terreantur mulieres viros inflammare per ea quae etiam Daemones inflammari viros volunt.

It is also noted by William that incubi are seen to vex more those women and girls who have beautiful hair, either because such women devote themselves to the care or adornment of their hair, or because they wish, or are accustomed, to inflame men by their hair, or because they take empty pride in it, or because divine goodness permits it, so that women may be frightened off inflaming men by the very things by which the Demons also wish men to be inflamed.

Pars II, Quaestio II, Cap. VI, pp. 300 and 305: Exorcism Before Baptism

Latin: Lyon, 1620, vol. I, Pars II, Quaestio II, cap. VI, p. 300 (scan) and p. 305 (scan), conventions as above. “[…]” marks omitted text. The margin note on p. 305 reads “In primitiva Ecclesia sine exorcismo baptismus peragebatur” (“In the primitive Church baptism was carried out without exorcism”). Highlighted: the sentences that bear on Lecky’s note.

P. 300. […] Unde cum prima satisfactio qua homo Deo consecratur sit in baptismo, oportet quod etiam homo prius exorcizetur quam baptizetur, multo fortiori ratione quam aliae res. Quia in ipso homine est causa quare Diabolus potestatem accepit in alia quae sunt propter hominem, scilicet peccatum originale vel actuale. […]

P. 305. Nec obiectio valet quod sine exorcismis in primitiva Ecclesia baptizabantur, aut etiam quod nunc characterem baptismalem sine exorcismo valeat baptizatus recipere, quia sic Gregorius in vanum exorcismos instituisset, et Ecclesia in suis caeremoniis potius erraret. Quare ne ausus sum omnino reprehendere qui maleficiatos sub conditione vellent rebaptizare, et neglecta fortassis recuperare.

P. 300. […] Hence, since the first dedication by which a man is consecrated to God is in baptism, a man must also be exorcised before he is baptised, by a much stronger reason than other things. For the reason why the Devil received power over other things that exist for man’s sake lies in man himself: namely original or actual sin. […]

P. 305. Nor is it a valid objection that in the primitive Church people were baptised without exorcisms, or that even now a baptised person can receive the baptismal character without exorcism; for in that case Gregory would have instituted exorcisms in vain, and the Church would be erring rather than right in her ceremonies. Therefore I have not dared altogether to censure those who would rebaptise the bewitched on condition, and so perhaps recover what had been neglected.

Lecky’s note (note 22) says that he did not identify the ancient exorcists’ inscriptions with those of the baptismal exorcists, “because Sprenger asserts that the employment of exorcising at baptisms was not introduced till a later period (Mall. Mal. Pars ii. Quaest. 2). Sprenger does not give his authority, but as he is usually well informed on matters of tradition, and as he treats the omission as a difficulty, I have adopted his view.” Set against the print:

Pars I, Quaestio VI, p. 65: “Femina” from Fe and minus

Latin: Lyon, 1620, vol. I, Pars I, Quaestio VI (p. 65; the running titles of the following pages read “Cur foem. superstitiosa reperiantur”; scan). Conventions as above. The right margin has the note “Foemina dicta à Fe, & Minus”. The middle of the page (the sentences on Terence, Lactantius, Proverbs, the creation of Eve, Cato and Samson’s wife) is omitted here; it is marked by dots. Highlighted: the etymology Lecky reports.

Sed quia adhuc modernis temporibus haec perfidia amplius in mulieribus, quam in viris invenitur, ut ipsa experientia docet, curiosius causam investigando, ultra praemissa dicere possumus, quod in omnibus viribus, tam animae, quam corporis, cum sint defectuosae, non mirum, si plura maleficia in eos, quos aemulantur, fieri procurant. …

Patet in prima muliere, quod ex natura minorem habet fidem, cum dixit serpenti interroganti, quare non ederent de omni ligno paradisi. Respondit de omni, &c. ne forte moriamur: in quo ostendit se dubitare, & fidem non habere ad verba Dei: quae omnia etiam Etymologia nominis demonstrat. Dicitur enim Foemina à Fe & minus: quia semper minorem habet & servat fidem: Et hoc ex natura, quod ad fidelitatem: licet ex gratia & natura fides in beatissima virgine nunquam defecerit, cum tamen in omnibus viris defecisset tempore Passionis Christi.

But since in modern times this perfidy is found more among women than among men, as experience itself teaches, we may say, investigating the cause more curiously, beyond what has been said above, that since women are defective in all the powers of soul as of body, it is no wonder that they procure more acts of witchcraft against those whom they envy. …

It is plain in the first woman, that by nature she has less faith, since when the serpent asked her why they did not eat of every tree of paradise, she answered “Of every”, etc., “lest perhaps we die”: in which she showed that she doubted and had no faith in the words of God. All of which the etymology of the name also demonstrates. For Femina is said to come from Fe and minus, because she always has and keeps less faith: and this by nature, as regards fidelity; although by grace and nature faith never failed in the most blessed Virgin, whereas in all the men it had failed at the time of Christ’s Passion.

Pars I, Quaestio IV, p. 41: The Names of the Devil

Latin: Lyon, 1620, vol. I, Pars I, Quaestio IV, the page Lecky cites as p. 41 (scan). The page number in the print is smudged in this scan (the first digit is a 4; the second is unclear), so “p. 41” is matched here by content, not by reading the number. Conventions as above. The right margin has “Diabolus, à Dia & Bolus”. Highlighted: what Lecky quotes.

Est enim usus scripturae & locutionis, quemlibet immundum Spiritum nominare Diabolum, à Dia, quod est duo, & bolus quod est morsellus: quia duo occidit, scilicet corpus & animam. Et secundum etymologiam licet Graece interpretetur Diabolus clausus ergastulo: & hoc sibi convenit, cum non permittitur sibi nocere quantum vellet. Vel Diabolus quasi defluens: quia defluxit, id est, corruit, & specialiter & localiter. Nominatur etiam Daemon, id est sapiens super sanguinem, vel sanguineus …

For it is the usage of Scripture and of speech to call any unclean spirit a Devil (Diabolus), from dia, which is “two”, and bolus, which is “a morsel”: because he kills two things, namely body and soul. And according to the etymology, although it is interpreted in Greek, Diabolus is “shut up in a prison”: and this suits him, since he is not permitted to do harm as much as he would wish. Or Diabolus as if “flowing down”: because he has flowed down, that is, fallen, both in rank and in place. He is also named Demon, that is “wise above blood”, or “bloody” …

Pars II, Quaestio I, cap. XI: St. Silvanus and the Devil

Latin: Lyon, 1620, vol. I, Pars II, Quaestio I, Caput XI (scan; the running title reads “Par. II. Quaest. I. Cap. XI.”, and the page number is cut off at the top of this scan). Conventions as above. The right margin has “Satan se transformat in Siluanum Nazarenum Episcopum”. Highlighted: the story Lecky retells. The sentence begins in a list of the harms that witches, with the Devil’s help, can do: to property (Job), to reputation (this story), to the body (Job’s leprosy).

De rebus namque patet Iob. 1. & 2. ut supra patuit. De fama patet in Legenda B. Hieronymi, ut refert Io. And. in Hieronymiano, quod Diabolus se transformavit in formam S. Siluani Episcopi in Nazareth, amatoris Hieronymi. Qui Daemon primo foeminam nobilem nocte in lecto procare & provocare coepit ad luxuriam verbis, & deinde factis sollicitavit ad malum, quae cum clamaret, Daemon sub specie sancti Episcopi sub lecto foeminae se abscondit, ubi requisitus & inventus per luxuriosa verba se mentitus est esse Episcopum Siluanum. In crastinum ergo sanctus vir, disparente Diabolo diffamatus est gravissime: quae diffamia tandem circa sepulchrum San. Hieronymi fatente Diabolo in obsesso corpore purgata est.

For as to property, it is plain from Job 1 and 2, as was shown above. As to reputation, it is plain in the Legend of Blessed Jerome, as John Andreae relates in the Hieronymianus (“Io. And. in Hieronymiano”), that the Devil transformed himself into the form of St. Silvanus, bishop at Nazareth, a lover of Jerome. This Demon began first, by night in bed, to solicit and provoke a noblewoman to lust with words, and then by deeds urged her to evil; and when she cried out, the Demon, in the appearance of the holy Bishop, hid himself under the woman’s bed, where, being sought and found, he gave himself out by lustful words to be Bishop Silvanus. On the next day, therefore, the holy man was most gravely defamed once the Devil had disappeared: which defamation was at last cleared, at the tomb of St. Jerome, when the Devil confessed in a possessed body.

Doubtful points

“Io. And.” is expanded as “John Andreae”, probably Giovanni d’Andrea’s Hieronymianus; I have not checked this against that work. “Procare” (for procari, “to solicit”) is as printed. “Amatoris Hieronymi” could also be read “a friend of Jerome”. The page number is lost, so the Lecky reference rests on the chapter number, which agrees.

Nider’s Formicarius in the Same Volume, pp. 503–504: Serenus and Equitius

Latin: Lyon, 1620, vol. I, p. 503 (scan), within the extracts from Johannes Nider’s Formicarius (running title “Formic. de malefic. decept.”) that this volume prints after the Malleus. Conventions as above. The speakers are Piger (“the Lazy One”) and Theologus. Highlighted: the angelic operations Lecky mentions. This is not the place Lecky points to: his note cites “Pars I. Quaest. vii” of Sprenger, then Nider “c. v.”; the page of Pars I, Quaestio VII that I looked at (p. 77) has nothing of the kind, and I did not read the rest of that question.

Piger. Licet haec sint innumerabilia bonorum Angelorum etiam in iniustos profluxa beneficia, de quibus heu nescius fui hactenus, sed gratus ero in antea, tamen de his non quaero principaliter, sed de beneficiis eorum circa generativam potentiam, ut puta an quosdam castificent, sicut mali spiritus quosdam malos infrigidant per sua maleficia.

Theologus. Haud dubie ita faciunt, sed ubi & quando expedit. Nam ita factum est de S. Sereno, abbate, de quo refert Cass. in coll. par. abba. Sereni, prima. Hic, inquit, pro interna cordis atque animae castitate nocturnis, diurnisque precibus, ieiuniis quoque ac vigiliis infatigabiliter insistens, cunctos aestus carnalis concupiscentiae tandem per divinam gratiam in se extinxisse percepit: Deinde maiori zelo castitatis succensus, praefatis usus remediis, à Deo petiit, ut interioris hominis castitas in corpus redundaret Dei dono; postremo autem adveniens ad eum Angelus in visione nocturna, eiusque velut aperiens uterum, quandam ignitam carnis strumam, de eius visceribus evellens ac proiiciens, suisque omnia, ut fuerant, locis intestina restituens: Ecce, inquit, incentiva carnis tuae abscissa sunt, & obtinuisse te noveris hodierna die perpetuam corporis puritatem, iuxta votum quo id poposcisti, ut ne ipse quidem naturali motu, qui etiam in parvulis atque lactantibus evitatur, ulterius pulseris.

Sicut de B. Equitio Abbate dicit B. Gregorius primo libro Dialogorum. Hunc, inquit, cum iuventutis suae tempore acri certamine carnis incentiva fatigarent, ipsae suae tentationis angustiae ad orationis studium solertiorem fecerunt. Cumque hac in re ab omnipotenti Deo remedium continuis precib. quaereret, nocte quadam assistente Angelo eunuchari se vidit, eiusque visioni apparuit, quod omnem motum ex genitalibus membris eius absciderit, atque ex eo tempore ita fuit alienus à tentatione ac si sexum non haberet in corpore. Qua virtute fretus ex Dei omnipotentis auxilio ut vitiis ante praeerat, ita coepit postmodum etiam foeminis praeesse.

Piger. Although these benefits of the good angels, flowing even to the unjust, are innumerable, and I was unaware of them till now (but shall be grateful hereafter), I am not asking mainly about those, but about their benefits regarding the generative power: for instance, whether they make some men chaste, as evil spirits make some wicked men cold by their witchcraft.

Theologus. Without doubt they do so, but where and when it is expedient. For so it happened with St. Serenus, abbot, of whom Cassian tells in the first Collation of Abbot Serenus. He, it says, persisting tirelessly in prayers by night and day, fasts and vigils, for the inner chastity of heart and soul, at length perceived that by divine grace he had put out in himself all the heats of carnal concupiscence. Then, kindled with greater zeal for chastity, and using the aforesaid remedies, he asked God that the chastity of the inner man might overflow into the body by God’s gift; and at last an Angel came to him in a night vision, and, as if opening his belly, plucked out of his viscera a kind of fiery lump of flesh and threw it away, and put all the intestines back in their places as they had been, saying: “Behold, the incentives of your flesh have been cut away, and know that today you have obtained perpetual purity of body, according to the vow with which you asked for it, so that you shall not be stirred even by that natural motion which is avoided even in infants and sucklings.”

So, too, of Blessed Equitius, abbot, Blessed Gregory says in book 1 of the Dialogues: when in his youth the incentives of the flesh troubled him with fierce struggle, the very anguish of his temptation made him more zealous for prayer. And when in this matter he sought a remedy from Almighty God with continual prayers, one night, with an Angel standing by, he saw himself made a eunuch; and it appeared in his vision that the angel had cut off all motion from his genital members; and from that time he was as free from temptation as if he had no sex in his body. Relying on this power, with the help of Almighty God, as he had before ruled over vices, so he began afterwards to rule over women too.

On the next page (p. 504) Nider tells a third story, of a monk called Helias whom three angels “made a eunuch”; there the text adds that the testicles seemed to be cut off, “not that it was really so, but because it seemed so”. Lecky does not mention Helias.

Pp. 461–462: The Cologne Approbation, and the Preachers

Latin: Lyon, 1620, vol. I, pp. 461–462 (scan, p. 461; p. 462), conventions as above. These pages are the preface to the approbation of the Malleus by the University of Cologne: the inquisitors’ own account of why they compiled the tract, before the doctors’ signatures (from p. 463). The sentence runs across the page break. Highlighted: the words Lecky’s “rebellion” rests on.

P. 461. Et quia nonnulli animarum rectores, et verbi Dei praedicatores, publice in eorum sermonibus ad populum asserere et affirmare non verebantur

P. 462. maleficas non esse, aut qui nihil in nocumentum creaturarum quacunque operatione efficere possent. Ex quibus incautis sermonibus nonnunquam saeculari brachio ad puniendum huiusmodi maleficas amputabatur facultas: et hoc in maximum augmentum maleficarum et confortationem illius haereseos. Ideo praefati inquisitores, totis eorum viribus, cunctis periculis et insultibus obviare volentes, tractatum quendam non tam studiose quam laboriose collegerunt. P. 461. And because some rectors of souls and preachers of the word of God did not fear to assert and affirm publicly in their sermons to the people

P. 462. that witches do not exist, or that they could do no harm to creatures by any operation. From these incautious sermons it sometimes happened that the secular arm was deprived of the power to punish such witches, and this was a very great increase of witches and a strengthening of that heresy. Therefore the aforesaid inquisitors, wishing to meet all dangers and insults with all their might, compiled a certain treatise, no less laboriously than carefully. […] But since it is agreeable to reason that things done for the common good should also be strengthened by the common approval of the Doctors, […] they offered it to the University of Cologne, or to some professors of sacred theology there, to be discussed and collated, so that if anything blameworthy, out of tune with Catholic truth, were found, it might be refuted by their judgement, and what was in harmony with Catholic truth might be approved.

Lecky’s sentence (note 83, p. 92) is: “An assembly of doctors of the University of Cologne, which was held in 1487, lamented, and severely and authoritatively condemned, a still more startling instance of rebellion, arising from a quarter in which it was least to be expected. When the panic was raging most fiercely in the diocese of Cologne, some priests had attempted to allay the alarm by questioning the reality of the crime.” Set against the print:

Lecky has turned the inquisitors’ complaint about incautious preachers into a condemnation by the Cologne doctors of a “rebellion”. The facts of the preachers’ statement are right; the framing and the doctors’ part are his own.
