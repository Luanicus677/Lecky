Sprenger, Malleus Maleficarum

Malleus maleficarum, attributed to Jakob Sprenger and Heinrich Kramer

Work
Malleus maleficarum (first printed 1486), the inquisitors’ handbook on witchcraft, in three parts (Pars). Lecky calls it “Sprenger” and cites it as “Mall. Mal.”. Early editions credit Jakob Sprenger and Heinrich Kramer; this page follows Lecky in saying “Sprenger” only where he does.
Edition used
Lyon, 1620, vol. I (Pars I–III) (scan); the edition Lecky’s volume and page numbers fit (see the edition note on the Grillandus page). The printed page numbers below are from the page images.
Cited in
Rationalism in Europe, ch. I, note 21 (the “angels” and the hair) and note 22 (exorcism at baptism); other Malleus citations are in notes 37, 78, 79, 80 and 82–84 (not yet transcribed)
Status
Three passages transcribed and translated. Translation is this edition’s own draft.

The Malleus is the best-known manual of the witch-hunters, and Lecky uses it as his chief witness for what inquisitors believed. In notes 21 and 22 he cites it for two details: that St. Paul’s instruction that women cover their heads “because of the angels” was read as a reference to incubi, and that exorcism before baptism was a later addition to the rite.

Two of Lecky’s three statements are close to the print. The third attributes to the Malleus a claim it does not make in so many words, and the first cites the wrong question number for this edition. See the comparisons below.

Pars I, Quaestio III, p. 32: “Because of the Angels”

Latin: Lyon, 1620, vol. I, Pars I, Quaestio III (“An per Daemones detur procreatio” in the running title), p. 32 (scan). Spelling regularised: u/v and æ normalised and abbreviations expanded. The right-hand margin carries the note “Mulieres velantur propter Incubos” (“Women are veiled because of incubi”). Highlighted: the sentence Lecky reports.

Insuper illud Apostoli, 1. Cor. 11, “Mulier debet habere velamen super caput suum propter Angelos”, multi Catholici exponunt quod sequitur “propter Angelos”, id est Incubos. Ad idem est Beda in Historiis Angelorum. Item Guliel. in lib. de universo, par. ultima, tractat. 6, multipliciter. Praeterea hoc determinat S. Doctor prima par. q. 25, et in secundo scripto di. 8, et Quodlibet. 6, quo atque super Esaiam c. 13 et 14. Unde talia negare, dicit S. Tho., imprudentiae est. Id enim quod multis videtur, non potest omnino falsum esse, secundum Philosophum, de somno et vigilia, in fine, et in 2. Ethicorum. Sileo de historiis multis et authenticis tam Catholicorum quam Ethnicorum, qui Incubos esse palam asserunt.

Moreover, as to the Apostle’s saying, 1 Cor. 11, “A woman ought to have a veil upon her head because of the angels”, many Catholics explain the words that follow, “because of the angels”, as meaning incubi. Bede, in his Histories of the Angels, is to the same effect. So too William, in the book On the Universe, last part, treatise 6, at some length. The Holy Doctor also decides this in the first part, q. 25, in the second Scriptum, dist. 8, and in Quodlibet 6, and in his commentary on Isaiah 13 and 14. Hence to deny such things, says St. Thomas, is a mark of imprudence; for what seems so to many cannot be wholly false, according to the Philosopher, in the end of On Sleep and Waking and in book 2 of the Ethics. I say nothing of the many authentic histories, of Catholics as of pagans, which openly assert that there are incubi.

Doubtful readings and untraced references

The phrase “quo atque super Esaiam” is read from a dark page and may be wrong. “Beda in Historiis Angelorum” is as printed; no work of that title by Bede has been identified here. The Thomist and Aristotelian citations (“S. Doctor”, “the Philosopher”) are given as printed and have not been traced to their texts. The question number is taken from the running heads of the two preceding pages, which read “Quaestio III”; the page itself carries only the shorter title “An per Daemones detur proc. &c.”.

Pars II, Quaestio II, Cap. I, p. 273: Incubi and Women’s Hair

Latin: Lyon, 1620, vol. I, Pars II, Quaestio II, cap. I, p. 273 (scan), conventions as above. The writer is not Sprenger: the sentence reports “Guilhelmus” (William of Paris, as the same text is credited in the Formicarius printed later in the volume). The margin note reads “Puellae pulchros crines habentes magis sentiunt incubos” (“Girls with beautiful hair feel incubi the more”). Highlighted: the clause that bears on Lecky’s sentence.

Notatur etiam a Guilhelmo quod Incubi plus vexare videntur mulieres et puellas pulchros crines habentes, ideo quia aut curae aut ornatui crinium incumbunt huiusmodi, aut quia per crines viros inflammare cupiunt vel consueverunt, aut quia in eis inaniter gloriantur, aut quia divina bonitas hoc permittit, ut terreantur mulieres viros inflammare per ea quae etiam Daemones inflammari viros volunt.

It is also noted by William that incubi are seen to vex more those women and girls who have beautiful hair, either because such women devote themselves to the care or adornment of their hair, or because they wish, or are accustomed, to inflame men by their hair, or because they take empty pride in it, or because divine goodness permits it, so that women may be frightened off inflaming men by the very things by which the Demons also wish men to be inflamed.

Lecky’s sentence (note 21) is: “It was observed that they had a peculiar attachment to women with beautiful hair; and it was an old Catholic belief that St. Paul alluded to this in that somewhat curious passage, in which he exhorts women to cover their heads because of the ‘angels’ (Sprenger, Mall. Mal. Pars i. Quaest. 4; and Pars ii. Quaest. 2).” Set against the two passages:

Pars II, Quaestio II, Cap. VI, pp. 300 and 305: Exorcism Before Baptism

Latin: Lyon, 1620, vol. I, Pars II, Quaestio II, cap. VI, p. 300 (scan) and p. 305 (scan), conventions as above. “[…]” marks omitted text. The margin note on p. 305 reads “In primitiva Ecclesia sine exorcismo baptismus peragebatur” (“In the primitive Church baptism was carried out without exorcism”). Highlighted: the sentences that bear on Lecky’s note.

P. 300. […] Unde cum prima satisfactio qua homo Deo consecratur sit in baptismo, oportet quod etiam homo prius exorcizetur quam baptizetur, multo fortiori ratione quam aliae res. Quia in ipso homine est causa quare Diabolus potestatem accepit in alia quae sunt propter hominem, scilicet peccatum originale vel actuale. […]

P. 305. Nec obiectio valet quod sine exorcismis in primitiva Ecclesia baptizabantur, aut etiam quod nunc characterem baptismalem sine exorcismo valeat baptizatus recipere, quia sic Gregorius in vanum exorcismos instituisset, et Ecclesia in suis caeremoniis potius erraret. Quare ne ausus sum omnino reprehendere qui maleficiatos sub conditione vellent rebaptizare, et neglecta fortassis recuperare.

P. 300. […] Hence, since the first dedication by which a man is consecrated to God is in baptism, a man must also be exorcised before he is baptised, by a much stronger reason than other things. For the reason why the Devil received power over other things that exist for man’s sake lies in man himself: namely original or actual sin. […]

P. 305. Nor is it a valid objection that in the primitive Church people were baptised without exorcisms, or that even now a baptised person can receive the baptismal character without exorcism; for in that case Gregory would have instituted exorcisms in vain, and the Church would be erring rather than right in her ceremonies. Therefore I have not dared altogether to censure those who would rebaptise the bewitched on condition, and so perhaps recover what had been neglected.

Lecky’s note (note 22) says that he did not identify the ancient exorcists’ inscriptions with those of the baptismal exorcists, “because Sprenger asserts that the employment of exorcising at baptisms was not introduced till a later period (Mall. Mal. Pars ii. Quaest. 2). Sprenger does not give his authority, but as he is usually well informed on matters of tradition, and as he treats the omission as a difficulty, I have adopted his view.” Set against the print:

Lecky, 1865Lyon, 1620
The claimSprenger “asserts” that exorcism at baptisms “was not introduced till a later period”p. 305 does not say this in so many words. It admits that the early Church baptised without exorcism (the margin note says so outright), and it implies that exorcisms were later “instituted” by a Gregory (Gregorius … exorcismos instituisset); the passage is in effect an answer to the objection, not an assertion of the late date.

Lecky’s summary is a fair reading of the substance, since the passage does concede that the early Church had no baptismal exorcism. “Asserts” is stronger than the text. Only these two pages were read; a plainer statement on another page of the same chapter has not been ruled out.
