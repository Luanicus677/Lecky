Bodin, Démonomanie des Sorciers

Jean Bodin, De la démonomanie des sorciers

Author
Jean Bodin (c. 1530–1596), French jurist and political philosopher, author of Les Six livres de la République (Wikipedia)
Work
De la démonomanie des sorciers, first published in Paris by Jacques du Puys in 1580. It has four books, followed by a “Réfutation des opinions de Jean Wier”. Lecky dates it 1581; the Internet Archive holds printings of 1580, 1582, and 1587, and this page uses the 1582 one.
Edition used
Paris: Jacques du Puys, 1582, as dated by the Internet Archive record (scan). Lecky’s page numbers for pp. 15, 40, 152, 217, 228 and 252 all agree with this printing’s pagination (these are folio numbers, so each covers a recto and a verso image: p. 40 = n102, p. 217 = n468 with its verso n469, p. 228 = n490; the offset is not constant, so find each page from the OCR).
Cited in
Rationalism in Europe, ch. I, note 74 (p. 15) and note 89 (p. 152); also note 68 (p. 40), note 88 (pp. 217, 228), and note 91 (p. 252, the third passage below)
Status
Six passages transcribed and translated (pp. 15, 152, 252, 40, 217–218, 228). Translation is this edition’s own draft.

Bodin’s book, a huge compilation of authorities and trial reports in French, was the standard reply to Wier’s attack on the witch trials. Lecky devotes pp. 95–99 of Chapter I to it as the sceptics’ chief opponent, and cites it for a handful of details and quotations.

The three passages here are the ones Lecky gives page numbers for and quotes in French or paraphrases closely. Lecky modernises Bodin’s spelling and condenses and rearranges his lists; where he adds to or departs from Bodin, the comparisons below say so.

Book I, p. 15: Baal-zebub and the Flies

French: Paris, 1582, p. 15 (scan). Spelling as printed, except that long s is normalised and abbreviation marks are expanded. Highlighted: the words Lecky quotes.

Or il est tout notoire, que les Amorrheans, & autres peuples que Dieu extermina, s’exerçoient en telles sortes de Sorceleries, sacrifians aussi les hommes aux Diables, ausquels ils parloient, & qu’ils adoroient, & principalement le Soleil, l’appellant par excellence Bahal c’est à dire en Hebrieu, Seigneur, d’où est venu Bahalzebuf, qui veut dire Maistre mouche, par ce qu’il n’y auoit pas vne mouche en son temple, comme on dict qu’au Palais de Venise il n’y a pas vne seule mouche & au palais de Tolede qu’il n’y en a qu’vne, qui n’est pas chose estrange, ou nouuelle: car nous lisons que les Cyrenaiques apres auoir sacrifié au Dieu Acaron Dieu des mouches, & les Grecs à Iuppiter surnommé Myiodes, c’est à dire Mouchard, toutes les mouches s’enuoloient en vne nuee, comme nous lisons en Pausanias in Arcadicis, & en Pline au liure xxix. chapitre vi.

Now it is well known that the Amorites and the other peoples whom God exterminated practised such kinds of sorcery, sacrificing men also to the Devils, with whom they spoke and whom they worshipped, chiefly the Sun, which they called by excellence Baal, that is to say in Hebrew, Lord; whence came Baalzebub, which means Master Fly, because there was not a fly in his temple, as it is said that in the Palace of Venice there is not a single fly, and in the palace of Toledo only one, which is no strange or new thing; for we read that the Cyrenaeans, after sacrificing to the god Acaron, god of flies, and the Greeks to Jupiter surnamed Myiodes, that is to say “Mouchard,” all the flies flew away in a cloud, as we read in Pausanias, in his Arcadica, and in Pliny, book xxix, chapter vi.

What is not traced

Neither the Pausanias nor the Pliny reference has been checked against its source here; this edition gives Bodin’s references as he printed them. “Mouchard” in modern French means a spy or informer; what Bodin means by it here has not been checked, and it is left untranslated.

Book III, pp. 151–152: The Pardon of Trois-Échelles

French: Paris, 1582, pp. 151–152 (scan, pp. 151–152), conventions as above. “[…]” marks the omission of a paragraph about Coligny and a sorcery case reported by Leovicer, which fills the rest of p. 151; the name “Trois-Echelles” is split across the page break. Highlighted: the sentences Lecky quotes.

I’en metteray vn exemple aduenu en Poitou l’an 1571. Le Roy Charles 9. apres disner commanda qu’on luy amenast Trois-Echelles, auquel il auoit donné sa grace pour accuser ses complices. Et confessa deuant le Roy, en presence de plusieurs grands Seigneurs, la façon du transport des Sorciers, des dances, des sacrifices faits à Satan, des paillardises auec les Diables en figure d’hommes & de femmes: & que chacun prenoit des poudres pour faire mourir hommes, bestes, & fruicts. […]

[p. 152] Trois-Eschelles alors en raconta beaucoup de semblables: Et faut croire que si le Roy, qui estoit d’vne forte complexion & robuste, eust faict brusler ce maistre Sorcier & ses complices, il est à presumer que Dieu luy eust donné pour telles executions heureuse & longue vie. Car la parole de Dieu est tres-certaine, que celuy qui fait eschapper l’homme digne de mort, verse sur luy mesme la peine d’autruy, comme le Prophete dist au Roy Achab, qu’il mourroit pour auoir donné grace à l’homme digne de mort. Or iamais n’auoit esté ouy qu’on donnast grace pour les Sorciers. Vray est qu’on peut dire que c’estoit pour accuser les complices, qu’on luy donnoit grace, mais tous eschapperent.

I will give an example that happened in Poitou in the year 1571. King Charles IX, after dinner, ordered that Trois-Échelles be brought to him, to whom he had given his pardon so that he would accuse his accomplices. And he confessed before the King, in the presence of several great lords, the manner of the sorcerers’ transport, the dances, the sacrifices made to Satan, the lewdness with the Devils in the shape of men and women; and that each of them took powders to kill men, beasts, and crops. […]

[p. 152] Trois-Échelles then related many similar things. And it must be believed that if the King, who was of a strong and robust constitution, had burnt this master sorcerer and his accomplices, it is to be presumed that God would have given him a happy and long life for such executions. For the word of God is most certain, that he who lets a man worthy of death escape pours upon himself the penalty of another, as the Prophet said to King Ahab, that he would die for having given pardon to the man worthy of death. Now it had never been heard that pardon was given for sorcerers. It is true one may say that it was in order to accuse his accomplices that he was pardoned; but they all escaped.

Lecky’s account of this story is in note 89 of Chapter I (the note reads simply “P. 152.”). Set against Bodin’s text:

Réfutation des opinions de Jean Wier, near p. 252: The Laws and Experience of All Peoples

The page number of this leaf is not legible in the scan; it is the page immediately before p. 252, and Lecky cites “p. 252”. The passage begins in the middle of a sentence that starts on the previous page. Bodin’s three marginal references are given after the text. Highlighted: the clauses Lecky quotes.

[…] aux loix de Platon, des douze tables, des Iurisconsultes, des Empereurs, & de tous les peuples & legislateurs, Perses, Hebrieux, Grecs, Latins, Allemans, François, Italiens, Espagnols, Anglois, qui ont decreté peines capitales contre les Sorciers, & contre ceux qui les recelent, ou qui les font euader: S’il faut plustost s’arrester à VVier que à l’experience de tous les peuples, Roys, Princes, Legislateurs, Magistrats, Iurisconsultes, qui ont cogneu au doigt, & à l’œil les impietez & meschancetez execrables, dont les Sorciers sont chargez: s’il faut plustost s’arrester au disciple du plus grand Sorcier, qui fut onques de son aage, que aux Prophetes, Theologiens, Docteurs, Iuges & Magistrats, qui ont descouuert la verité par mille & mille presomptions violentes, accusations, tesmoignages, recolemens, confrontations, conuictions, recognoissances, repentances, & confessions volontaires iusques à la mort. Nous auons le iugement de Dieu6, qui a declaré qu’il auoit arraché de la terre les peuples de la Palestine, pour les horribles Sorcelleries dont ils vsoient, & non pour autre chose, & a menassé d’exterminer non seulement les Sorciers, ains aussi tous ceux qui les souffriront viure,7 & qui a dit à Hieremie qu’il preschast haut & clair qu’il raseroit8 à feu & à sang la ville de Hierusalem, & tous les habitans pour les execrables Sorcelleries du Roy Manassés. Voila ce qu’il m’a semblé, qu’on peut respondre aux liures de VVier […]

Notes on the translation

Récolement is a term of French criminal procedure: the reading back of a witness’s deposition to the witness for confirmation, before the confrontation. The gloss is this edition’s own. The “disciple of the greatest sorcerer of his age” is Agrippa, whom Wier served; Lecky identifies him in note 87.

Lecky quotes this passage in English on his p. 101 (note 91: “Bodin, p. 252”), as something Montaigne “was, no doubt, perfectly aware” of. Set against Bodin’s text:

The laws“the laws of Plato, of the twelve tables, of the consuls, of the emperors, and of all nations and legislators”“des Iurisconsultes” (“of the jurisconsults”). “Consuls” does not appear.
The peoples“Persian, Hebrew, Greek, Latin, German, French, Italian, Spanish, English”Perses, Hebrieux, Grecs, Latins, Allemans, François, Italiens, Espagnols, Anglois (same nine, in the same order)

Book I, f. 40: God “au grand nez”

French: Paris, 1582, p. 40 (scan). This printing numbers folios (leaves), not pages, and p. 40 is the recto. Spelling as printed, with long s normalised. The right margin has “Dicta frontis inspectio” and “Exodi cap. 34”. The two Hebrew phrases are printed in Hebrew letters; I give them in transliteration, which is my reading (’erekh ’appayim and qetsar ’appayim). Highlighted: the passage I take to be the “astounding instance of verbal criticism” Lecky says he does not “venture to quote” (his note 68). He does not describe it, so this identification is my inference.

… Et telles predictions ne sont point illicites, si ce n’est qu’on les voulust asseurer comme chose infaillible, comme nous pouuons dire en cas pareil de la metoposcopie, qui iuge des passions interieures de l’homme au seul regard du visage, entre lesquelles il y en a de naturelles: comme la rougeur soudaine signifie la honte, paillir soudain signifie crainte, & qui ont leurs causes naturelles: Mais il y en a qui sont plus humaines, que naturelles, comme les yeux de Hyboux luysans, signifient le plus souuent cruauté: Tels les auoit Sylla & Caton le Censeur, ou bien s’ils sont marquez de gouttes de sang. Ainsi dict-on des Camus, qu’ils sont choleres & impatiens: Et au contraire les grands nez sont plus prudens & patiens. C’est l’vn des epithetes que Dieu s’est donné à luymesmes, parlant à Moyse, entre les onze proprietez il s’appelle [’erekh ’appayim] c’est à dire, Grand-nez, ainsi que l’edition Complutense d’Espagne, & d’Anuers, de mot à mot interpreté, & en plusieurs lieux de la Bible, où il s’appelle le Dieu au grand nez, que tous les interpretes tournent patient, & par son contraire [qetsar ’appayim] c’est à dire, Court nez: les Hebrieux interpretent, Soudain en cholere. En quoy il nous est monstré aussi, que la Metoposcopie naturelle n’est point illicite, & de faict en tout l’Orient ils sont fort experimentez en cela. Si est-ce qu’il ne faut pas en faire loy infaillible: car il se trouue des hommes si masquez, & qui sçauent si bien couurir, & dissimuler leurs naturels, qu’ils sont entierement maistres de leurs visages, en sorte que plusieurs se voyans trompez en ont …

… And such predictions are not unlawful, unless one wished to assert them as infallible, as we can say in a like case of metoposcopy, which judges the inner passions of a man from a mere look at the face; among these some are natural: as sudden blushing signifies shame, sudden paling signifies fear, and these have their natural causes; but there are some that are more human than natural, as the shining eyes of owls signify most often cruelty: Sulla and Cato the Censor had such, or if they are marked with drops of blood. So of the snub-nosed it is said that they are choleric and impatient; and on the contrary, large noses are more prudent and patient. It is one of the epithets that God has given to himself, speaking to Moses, among the eleven properties he calls himself [’erekh ’appayim], that is, Big-nose, as the Complutensian edition of Spain and that of Antwerp interpret it word for word, and in several places of the Bible where he is called the God with the big nose, which all interpreters turn “patient”; and by its contrary [qetsar ’appayim] that is, Short-nose: the Hebrews interpret it “Quick to anger”. By which it is shown to us also that natural metoposcopy is not unlawful, and in fact in all the East they are well practised in it. Yet it must not be made an infallible law: for there are men so masked, and who know so well how to cover and dissimulate their natures, that they are entirely masters of their faces, so that many, seeing themselves deceived, have …

Notes on the Hebrew and the editions

The Hebrew idiom ’erekh ’appayim (Exodus 34:6) is literally “long of nose (or nostrils)”, and is normally translated “slow to anger” or “long-suffering”; qetsar ’appayim is the opposite. That is standard knowledge, not something I checked in Bodin. “L’edition Complutense d’Espagne, & d’Anuers” presumably means the Complutensian and the Antwerp polyglot Bibles; I have not checked what they print. Bodin’s page is on physiognomy: it follows a discussion of prophecies from natural signs.

Book IV, ff. 217–218: Stoning, Burning, and the Plague

French: Paris, 1582, p. 217 (recto) and its verso (scan), the page after the plea for burning alive. Conventions as above. The margin of p. 217 has “2. in lib. tertio” and a Hebrew line against “Moyse Raban”. Highlighted: what Lecky’s notes 88 and 137 rest on.

… Nous auons remarqué cy dessus que la Sorciere nommee Sibylle Dinscops, au Duché de Cleues, estant brusllee, la main qu’on voyoit qui persecutoit tous les passans, cessa soudain. Apres que la Sorciere de Bieure qui est pres de ceste ville de Lao fut brusllee, les mortalitez d’hommes & bestes, qui aduenoient par les venefices, cesserent. Encores est il à noter, ce que i’ay apprins de maistre Adam Martin, qui luy a faict son procez: c’est qu’elle menaça vne femme qu’elle n’allaicteroit iamais enfant, soudain son laict seicha: & combien qu’elle eust depuis plusieurs enfans, si est-ce que son laict tarissoit tousiours: mais son laict retourna aussi tost que la sorciere fut executee, & fut brusllee toute viue par vn iuste iugement de Dieu, contre l’aduis des Iuges, qui auoient ordonné qu’elle fust estranglee: mais le Bourreau n’y peut donner ordre, combien que la peine de lapidation ordonnee par la loy de Dieu est plus rigoureuse, que brusler vif: ainsi que Moyse Raban a noté. Et me souuient auoir leu au liure intitulé Malleus maleficarum, que la peste ne cessa point en vn bourg d’Allemagne au pays de Constance, iusques à ce qu’on eut deterré vne Sorciere, & redigé son corps en cendres. Comme en cas pareil il y eut vne femme au village de Verigny pres de Concy; laquelle fut attainte & accusee de plusieurs malefices: & pour la difficulté de la preuue relaschee: depuis i’ay sceu des habitans qu’il estoit mort vne infinité de bestail, & de personnes. Elle mourut au mois d’Auril 1579: depuis sa mort tous les habitans de Veriguy, & le bestail sont en repos, & ne se meurent plus comme de coustume. Qui est bien pour monstrer que la cause principale cessant, les effects cessent, encores que Dieu face tomber les afflictions sur ceux qu’il luy plaist.

… We have noted above that when the Sorceress named Sibylle Dinscops, in the Duchy of Cleves, was burnt, the hand that was seen persecuting all passers-by ceased suddenly. After the Sorceress of Bieure, which is near this town of Laon, was burnt, the mortalities of men and beasts, which came by poisonings, ceased. It is also to be noted what I learned from Master Adam Martin, who conducted her trial: that she threatened a woman that she would never nurse a child, and suddenly her milk dried up; and though she afterwards had several children, still her milk always dried up: but her milk returned as soon as the sorceress was executed, and she was burnt quite alive by a just judgement of God, against the advice of the Judges, who had ordered that she be strangled: but the Executioner could not manage it, although the penalty of stoning ordained by the law of God is more rigorous than burning alive, as Moses Raban has noted. And I remember having read in the book entitled Malleus maleficarum, that the plague did not cease in a borough of Germany in the country of Constance until a Sorceress was dug up and her body reduced to ashes. As in a like case there was a woman in the village of Verigny near Coucy, who was convicted and accused of several maleficia, and, because of the difficulty of the proof, released: afterwards I learned from the inhabitants that an infinity of cattle and people had died. She died in the month of April 1579: since her death all the inhabitants of Verigny and the cattle are at rest, and no longer die as they used to. Which is well to show that, the principal cause ceasing, the effects cease, though God make afflictions fall on whom he pleases.

Doubtful points

“Moyse Raban” is not identified here; “Rabin Maymon” on f. 228 (below) is presumably Maimonides. “Le Bourreau n’y peut donner ordre” is translated “could not manage it”; the sense may be that the executioner could not help it. I have not looked for the story in the Malleus. The text before “sonne accusé…” on the same page, about the procedure in cases of proof, is not transcribed.

Lecky’s Notes 68, 88 and 137 Compared

Lecky (ch. I)BodinComment
Note 68: “another astounding instance of verbal criticism, which I do not venture to quote, in Bodin, Dem. p. 40.”f. 40: God’s epithet ’erekh ’appayim read as “Grand-nez”, within a discussion of physiognomy.The page and the “verbal criticism” fit. Nothing on the page is indecent in the ordinary sense; the likely reason Lecky does not quote it is its irreverence (“le Dieu au grand nez”). That is my inference.
Text, ch. I: “Some persons … question[ed] the lawfulness of strangling the witch before she was burnt … the penalty in the Levitical code was stoning, and stoning had been pronounced by the Jewish theologians to be a still more painful death than the stake.” Note 137: “Bodin, p. 217.”f. 217: the judges had ordered a witch strangled; she was “burnt quite alive by a just judgement of God”, though stoning “is more rigorous than burning alive, as Moses Raban has noted”. f. 228: stoning “the cruellest death of all, as Rabbi Maymon says”.The “stoning is worse than the stake” point is Bodin’s, on both pages, citing a rabbinic authority. The claim that people questioned strangling before burning is not on the page: Bodin reports the judges’ order to strangle and the executioner’s failure, and approves the burning as God’s judgement. I: those who pardon sorcerers “had outraged the majesty of Heaven … and pestilence and famine would inevitably desolate their dominions.” Note 88: “Pp. 217, 228.”ff. 217–218: plague and cattle-death in villages ended only when a witch was burnt or died. f. 228: God “will extend his fury against the people that shall suffer these wickednesses unpunished”.Plague is on pp. 217–218. “Famine” is not on the pages I read; the verso of f. 228, not transcribed, may have it. Bodin does not make the argument about pardons on these pages (that is the Trois-Échelles passage, p. 152); Lecky is gathering Bodin’s general view.
