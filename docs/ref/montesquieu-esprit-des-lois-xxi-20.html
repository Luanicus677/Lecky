Montesquieu, De l’esprit des lois, XXI, 20

The scholastics, the Jews and the bill of exchange

Author
Montesquieu (1689–1755). Lecky: “(Esprit des Lois, lib. xxi. c. 20)” (Chapter VI, note 39).
Edition used
A text, not a scan: the 1777 edition as transcribed on French Wikisource (L21/C20). Eighteenth-century spelling (“scholastiques”, “&”). The translation is this edition’s own draft.
Cited in
Rationalism, ch. VI, note 39.
Status
Text compared; no page image seen. Lecky’s Turgot quotation in the same note (Mémoire sur les prêts d’argent, § 14) is on the Turgot page only in part.

Lecky says Montesquieu, speaking of the scholastic writings on usury, “says, with a little exaggeration”: “Ainsi nous devons aux spéculations des scholastiques tous les malheurs qui ont accompagné la destruction du commerce”. The sentence is the end of a chapter on how commerce emerged in the Middle Ages from the violence against the Jews; Montesquieu continues: “& à l’avarice des princes l’établissement d’une chose qui le met en quelque façon hors de leur pouvoir.” Lecky’s “t Ainsi” is a misread opening quotation mark; “speculations” and “accompagne” are the OCR of his note, which lacks the accents.

“Nous devons aux spéculations des scholastiques tous les malheurs …”

French: book XXI, chapter XX (1777 ed., French Wikisource). Highlighted: Lecky’s quotation.

… Les Juifs, proscrits tour-à-tour de chaque pays, trouverent le moyen de sauver leurs effets. … Ils inventerent les lettres de change ; & par ce moyen, le commerce put éluder la violence & se maintenir par-tout … Les théologiens furent obligés de restreindre leurs principes ; & le commerce qu’on avoit violemment lié avec la mauvaise foi, rentra pour ainsi dire dans le sein de la probité. Ainsi nous devons aux spéculations des scholastiques tous les malheurs qui ont accompagné la destruction du commerce ; & à l’avarice des princes l’établissement d’une chose qui le met en quelque façon hors de leur pouvoir.

… The Jews, proscribed in turn from every country, found the means of saving their effects. … They invented bills of exchange; and by this means commerce was able to elude violence and maintain itself everywhere … The theologians were obliged to restrict their principles; and commerce, which had been violently tied to bad faith, returned, so to speak, into the bosom of probity. Thus we owe to the speculations of the scholastics all the misfortunes that accompanied the destruction of commerce; and to the avarice of princes the establishment of a thing which puts it in some manner beyond their power.

Lecky’s “with a little exaggeration” is his own comment; Montesquieu’s sentence is part of a passage about the Jews and the bill of exchange, which also bears on Lecky’s note 81 (the invention of the letter of exchange).
