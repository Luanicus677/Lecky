Henry, The Life and Times of John Calvin, vol. II

“Placet mihi Luthero”: Luther signs for the sword

Author
Paul Henry (1792–1853), pastor of the French church in Berlin, whose Das Leben Johann Calvins (1835–1844) was translated by Henry Stebbing.
Edition used
The Life and Times of John Calvin, the Great Reformer, translated from the German by Henry Stebbing, vol. Lecky: “Luther, in reply to Philip of Hesse, distinctly asserted” the right of the civil magistrate to punish heresy; his note adds “And also in reply to the Wittenberg theologians”, and that at an earlier period Luther had advocated toleration. Henry’s page has all three points. Luther “wished obstinate heretics to be punished. He was not true therefore to his early sentiments.” He told Philip of Hesse “that it was lawful to inflict capital punishment on heretics”, an opinion also signed by Melanchthon, Bugenhagen and Cruciger. And to the Wittenberg theologians’ question “whether it was lawful to punish anabaptists with the sword”, Luther wrote his assent in his own hand: “placet mihi Luthero”, it pleases me, Luther.

Henry’s purpose is to show that Luther “would have formed the same judgement on the case of Servetus as Calvin”: the section defends Calvin by spreading the blame. Lecky uses it to the opposite end, that no reformer of the first generation can be credited with toleration.

“Placet mihi Luthero” (vol. II, p. 236)

English: London 1849, vol. II, p. 236, scan n249. Henry’s footnotes give his sources: De Wette’s edition of Luther’s letters, t. iii. s. 347; the Altenburg edition of Luther’s works, t. v. s. 286, and the German exposition of Psalm 82; and, for the Wittenberg opinion, Heidelberg University Library, cod. 435, bl. 33. Highlighted: what Lecky’s note reports.

… Luther considered it very dangerous to allow the principle of intolerance to become general, but still he wished obstinate heretics to be punished. He was not true therefore to his early sentiments. The anabaptists were put to death in Saxony, as everywhere else. Luther also stated it as his opinion to Philip of Hesse, that it was lawful to inflict capital punishment on heretics; and this opinion was subscribed by Melancthon, Bugenhagen and Cruciger. He urges them to pursue this course in the case of such as deny that Christ is God.

It seems evident therefore that Luther would have formed the same judgement on the case of Servetus as Calvin. To an inquiry of the Wittenberg theologians, “whether it was lawful to punish anabaptists with the sword,” we find his assent written in his own hand, “placet mihi Luthero.” So we also read, “Wherever they are found to be the authors, or receivers, and have forbidden articles, they may be capitally punished, as those who have instituted conventicles, against the mandates which have been publicly issued.”

Lecky’s detail that Luther’s earlier tolerance came “when his translation of the New Testament was proscribed” is not on this page; Henry says only that he was untrue to “his early sentiments”.
