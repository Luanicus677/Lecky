Luther, De servo arbitrio

Martin Luther, On the Bondage of the Will

Author
Martin Luther (1483–1546), De servo arbitrio (1525), his reply to Erasmus on free will; Luther later called it one of the few of his books he did not wish to retract (Lecky says so in note 32, citing Vaughan’s preface; not checked here).
Edition used
Beati patris Martini Lutheri Liber De servo arbitrio, contra Desid. Erasmum … cum brevibus annotationibus, ed. Sebastian Schmidt, 1664, sumptibus Joh. Christoph. Nagel (title as given in the archive.org record): archive.org/details/bub_gb_Sp8ZXu8IePYC. This is not the edition Lecky used: he says he gives the section numbers of Vaughan’s English translation (1823) because “in the original edition (1526) there are no divisions, and the pages are not numbered”. The 1664 edition has page numbers, and an editor’s commentary, Annotatio, designed to defend Luther against the charge of Calvinism, which I did not read beyond the pages below. The translations are this edition’s own drafts.
Cited in
Rationalism, ch. IV, note 30 and note 31.
Status
Two of the three passages Lecky quotes (notes 30 and 31). The third (note 32, “Hoc fulmine sternitur …”) is near p. 34 of this edition but I did not read it on the image and have not transcribed it. Page numbers: n94 = p. 69, n95 = p. 70, n96 = p. 71; n87 = p. 62 (inferred from the constant offset of 25, not read from the page number).

In Chapter IV Lecky illustrates the severity of Luther’s predestinarianism with two Latin quotations from De servo arbitrio: the will placed “like a beast” between God and Satan, and the “highest degree of faith”, to believe that God is merciful who saves so few and damns so many.

Both are in the 1664 text. The first agrees except for an abbreviation Lecky expands; in the second Lecky drops the word “sit”. See the comparison.

The Will as a Beast (pp. 69–71)

Latin: 1664 edition, pp. 69–70 (p. 69, p. 70; the “Satan” half continues at the foot of the annotation, p. 71). The text is Luther’s; the editor’s “Annotatio (t)” on p. 70 comments on “ceu iumentum”. Spelling regularised. Highlighted: the words Lecky quotes.

… Sic humana voluntas in medio posita est ceu iumentum: si insederit Deus, vult et vadit, quo vult Deus, ut Psal. dicit: Factus sum sicut iumentum, et ego semper tecum. Si insederit Satan, vult et vadit, quo vult Satan, nec est in eius arbitrio ad utrum sessorem currere aut eum quaerere, sed ipsi sessores certant ob ipsum obtinendum et possidendum.

… So the human will is placed in the middle like a beast of burden: if God has taken his seat on it, it wills and goes where God wills, as the Psalm says: I have become as a beast of burden, and I am always with you. If Satan has taken his seat, it wills and goes where Satan wills, nor is it in its power to run to either rider or to seek him, but the riders themselves contend for obtaining and possessing it.

What I did not check

I read the first sentence in the main text (the foot of p. 69 and top of p. 70). The “Si insederit Satan …” sentence I read only as it is quoted at the head of the next page, after the editor’s annotation (p. 71); I did not look for it in the main text. I have not tried to match Vaughan’s section number (“pars i. sec. 24”). The editor’s own comment (that Luther does not mean the will is equally disposed to good and evil) is worth reading but is not transcribed.

The Highest Degree of Faith (p. 62)

Latin: 1664 edition, p. 62 (scan). The passage occurs twice on the page: in the main text and again in the editor’s “Annotatio (r)” below it, which quotes it in full. Spelling regularised. Highlighted: the words Lecky quotes.

… Hic est fidei summus gradus, credere illum esse clementem, qui tam paucos salvat, tam multos damnat, credere iustum, qui sua voluntate nos necessario damnabiles facit, ut videatur, referente Erasmo, delectari cruciatibus miserorum, et odio potius quam amore dignus. Si igitur possem ulla ratione comprehendere, quomodo is Deus sit misericors et iustus, qui tantam iram et iniquitatem ostendit, non esset opus fide.

… This is the highest degree of faith: to believe him merciful who saves so few and damns so many; to believe him just who by his own will makes us necessarily damnable, so that he seems, as Erasmus reports, to take delight in the torments of the wretched, and to be worthy of hatred rather than love. If therefore I could by any reasoning comprehend how that God is merciful and just who shows such wrath and iniquity, there would be no need of faith.

Lecky’s Quotations Compared

Lecky, 18651664
Note 30Lecky: ‘Sic humana voluntas in medio posita est ceu jumentum, si insederit Deus vult et vadit quo vult Deus, ut Psalmus dicit: “Factus sum sicut jumentum et ego semper tecum.” Si insederit Satan vult et vadit quo vult Satan; nec est in ejus arbitrio ad utrum sessorem currere aut eum quærere, sed ipsi sessores certant ob ipsum obtinendum et possidendum.’
1664: … ceu jumentum: si insederit Deus, vult & vadit, quo vult Deus, ut Psal. dicit: Factus sum sicut jumentum & ego semper tecum. Si insederit Satan, vult & vadit, quo vult Satan, nec est in eius arbitrio … possidendum.
Note 31Lecky: ‘Hic est fidei summus gradus, credere illum esse clementem qui tam paucos salvat tam multos damnat, credere justum qui sua voluntate nos necessario damnabiles facit, ut videatur, referente Erasmo, delectari cruciatibus miserorum, et odio potius quam amore dignus. Si igitur possem ulla ratione comprehendere quomodo is Deus misericors et justus, qui tantam iram et iniquitatem ostendit, non esset opus fide.’
1664: the same words with different punctuation, except that the print has “quomodo is Deus sit misericors & iustus”.

The differences are slight. Lecky writes “ut Psalmus dicit” where the 1664 text abbreviates “ut Psal. dicit” (a likely silent expansion, or a different edition); his punctuation is lighter. In note 31 Lecky leaves out one word: “quomodo is Deus sit misericors et iustus” in the print becomes “quomodo is Deus misericors et justus”, which makes his sentence ungrammatical as printed. Neither change alters the sense. The pages Lecky gives (Vaughan’s sections 23 and 24) could not be checked here. One caution on interpretation: the 1664 editor, Schmidt, argues in his commentary that these sentences, like the “beast” image, do not mean that Luther taught absolute predestination (he says Luther intends only the “passive aptitude” of the will); Lecky reads them in the opposite way. I did not evaluate that dispute.
