Dalyell, The Darker Superstitions of Scotland

John Graham Dalyell, 1834

Author
John Graham Dalyell (1775–1851), Scottish antiquarian and naturalist. His book collects, with citations, the Scottish records of witchcraft.
Edition used
The Darker Superstitions of Scotland, illustrated from history and practice (Edinburgh, 1834): archive.org/details/darkersuperstit00dalygoog, a Google Books scan. Its page numbers agree with the ones Lecky gives (pp. 623–624, 645). Image index n = printed page + 16 in this stretch of the book (p. 622 = n638, p. 645 = n661).
Cited in
Rationalism, ch. I, notes 146, 147, 148, 151 and 153. Other Dalyell notes (150, 152, 155, 157, 159; pp. 645, 657, 669–670) are not yet covered.
Status
Four pages transcribed (in part) from the page images. Everything is in English and is quoted as printed, including Dalyell’s Scots quotations.

Lecky’s picture of the Scottish ministers hunting witches rests on a handful of pages of Dalyell. They bear him out on the Aberdeen inquisition of 1603 and on “waking” (keeping a prisoner from sleep, up to nine nights), but in three places Lecky states as fact what Dalyell reports with a qualification: that boxes “were placed in the churches” to receive accusations (Dalyell says “it is said”, and cites Bodin on Milan), that a minister from the pulpit “denounced her by name” (I did not find that on the page cited), and that pricking was a “regular profession” (not on p. 645, but earlier in the chapter).

Dalyell also says that waking was not peculiar to Scotland: it was used in England, “common on the continent”, and in the Turkish dominions. See the comparison.

The Box in the Church (p. 623) and the Aberdeen Inquisition (p. 624)

English: Dalyell, 1834, pp. 623–624 (p. 623, p. 624). As printed (the scan’s long text has been read from the image). Highlighted: the sentences behind Lecky’s notes 146–148.

[p. 623] … Clandestine information, it is said, was sought, “by placing an empty box in church, to receive a billet with the sorcerer’s name, and the date and description of his deeds.”‖ Too many readily assented, and a secret enquiry on the truth or probability of the accusation, before some of the ecclesiastical judicatories, such as the kirk session, followed.

‖ Bodinus, lib. iv. c. 1. p. 321. illustrates the custom of Milan from Scotish practice.

[p. 624] But besides the operation of private calumnies, a general discovery of offenders was occasionally contemplated by the zealous, and conducted on a scale of unexampled magnitude, like huntsmen beating up extensive coverts for dislodging beasts of prey. A whole presbytery was up in arms. Thus, “at the College of auld Abirdene, the sext of Januar 1603,” it was ordained that every minister should make “ane subtill and privie inquisitioun, heirin: videlicet, the minister with tua of his elderis, that feris God and ar maist zealous for his glorie, at ilk particular kirk respectivè, tak the aithis of the inhabitants within thair charge, quhat thay knaw of wiches and consultaris with thame: and wreitt thair depositiounis, and returne the same to the presbyterie, with the names of sic as ar metest to be assysouris to thame, that the same may be sent to the Marques with all hastie expeditioun, conforme to the desyre of his Lordschip’s letter.” Meantime the conduct of individuals underwent a secret scrutiny …

What the box note says

Dalyell’s footnote to the “box” sentence cites Bodin’s Démonomanie (book IV, ch. 1, p. 321), and says Bodin “illustrates the custom of Milan from Scotish practice”. That is hard to read: on the ordinary sense, Bodin uses the Scottish practice to illustrate Milan. Reginald Scot’s Discovery of Witchcraft (1665, Book II ch. 2) reports that Bodin “commendeth much the Scottish custome”, a hollow chest in the church with three locks (see the Scot page); so the Scottish box is Bodin’s report, not independent evidence. I have not looked at Bodin’s p. 321 itself. What matters here is that Dalyell himself reports the Scottish box as something “it is said”, not as established.

“Watching” (pp. 644–645)

English: Dalyell, 1834, pp. 644–645 (p. 644, p. 645), under the heading “Watching”. As printed. Highlighted: the sentences behind Lecky’s notes 150–153.

[p. 644] Watching.—Though privation of sleep be described as “the choicest means they use in Scotland for discovery of witches,” it was not restricted to them only; for, after other expedients for detecting a conspiracy, had failed to obtain confession, the commander of the forces was enjoined to employ the most trusty officers or soldiers to watch “Master William Spence be turnes, and not to suffer him to sleep by night or by day, and, for that end, to use all effectual meanes for keeping him still awake.” Another was “withhaldin from sleep to the great perturbation of his brayne.” By means of tortures, pricking, watching,

[p. 645] and keeping several women from sleep, James Gillespie, minister of Rind, and some coadjutors, were charged with having obtained false confessions, whereon the innocent had suffered death. Several also, under a guard of drunken fellows at Pittenweem, were kept days and nights awake, which “cruel usage made some of them to be so wise as to acknowledge every question that was asked of them, whereby they found the minister and baillies well pleased, and themselves better treated.” One was kept five days and nights awake by continual pricking, to the great effusion of her blood: and this kind of torture is alleged to have been protracted even to nine nights.

Suspected sorcerers were treated thus in England. Conjoined with other torture it was common on the continent, and esteemed singly as the most effectual inducement to confess. Keeping the suspected awake for the same purpose, is still practised in the Turkish dominions: and it may be traced to ancient date, in Proto Sebastus having been kept awake three days and nights, by his guards pricking him.

Lecky’s Statements Compared

Lecky (ch. I)DalyellComment
“Assembled in solemn synod, the College of Aberdeen, in 1603, enjoined every minister to take two of the elders of his parish to make ‘a subtle and privy inquisition,’ and to question all the parishioners upon oath as to their knowledge of witches.” Note 146: “p. 624.”p. 624: the “College of auld Abirdene, the sext of Januar 1603” ordained every minister to make “ane subtill and privie inquisitioun” with two elders, taking “the aithis of the inhabitants … quhat thay knaw of wiches and consultaris with thame”.Accurate, and the page agrees. It was a presbytery meeting (“a whole presbytery was up in arms”), “College” being the place; “solemn synod” is Lecky’s phrase. Lecky leaves out that the returns were to go to the Marquis (of Huntly) at his letter’s desire.
“Boxes were placed in the churches for the express purpose of receiving the accusations.” Note 147: “Ibid. p. 623.”p. 623: “it is said, was sought, ‘by placing an empty box in church, to receive a billet …’”, with a note citing Bodin on Milan.Lecky drops Dalyell’s “it is said” and states the practice as a fact. Dalyell does not give a Scottish source for it on this page.
“When a woman had fallen under suspicion, the minister from the pulpit denounced her by name, exhorted his parishioners to give evidence against her, and prohibited anyone from sheltering her.” Note 148: “Ibid. p. 624, &c.”p. 624: public accusation “was invited, as by the kirk session directing their officer to intimate, that those who had anything to say against ‘Jonet …’” (the page breaks off).The next page (not read) presumably continues; the “&c.” covers it. On p. 624 the invitation is by the kirk session’s officer, not the minister from the pulpit, and I did not see the sheltering prohibition on this page.
“Some prisoners have been waked for five nights; one, it is said, even for nine.” Note 153: “Dalyell, p. 645.”p. 645: “One was kept five days and nights awake by continual pricking … and this kind of torture is alleged to have been protracted even to nine nights.”Accurate: Lecky keeps Dalyell’s hedge (“it is said”). Dalyell’s “five” is one woman at Pittenweem, kept awake by pricking, which Lecky generalises to “some prisoners”.
Note 151: “Dalyell, p. 645. The ‘prickers’ formed a regular profession in Scotland.”pp. 644–645: “pricking” is mentioned as part of the tortures; the probe of “the pricker” is discussed on p. 644.“Regular profession” is not on p. 645. In the OCR of earlier pages (pp. 641–643, not read on the image) a fee paid to “the celebrated pricker” John Kincaid is recorded, which would support Lecky’s statement; the page reference may simply be a few pages out. Unverified.
(Text, ch. I) “the most effectual … method of obtaining confession was by what was termed ‘waking her.’”p. 644: sleep-deprivation described as “the choicest means they use in Scotland for discovery of witches” (quoting Remesey, 1684); p. 645: “common on the continent, and esteemed singly as the most effectual inducement to confess”.Dalyell’s “most effectual” refers to the practice on the continent. For Scotland he quotes a contemporary calling it “the choicest means”. Dalyell also stresses that it was used in England, on the continent and in Turkey, so it was not peculiarly Scottish, which Lecky’s account does not say.
