Marangoni, Delle cose gentilesche e profane

Orpheus and the Verbo divino (ch. XI, p. 45)

Author
Giovanni Marangoni (1673–1753), canon and antiquary of Rome. Lecky cites “Marangoni, Delle Cose Gentilesche, p. 45” in Chapter III, note 11 and “Ibid. p. 45” in note 13; note 10 (Orpheus) cites Eusebius without a page.
Edition used
The Rome 1744 edition: Internet Archive gri_33125010851489; p. 45 = image n66 (image = page + 21). The translation is this edition’s own draft.
Cited in
Rationalism, ch. III, note 10 (by content), 11 and 13 (by page; unmatched).
Status
p. 45 read on the image. Only the Orpheus matter is on it; I did not read the neighbouring chapters, where the genii of the seasons and the veiled aquatic deity may be.

Lecky’s text says “Orpheus drawing all men to him by his music, symbolised the attractive power of Christianity” and, in note 10, that Eusebius speaks of Orpheus as symbolising Christ. Marangoni’s p. 45, in his chapter on churches and the Orpheus symbol, says exactly this: the fable of Orpheus drawing trees and beasts by his eloquence is the pagan echo of the Word of God, and he quotes Eusebius’s oration on Constantine in Latin. But Lecky’s notes 11 (the genii of the seasons) and 13 (the pagan aquatic deity) cite the same p. 45, which has nothing on either; the citations seem to be copied forward, or from another edition.

Orpheus as the Verbo divino (p. 45)

Italian and Latin: p. 45, scan (the long s is printed as s). Highlighted: the sentence behind Lecky’s text and note 10.

Ma ritornando all’ Immagine di Orfeo ; ella fu al certo una Misteriosa favola de’ Greci, per dinotare l’efficace eloquenza delle di lui parole, colle quali a se tirasse gl’alberi, e le piante, e mitigasse gli animali più fieri ; volendo con ciò significare, che, colla dolcezza del suo parlare, a se attraeva gl’Uomini, ed i loro affetti … così eglino inventarono, o pure appropiarono ad Orfeo uomo eloquentissimo la proprietà, ed efficacia del Verbo Divino … Tutto ciò mirabilmente spiegò Eusebio Vescovo Cesariense nella sua famosa Orazione delle lodi del Grande Imperadore Costantino ; ed eccone le sue parole : Orphaeus, ut in fabulis est Graecorum, omnia ferarum genera cantu permulcere, et instrumenti fidibus plectro percussis, belluarum immanium furores mitigare potuit. …

But returning to the image of Orpheus: it was certainly a mysterious fable of the Greeks, to signify the effective eloquence of his words, with which he drew to himself trees and plants and soothed the fiercest animals; meaning by this that with the sweetness of his speech he drew men and their affections to himself … so they invented, or else appropriated to Orpheus, a most eloquent man, the property and efficacy of the Divine Word … All this Eusebius, Bishop of Caesarea, wonderfully explained in his famous Oration in praise of the Great Emperor Constantine; and here are his words: Orpheus, as in the fables of the Greeks, was able by song to soothe all kinds of wild beasts, and by striking the strings of his instrument with the plectrum to calm the fury of savage beasts. …

The Eusebius passage is the one Lecky’s note 10 cites (“Orpheus is spoken of by Eusebius as in this respect symbolising Christ”). I have not checked Eusebius’s oration itself.
